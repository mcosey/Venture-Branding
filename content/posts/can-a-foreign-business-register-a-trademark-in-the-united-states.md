Suppose you own a business outside the United States and want to expand into the American market. You may already have a registered trademark in your home country, or perhaps you're preparing to introduce your products or services to U.S. customers for the first time.

Can you register your trademark in the United States if neither you nor your business is based there?

**Yes.** Foreign individuals and businesses can obtain federal trademark registrations through the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). You generally do not need to be a U.S. citizen, reside in the United States, or establish an American company to qualify.

However, foreign applicants must satisfy U.S. trademark requirements, and certain rules apply specifically to applicants based outside the country.

## Can a Foreign Individual or Business Own a U.S. Trademark?

Yes. U.S. trademark law permits qualifying foreign individuals and legal entities to own and register trademarks.

For example, a software company headquartered in Germany may seek U.S. trademark protection for its product name. Similarly, an entrepreneur living in Brazil may apply to register a trademark associated with their business.

The USPTO evaluates applications under U.S. trademark law, including whether the proposed mark is eligible for registration, whether it conflicts with existing rights, and whether the applicant satisfies the requirements of the applicable filing basis.

## Do I Need to Establish a U.S. Company?

No. Foreign businesses generally do not need to form a U.S. corporation or limited liability company to obtain a federal trademark registration.

An existing foreign company may apply in its own name, provided it is the appropriate legal owner of the trademark and otherwise qualifies for registration.

For instance, a Canadian corporation seeking to register its software product name would not ordinarily need to establish a U.S. subsidiary solely to own the trademark.

Ownership nevertheless matters. A trademark application must identify the proper applicant, and incorrectly identifying the owner can create significant legal problems.

Attorneys therefore evaluate which individual or entity owns the relevant rights, particularly when a business operates through multiple companies or affiliates.

## Do I Need to Be Selling Products or Services in the United States?

Not necessarily. Whether a foreign applicant must demonstrate actual commercial use before registration depends on the application's **filing basis**.

U.S. trademark law recognizes several filing bases with different requirements.

**Use in commerce: Section 1(a)**

A business already using its trademark in qualifying U.S. commerce may apply under Section 1(a) of the Lanham Act.

This generally requires evidence showing how the trademark is used with the identified goods or services, along with the relevant dates of use.

Commercial transactions involving U.S. customers may qualify, although whether particular activities satisfy the legal requirements depends on the circumstances.

**Intent to use: Section 1(b)**

A foreign business that has not yet begun qualifying commercial use may apply under Section 1(b) if it has a bona fide intention to use the trademark in commerce.

This allows an application to proceed before the relevant goods or services are launched.

However, an intent-to-use application generally cannot mature into registration until the applicant establishes qualifying use in commerce and submits the required evidence.

**Foreign registration: Section 44(e)**

Certain foreign applicants may qualify for U.S. registration based on an existing trademark registration from their country of origin.

Under Section 44(e), an eligible applicant may obtain a U.S. registration without proving actual U.S. trademark use before registration.

The applicant must still have a bona fide intention to use the mark in U.S. commerce and satisfy other applicable requirements.

The foreign registration must meet requirements concerning ownership, the mark, and the covered goods or services. It must also generally remain in force when the U.S. registration issues.

**Foreign application priority: Section 44(d)**

Certain applicants with an earlier foreign trademark application may claim priority from that application by filing a corresponding U.S. application within six months.

This can establish an earlier effective filing date for priority purposes.

However, Section 44(d) does not independently provide a basis for registration. The applicant must also satisfy the requirements of an appropriate registration basis.

These distinctions matter because the filing basis affects the application requirements and whether proof of U.S. trademark use is necessary before registration.

## Does My Trademark Registration in Another Country Protect Me in the United States?

Generally, no. Trademark rights are territorial, meaning protection granted in one country does not automatically extend to another.

For example, a business with a registered trademark in France does not automatically receive equivalent protection in the United States.

A qualifying foreign registration may support a U.S. application under Section 44(e), but the USPTO must still determine whether the mark satisfies applicable U.S. registration requirements.

A foreign registration therefore does not guarantee that the same trademark is available or registrable in the American market.

The Madrid Protocol also allows eligible foreign trademark owners to request protection in the United States through an international filing system. These requests remain subject to examination under U.S. trademark law.

## Do Foreign Businesses Need a U.S. Trademark Attorney?

Generally, yes, if the applicant is domiciled outside the United States.

Since August 3, 2019, the USPTO has required foreign-domiciled trademark applicants and registrants to be represented before the agency by a U.S.-licensed attorney.

For individuals, domicile generally means the place where a person resides and intends to maintain their principal home.

For businesses, domicile generally means the principal place of business where senior executives or officers ordinarily direct and control the company's activities.

The requirement therefore depends on **domicile rather than citizenship**.

For example, a French citizen whose principal home is in the United States may not be required to retain counsel solely because of their nationality. Conversely, a U.S. citizen whose principal home is abroad may be subject to the requirement.

There is a procedural distinction for applications submitted through the Madrid Protocol. Foreign-domiciled applicants may initially designate the United States through the international filing system without appointing U.S. counsel. However, U.S.-licensed representation is required for covered submissions to the USPTO, including responses to refusals.

The representation requirement is intended to promote compliance with U.S. trademark law and improve the accuracy of USPTO filings.

## What Does the U.S. Trademark Application Process Involve?

Foreign applicants generally undergo the same substantive USPTO examination process as domestic applicants, although filing bases and representation requirements may differ.

A trademark attorney evaluating a potential U.S. application may consider:

- **Ownership:** Whether the applicant is the proper individual or legal entity.

- **Trademark availability:** Whether existing registered or unregistered marks present potential conflicts.

- **Filing basis:** Whether the application will rely on actual use, intent to use, a qualifying foreign application, or a foreign registration.

- **Goods and services:** Whether the application accurately identifies the offerings associated with the trademark.

- **Commercial activity:** Whether any required evidence of use satisfies U.S. legal standards.

- **Foreign records:** Whether existing foreign applications or registrations support the proposed filing basis or priority claim.

The USPTO examines the application and may issue an office action identifying legal or procedural issues.

Even when an applicant relies on a foreign registration, the USPTO may refuse registration because of conflicting trademark rights or other grounds under U.S. law.

Foreign applicants should also understand that obtaining registration does not eliminate future obligations.

U.S. registrations generally require qualifying commercial use and periodic maintenance filings, including registrations initially obtained without proof of U.S. use. Use occurring exclusively in another country ordinarily does not satisfy these requirements.

## The Bottom Line

Foreign individuals and businesses can obtain U.S. federal trademark registrations without becoming American citizens or establishing U.S. companies.

The requirements depend on the applicant's trademark rights, commercial activities, and filing basis. Certain foreign applicants may obtain registration before establishing qualifying U.S. commercial use, but continued protection generally requires compliance with U.S. use and maintenance requirements.

Foreign-domiciled applicants must also generally retain U.S.-licensed counsel for trademark matters before the USPTO.
