Suppose you’ve settled on a name for your business. While searching the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) database, you discover that another company previously registered the same name. However, the registration is now marked as “DEAD.”

Does that mean the name is available for your business to use and register?

Possibly. A trademark that has genuinely been abandoned may become available for another business to adopt. However, an abandoned application or expired federal registration does not necessarily mean that the previous owner has lost its underlying trademark rights.

## What Does It Mean When a Trademark Is Listed as “Dead”?

When searching the USPTO database, you may encounter trademark records designated as either “LIVE” or “DEAD.”

A dead record indicates that an application or registration is no longer active. This can occur for several reasons, including missed application deadlines, failure to submit required maintenance filings, or voluntary abandonment.

Three legally distinct circumstances are worth understanding:

- **Abandoned application:** A trademark application is no longer active.

- **Canceled or expired registration:** A previously registered trademark no longer has an active federal registration.

- **Abandoned trademark:** The underlying trademark rights have been lost through legal abandonment.

A business that allows its federal registration to expire may continue using the trademark and retain common-law trademark rights. In certain circumstances, an inactive application or registration may also be revived or reinstated.

Although a dead record cannot itself bar another application from registration while it remains inactive, the underlying trademark rights may still present legal obstacles.

## When Is a Trademark Legally Considered Abandoned?

Section 45 of the Lanham Act establishes the federal definition of trademark abandonment.

Abandonment through nonuse generally requires two elements:

- The owner has discontinued use of the trademark.

- The owner does not intend to resume that use.

Intent may be inferred from the surrounding circumstances.

For example, a company might temporarily discontinue a product while developing a replacement or addressing manufacturing difficulties. If the company intends to resume qualifying trademark use within the reasonably foreseeable future, the interruption does not necessarily constitute abandonment.

Conversely, permanently discontinuing a product without plans to resume use of its trademark may support a finding of abandonment.

The law requires genuine use in the ordinary course of trade. Token commercial activity undertaken merely to preserve trademark rights is insufficient.

Abandonment can also affect some goods or services without affecting others. A business may discontinue use of a trademark for one product line while retaining rights associated with its remaining products.

Trademark rights can also be abandoned through conduct that causes a mark to become generic or otherwise lose its significance as a trademark. However, abandonment through nonuse is particularly relevant when another business seeks to adopt a previously used name.

## Does a Trademark Automatically Become Available After Three Years of Nonuse?

No. Under the Lanham Act, three consecutive years of nonuse constitute *prima facie* evidence of abandonment.

In practical terms, three years of nonuse create a rebuttable presumption of abandonment. The trademark owner may present evidence challenging that presumption, including evidence of plans to resume commercial use.

A general desire to use a trademark again someday may not be sufficient. Courts may examine whether the circumstances demonstrate an intention to resume use within the reasonably foreseeable future.

Abandonment can also occur before three years have elapsed if both nonuse and an intention not to resume use are established.

The three-year rule is an evidentiary standard, not an automatic expiration date for trademark rights or a mandatory waiting period before abandonment can occur.

## What If the Previous Business Has Closed?

The closure, dissolution, or bankruptcy of a business does not necessarily establish that its trademarks have been abandoned.

Trademark rights may have been transferred to another company through an acquisition or asset sale. A trademark may also remain in qualifying commercial use through licensees or successor businesses.

A notable example involved the LEHMAN BROTHERS trademark.

Following Lehman Brothers’ bankruptcy in 2008, Barclays acquired certain businesses and assets, including the LEHMAN BROTHERS trademarks. Barclays subsequently allowed the acquired U.S. trademark registrations to expire.

When an unrelated company later sought to register LEHMAN BROTHERS for alcoholic beverages and restaurant services, a dispute arose over whether the earlier trademark had been abandoned.

In *Tiger Lily Ventures Ltd. v. Barclays Capital Inc.* (2022), the U.S. Court of Appeals for the Federal Circuit upheld a finding that the trademark had not been abandoned. The evidence included continued licensed use of the mark in connection with financial activities undertaken while winding down a Lehman Brothers affiliate, as well as Barclays’ own continued use.

The case illustrates why the disappearance of a business from its traditional marketplace does not necessarily extinguish its trademark rights.

## Can I Register a Trademark That Has Actually Been Abandoned?

Generally, yes. A genuinely abandoned trademark may become available for another party to adopt and register.

However, other businesses may have independently established rights in identical or similar marks. The proposed trademark must also satisfy ordinary registration requirements, including those concerning distinctiveness and likelihood of confusion.

A business adopting an abandoned trademark generally establishes new trademark rights rather than inheriting the former owner’s historical rights or priority.

For example, a company adopting a previously abandoned brand name ordinarily cannot claim the original owner’s decades of commercial use as its own.

The new owner’s rights generally depend on its own qualifying use and applicable trademark priority rules.

## What If Consumers Still Recognize the Previous Brand?

Some discontinued brands remain recognizable long after their products or services disappear from the marketplace. This continuing commercial recognition is sometimes described as **residual goodwill**.

Consumers may remember the former brand or assume that newly introduced products bearing the name are connected with the original business.

Continued public recognition does not, by itself, prevent legal abandonment. However, the circumstances surrounding a former brand’s reputation may raise additional legal questions, particularly if a new business markets its products in a manner that falsely suggests a connection with the former owner.

These issues can be particularly relevant when a business seeks to revive a historically recognizable brand.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Evaluate Whether an Abandoned Mark Is Available?

When evaluating a previously used trademark, an attorney may examine several sources of information beyond the current status of its USPTO record:

- **Trademark records:** The history of relevant applications and registrations, including why they became inactive.

- **Commercial use:** Evidence of current and historical use of the mark.

- **Ownership history:** Potential assignments, acquisitions, or licensing arrangements.

- **Intent to resume use:** Evidence concerning whether discontinued commercial activity is temporary or permanent.

- **Other trademark rights:** Potential conflicts involving unrelated businesses using identical or similar marks.

The USPTO database provides valuable information, but it does not necessarily establish the complete history or current status of the underlying trademark rights.

These findings help attorneys evaluate whether a previously used mark may be available for adoption and registration.

## The Bottom Line

An abandoned trademark may become available for another business to use and register. A dead application or expired federal registration, however, does not necessarily establish legal abandonment.

Even three consecutive years of nonuse create a rebuttable presumption rather than conclusively establishing that trademark rights have been lost.

Whether a previously used trademark is available depends on the circumstances surrounding its use, the former owner’s rights, and other applicable trademark requirements.
