You've selected a name for your business, completed a trademark clearance review, and filed an application with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO).

What happens next?

Filing begins a process that can take months and may involve additional legal work. Before registration, the USPTO must examine your application, resolve any outstanding issues, and provide an opportunity for third parties to oppose registration.

Here's what that process generally looks like.

## What Happens Immediately After Filing?

Once an application meets the USPTO's minimum filing requirements, it receives a serial number that identifies the application throughout the registration process.

The application also becomes part of the USPTO's public records.

Its progress can be monitored through the Trademark Status and Document Retrieval (TSDR) system, which provides access to application status, official correspondence, and other documents.

At this stage, your application is pending. The USPTO has not yet approved the mark for registration.

You may generally use the ™ symbol, or ℠ for a service mark, to indicate a trademark claim even before filing an application. The ® symbol, however, is reserved for marks that have received federal registration and may be used only with the goods or services covered by that registration.

## How Long Does Trademark Examination Take?

Applications generally wait several months before being assigned to a USPTO examining attorney.

As of October 1, 2026, the USPTO reported an average of approximately 4.3 months from filing to the first examining action.

That figure does not represent the total time required for registration. The process may take longer if the examining attorney raises objections, a third party opposes registration, or the applicant has not yet established qualifying trademark use.

The USPTO publishes updated estimates on its Trademark Processing Wait Times page.

## What Does the Examining Attorney Review?

A USPTO examining attorney evaluates whether the application meets federal registration requirements.

The examination may address:

- Potential conflicts with existing federal trademark applications or registrations.

- Whether the proposed mark is sufficiently distinctive.

- Whether the goods or services are properly identified.

- Whether the application satisfies its filing-basis requirements.

- Whether any submitted specimen adequately demonstrates trademark use in commerce.

For example, suppose a startup applies to register TempoGarden for a productivity app.

The examining attorney may evaluate whether a similar mark is already registered or pending for related goods or services. The attorney may also examine whether the proposed mark is merely descriptive or whether the application's description of the software requires clarification.

If the application meets the relevant requirements, it proceeds toward publication. Otherwise, the USPTO may issue an *office action*.

## What Happens If the USPTO Issues an Office Action?

An office action is an official communication identifying legal or procedural issues with an application.

Some involve relatively straightforward matters, such as clarifying the description of goods or services. Others raise substantive objections, including likelihood of confusion with another mark or insufficient distinctiveness.

An office action does not necessarily mean the application will ultimately be refused.

Depending on the issues raised, an attorney may respond with legal arguments, supporting evidence, or permissible amendments. Certain refusals may require more extensive analysis.

For most domestic applications, the deadline to respond to an examining attorney's office action is **three months from issuance**. A three-month extension may be available for an additional fee. Different deadlines apply to certain international applications.

Failure to respond within the applicable deadline can result in abandonment.

If the USPTO maintains a final refusal, further proceedings may be available, including reconsideration or an appeal to the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board (TTAB).

## What Happens After the Application Is Approved?

Once the examining attorney approves the application, the proposed mark is published in the USPTO's *Trademark Official Gazette*.

Publication allows third parties who believe they would be damaged by registration to challenge the application.

Generally, they have 30 days from publication to file an opposition or request additional time to oppose.

An opposition is a proceeding before the TTAB. If one is filed, the registration process may be substantially delayed while the dispute is resolved.

If the opposition period ends without a successful challenge, the application advances to the next stage.

## When Does My Trademark Actually Register?

This depends largely on whether the application was based on existing trademark use or an intent to use the mark in commerce.

### Use-Based Applications

If the application was filed based on qualifying use in commerce, and the USPTO has accepted the required evidence, registration generally follows the publication period once any opposition matters have been resolved.

The USPTO then issues a registration certificate.

### Intent-to-Use Applications

An intent-to-use application follows a different process.

Suppose TempoGarden filed while its founders were still developing the app.

Even if the application passes examination and publication, the mark generally cannot register until the applicant establishes qualifying use in commerce.

The USPTO therefore issues a *Notice of Allowance*.

The applicant generally has six months from that notice to submit a Statement of Use with acceptable evidence or request a six-month extension.

Up to five six-month extensions are available, subject to applicable requirements and fees. This permits a maximum of three years from the Notice of Allowance to submit an acceptable Statement of Use.

The USPTO must accept the required evidence before registration can issue.

For pre-launch founders, the timing of registration may therefore depend on when their products or services begin qualifying commercial use.

## What Should Founders Know While an Application Is Pending?

Much of the prosecution process involves waiting for USPTO action. However, developments within the business can also affect a pending application.

For example, a startup may change its product, expand into new services, or revise its branding before registration.

These developments can raise questions about whether the pending application still reflects the company's intended trademark use.

The USPTO generally does not permit applicants to broaden the identified goods or services after filing. Material changes to the mark itself can also require a new application.

For these reasons, changes in branding or product offerings may warrant review by the attorney handling the application.

Pending applications also require deadline management. Trademark attorneys typically monitor official correspondence, track response dates, and coordinate any required filings with their clients.

Applicants should also be aware of fraudulent trademark notices.

Because federal trademark records are public, third parties may send official-looking invoices or communications requesting payment for unnecessary services. The USPTO warns that some solicitations falsely imply they come from a government agency.

Authentic USPTO correspondence can be checked through TSDR. The agency also maintains guidance on recognizing trademark scams.

## What Happens After Registration?

Registration is an important milestone, but maintaining it requires continued qualifying trademark use and periodic filings.

For most U.S. registrations not based on the Madrid Protocol, the principal maintenance deadlines are:

- **Years 5–6:** A declaration of continued use or qualifying excusable nonuse.

- **Years 9–10:** A declaration of use or excusable nonuse and an application for renewal.

- **Every ten years thereafter:** Additional maintenance and renewal filings.

Missing these requirements can result in cancellation or expiration of the registration.

Changes in ownership, the goods or services offered, or how the mark is used may also require legal attention.

## The Bottom Line

Filing a trademark application is an important first step, but registration is not automatic.

The USPTO must examine the application, address any outstanding legal requirements, and allow third parties an opportunity to oppose registration. Applicants filing before commercial use must also satisfy additional requirements before registration can issue.

For business owners, understanding this process helps explain why trademark prosecution can involve more than preparing an initial application.

It also underscores the importance of maintaining accurate application information, responding to official correspondence, and tracking deadlines throughout the registration process.
