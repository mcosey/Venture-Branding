Starting a business introduces founders to several unfamiliar tax documents. Some establish the company’s tax identity, while others relate to reporting income, expenses, and payments.

Which documents a founder encounters depends on the company’s structure, tax classification, and business activities. This article introduces some of the most common.

## 1. Your Employer Identification Number (EIN)

An Employer Identification Number, or EIN, is a federal tax identification number issued by the Internal Revenue Service (IRS).

Despite its name, a business does not need employees to obtain an EIN. Certain businesses, including some single-member LLCs, may not need a separate EIN for federal tax purposes but may obtain one for banking or other business purposes.

Founders may encounter three related documents:

- **Form SS-4:** The application for an EIN. Eligible applicants may also apply through the IRS’s online application.

- **CP 575:** An IRS notice confirming the company’s EIN.

- **Letter 147C:** An IRS letter verifying a previously assigned EIN.

Banks and other institutions may request written confirmation of a company’s EIN when providing business services.

Obtaining an EIN directly from the IRS is free.

## 2. Understanding How Your Business Is Taxed

A company’s legal structure and federal tax classification are not necessarily the same.

An LLC, for example, is a legal entity formed under state law. Its federal income tax treatment generally depends on its ownership and any applicable tax elections.

| **Business Structure or Tax Classification** | **General Federal Income Tax Treatment** |
| --- | --- |
| Single-member LLC | Income and expenses are generally reported on the owner’s tax return by default. |
| Multi-member LLC | Generally taxed as a partnership by default. |
| C corporation | Generally pays income tax separately from its shareholders. |
| S corporation | Generally passes income and losses through to its shareholders. |

These default LLC classifications apply to domestic entities. Eligible LLCs may also elect corporate tax treatment.

This explains why two founders who both operate LLCs may encounter different tax forms.

## 3. Annual Business Tax Returns

The federal income tax documents a founder may encounter include:

| **Document** | **Purpose** |
| --- | --- |
| **Schedule C (Form 1040)** | Reports business income and expenses as part of an individual’s tax return. Commonly used by sole proprietors and individually owned LLCs with default tax treatment. |
| **Form 1065** | Reports the income, expenses, and other tax items of a business taxed as a partnership. |
| **Form 1120** | The federal income tax return for a C corporation. |
| **Form 1120-S** | The federal income tax return for an S corporation. |
| **Schedule K-1** | Reports an owner’s share of income, losses, deductions, and other tax items from a partnership or S corporation. |

For example, a founder operating a single-member LLC with default tax treatment may report business income and expenses on Schedule C as part of their personal tax return.

A founder who owns an interest in an LLC taxed as a partnership may instead receive a Schedule K-1 from the company.

Under pass-through taxation, an owner may owe taxes on their share of business income even if the company has not distributed that income to them in cash.

## 4. Other Common Tax Documents

### Form W-9: Taxpayer Identification Information

A W-9 provides a requesting party with a U.S. taxpayer’s identification and certification information.

For example, if your business provides consulting services to another company, that company may request a W-9 for its tax reporting purposes.

### Form 1099-NEC: Nonemployee Compensation

Form 1099-NEC reports certain payments made to nonemployees for their services.

A founder who hires a freelance designer or software developer may encounter this form. Founders who provide services to other businesses may also receive one.

Issuing a Form 1099-NEC does not, by itself, establish that a worker is legally classified as an independent contractor.

### Form 1099-K: Payment Transactions

Form 1099-K reports qualifying payment transactions processed through payment settlement entities.

A founder selling products or software through an online payment platform may receive this document.

The reported amount reflects gross payment transactions rather than net business profit. It generally does not account for expenses, processing fees, or refunds.

Business income may still be reportable even if the company does not receive a Form 1099.

### Form 1040-ES: Estimated Taxes

Business owners who receive income without sufficient tax withholding may need to make estimated tax payments throughout the year.

Form 1040-ES helps individuals calculate and make those payments.

Whether estimated payments are necessary depends on the individual’s income, withholding, and other tax circumstances.

## 5. What If Your Business Has Not Made Any Money?

A newly formed business may spend months developing a product before generating revenue. However, having no revenue does not necessarily eliminate its tax filing obligations.

Domestic C corporations generally must file federal income tax returns even when they have no taxable income. Domestic partnerships may qualify for a filing exception if they have neither gross income nor expenditures treated as federal tax deductions or credits.

Businesses may also incur formation expenses, software costs, and professional fees before beginning operations. The tax treatment of these expenditures varies.

A qualified tax professional can help determine whether a company has filing obligations and how its expenses should be reported, even before it begins generating revenue.

## 6. Keeping Business Tax Records

Common business tax records include:

- EIN confirmation notices.

- Previously filed tax returns.

- Receipts and invoices.

- Business bank statements.

- Tax documents received from customers and payment platforms.

- Records of business income and expenses.

These documents help substantiate amounts reported on tax returns and distinguish business transactions from personal expenditures.

Organized records can make tax preparation easier, particularly as the business grows.

## 7. State and Local Tax Obligations

Depending on where a company operates and the nature of its activities, it may also encounter state income taxes, franchise taxes, sales and use taxes, or local business taxes.

Some obligations may arise even when a company is not profitable. Operating in multiple states may also introduce additional tax registration and reporting requirements.

These obligations vary by jurisdiction and are separate from the federal tax documents discussed above.

## Conclusion

Founders do not need to become tax specialists to understand their businesses. Familiarity with basic tax documents can make it easier to communicate with accountants and recognize documents requested by customers, banks, and government agencies.

Which documents ultimately apply depends on the company’s structure and activities. A qualified tax professional can help founders understand those requirements as their businesses develop.
