You've settled on a name for your startup. You've purchased the domain, started developing your product, and perhaps even designed a logo.

But you haven't launched, and you aren't making any money yet.

Can you still trademark the name?

**Yes. You can apply for federal trademark registration before your business launches.** You don't necessarily need customers, revenue, or even a registered LLC.

You do, however, need a genuine intention to use the trademark in commerce. And there are a few things worth considering before you file.

## How Can I Apply Before Launching?

Ordinarily, trademark rights in the United States arise through the use of a mark in connection with goods or services.

But federal law also allows businesses to apply for registration *before* that use begins.

Under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act, an applicant with a **bona fide intent to use** a trademark in commerce can file what's known as an *intent-to-use* (ITU) application.

Suppose you're developing a productivity app called TempoGarden. You've chosen the name, started coding the app, and plan to launch in six months.

You may be eligible to file an ITU application now, even though customers cannot yet access the product.

Why file early?

An ITU application can establish an earlier priority date if the mark ultimately registers on the Principal Register. Subject to existing trademark rights and other limitations, that priority can date back to when you filed rather than when you launched.

There's a catch: **an ITU application is not a trademark registration.** You'll eventually need to establish qualifying use of the mark in commerce before the USPTO can register it.

## What Counts as a Genuine Intent to Use?

You can't file a trademark application simply because you like a name and might use it someday.

A bona fide intent to use requires a genuine, good-faith plan to use the mark for the goods or services identified in your application.

For a startup, evidence of that intent might include product development records, prototypes, business plans, market research, or communications with developers and potential suppliers.

You generally won't need to submit that evidence with your initial application. But it can become important if your intent is later challenged.

This doesn't mean your product must be nearly finished before you file. It means there should be something more than a speculative idea.

## Do I Need to Form an LLC Before Filing?

No. Individuals can apply for federal trademark registration.

But the identity of the applicant matters.

Imagine you file an ITU application personally and incorporate your startup several months later. You might assume you can simply transfer the application into your new company's name.

Unfortunately, it isn't necessarily that simple.

Federal law generally restricts the assignment of ITU applications before an acceptable allegation of use has been filed. An exception exists for certain transfers to successors of an existing, ongoing business.

Transferring an application improperly can jeopardize its validity.

If you intend for a corporation or LLC to own your trademark, it's worth considering that structure before filing. A company that hasn't legally been formed cannot ordinarily be named as the applicant.

## What If I'm Not Making Any Money Yet?

Revenue and trademark use aren't the same thing.

You can file an ITU application without having earned a dollar. And even when it's time to establish actual use, receiving payment isn't always required.

What matters is whether your activities satisfy the legal requirements for *use in commerce*.

Those requirements differ depending on whether your mark identifies goods or services.

For instance, launching a website with a “Coming Soon” message generally isn't enough to demonstrate use of a trademark for a product that isn't available.

Likewise, advertising a service you haven't begun providing generally won't establish qualifying service-mark use.

A functioning software product or genuine beta release may present a different situation, depending on how the mark is being used and whether the relevant commercial requirements are satisfied.

For founders, the point is simple: **you don't have to be profitable to establish trademark rights, but merely preparing to launch isn't necessarily actual trademark use.**

## What Happens After I File?

The USPTO examines your application and may issue an office action if it identifies legal or procedural problems.

If the application proceeds through examination and publication without a successful opposition, the USPTO issues a *Notice of Allowance*.

That notice does not mean the mark is registered. It means the application has reached the stage where you must establish qualifying use.

You generally have six months from the Notice of Allowance to file a Statement of Use with acceptable evidence, or request a six-month extension.

Up to five six-month extensions are available, subject to the applicable requirements and fees. This permits a maximum of three years from the Notice of Allowance to file an acceptable Statement of Use.

There are additional costs, too. As of October 2026, the USPTO's standard electronic application fee is $350 per class of goods or services, with a further $150 per class for a Statement of Use and $125 per class for each extension request. Additional application surcharges and attorney fees may apply.

Current fees are available on the [USPTO's fee schedule](https://www.uspto.gov/trademarks/trademark-fee-information).

## Should I File Before Launching or Wait?

That depends on how far along your business is.

If you've settled on a name, identified what you'll be selling, and are making genuine preparations to launch, filing early may be worthwhile.

But there are reasons to wait.

If you're still experimenting with names or expect your product to change substantially, an early application might create unnecessary costs.

For example, the USPTO generally won't allow you to broaden the goods or services identified in an application after filing. If your startup pivots into a substantially different business, you may need another application.

There is also a consideration for founders operating in stealth mode: **trademark applications are public records.** Filing can reveal a proposed brand before you're ready to announce it.

And regardless of when you file, trademark clearance matters. An ITU application doesn't establish that your name is available. Other businesses may already have superior rights in identical or confusingly similar marks.

A trademark search and appropriate legal analysis can help identify those problems before you invest heavily in the brand.

## The Bottom Line

You don't have to wait until your startup launches to begin pursuing trademark protection.

An intent-to-use application can be valuable when you've selected a name, have a genuine plan to use it, and want to establish an early filing date.

But filing early isn't always the best decision. Your proposed mark, intended products or services, ownership structure, and launch timeline all matter.

The goal isn't simply to file as soon as possible. It's to pursue protection at a point when you understand what you're building, what you're protecting, and what it will take to obtain registration.
