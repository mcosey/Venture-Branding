Raising venture capital changes more than a startup's ownership structure. Investors frequently negotiate rights that give them a voice in significant corporate decisions, even when they own a minority of the company.

A founder might retain 60% of a company's equity after a financing but still need investor approval to raise additional capital, amend certain corporate documents, or approve a sale of the business.

These arrangements allow investors to protect their investments and participate in corporate governance. They can also limit the decisions founders can make independently. Understanding how these rights operate helps founders evaluate the control implications of raising outside capital.

## 1. Does Majority Ownership Mean You Control the Company?

Founders often associate control with the percentage of company stock they own.

Ownership matters, particularly for decisions requiring stockholder approval. However, not every corporate decision is made through a general stockholder vote.

For a typical Delaware corporation, authority is divided among stockholders, directors, and officers.

**Stockholders** elect directors and vote on matters requiring stockholder approval. Different classes or series of stock may have distinct voting rights.

**Directors** oversee the company's management and authorize decisions within the board's authority, including significant financing transactions and other major corporate actions.

**Officers**, including the chief executive officer, manage the company's operations under the direction and authority of the board.

Venture investors can influence corporate decisions through board representation, preferred stock voting rights, and negotiated approval requirements.

A founder's ownership percentage therefore does not necessarily determine which decisions the founder can make without investor involvement.

### Example: A Founder Retains 60% Ownership

Suppose a startup completes a Series A financing with the following ownership structure:

| **Stockholder group** | **Ownership** |
| --- | --- |
| Founder | 60% |
| Series A investors | 40% |
| **Total** | **100%** |

Assume these percentages also represent the parties' voting power on matters submitted to all shares voting together.

The founder retains a majority of that voting power. However, the financing establishes a three-member board consisting of one founder designee, one investor designee, and one independent director selected through an agreed process.

A voting agreement requires the participating stockholders to support that board composition.

The Series A Preferred Stock also carries protective provisions requiring approval from holders of a majority of the outstanding Series A shares before certain actions can be taken.

The founder therefore retains majority voting power but does not necessarily control board decisions or actions requiring separate Series A approval.

## 2. How Do Venture Investors Obtain Board Representation?

Venture capital investors frequently negotiate the right to designate one or more directors as part of a priced financing.

For Delaware corporations, Section 141 of the Delaware General Corporation Law generally places management of corporate business and affairs under the direction of the board, subject to applicable statutory and charter provisions.

Board representation gives investors a formal role in corporate oversight.

### How Board Seats Are Allocated

A venture financing might establish a three-member or five-member board.

For example, a five-member board could consist of two founder or common stockholder designees, two investor designees, and one independent director selected through an agreed process.

The arrangement may change as the company raises additional financing.

Director designation rights may be implemented through voting agreements, provisions in the company's certificate of incorporation, or a combination of the two.

These arrangements address how directors are selected, removed, and replaced, subject to applicable law.

### Why Board Composition Matters

Return to the founder who owns 60% of the company's equity.

The company's three-member board includes one founder designee, one investor designee, and one independent director.

Assuming each director has one vote and ordinary majority approval requirements apply, most board decisions require support from at least two directors.

The founder's 60% stock ownership does not give the founder's board designee two votes.

If the founder-designated and investor-designated directors disagree, the independent director's vote may determine the outcome.

This does not mean the independent director represents the investor. Directors owe fiduciary duties in their capacity as directors, regardless of who designated them.

Neither the founder nor the investor necessarily controls the board under this arrangement.

### Does an Investor Director Control Daily Operations?

Not ordinarily.

An investor-designated director participates in board decisions but does not automatically acquire authority to manage daily operations.

The CEO and other officers generally continue managing the business within their delegated authority, subject to board oversight.

However, a founder's ownership of stock is separate from their position as CEO. Depending on the company's governing documents and applicable agreements, the board may have authority to appoint or remove the CEO even if that individual retains substantial equity ownership.

Founders should therefore distinguish their rights as stockholders from their authority as officers and directors.

## 3. What Are Protective Provisions and Investor Veto Rights?

Board representation is only one way investors obtain influence over corporate decisions.

Venture investors also commonly negotiate *protective provisions*, which require specified investor approval before the company takes certain actions.

These provisions are frequently established as rights of preferred stock in the corporation's certificate of incorporation.

A protective provision may effectively give preferred stockholders a veto over a designated transaction, even when that transaction otherwise has sufficient board or general stockholder support.

### What Decisions May Require Investor Approval?

Protective provisions commonly address actions such as:

- Creating or issuing securities with rights senior to, or equal in priority with, existing preferred stock.

- Amending specified provisions of the certificate of incorporation.

- Declaring dividends or redeeming certain shares.

- Incurring indebtedness above a negotiated threshold.

- Changing the authorized size of the board.

- Completing certain mergers, acquisitions, or liquidation transactions.

Not every financing includes all these restrictions. Their scope, approval thresholds, and exceptions are negotiated.

Some protective provisions require preferred stockholders to vote together as a single group. Others grant separate approval rights to particular preferred stock series.

That distinction becomes important when a company has raised multiple rounds of financing.

### How Can Investors Block a Financing When the Founder Owns Most of the Company?

Consider our hypothetical company.

The founder owns 60% of the equity, while the Series A investors collectively own 40%.

Assume the certificate of incorporation requires approval from holders of a majority of the outstanding Series A shares before the company issues a new series of preferred stock ranking senior to Series A.

The company later seeks to raise a Series B financing by issuing senior preferred stock.

Even if the founder supports the financing and the board authorizes it, the company must obtain the required Series A approval.

If the Series A holders with sufficient voting power withhold their consent, the company cannot complete the proposed issuance on those terms without addressing that approval requirement.

The founder's majority ownership does not override the separate preferred stock right.

Investors negotiate these provisions because subsequent financings and other corporate actions can materially affect their economic interests. However, broadly drafted restrictions can also limit the company's flexibility to obtain additional capital.

Counsel should therefore evaluate the scope of each approval right, its voting threshold, and whether appropriate exceptions or narrower restrictions should be negotiated.

### Are Protective Provisions the Same as Board Approval?

No.

A transaction may require board authorization, stockholder approval under corporate law, and a separate preferred stockholder vote under the company's certificate of incorporation.

Additional contractual consent requirements may arise under investor agreements.

Delaware law permits certain contractual governance arrangements, including under Section 122(18) of the Delaware General Corporation Law, subject to applicable limitations.

Satisfying one approval requirement does not necessarily satisfy the others.

The relevant corporate documents and agreements must be reviewed together to determine which approvals are necessary.

## 4. How Do Voting Agreements Affect Founder Control?

A voting agreement is a contract through which specified stockholders agree to exercise their voting rights in particular ways.

In venture financings, voting agreements commonly address director elections and certain company-sale transactions.

### Director Elections

Suppose a founder and the Series A investors enter into a voting agreement requiring them to support designated director nominees.

The agreement might require the parties to vote for one founder designee, one investor designee, and one independent director selected through an agreed process.

Even though the founder holds a majority of the company's ordinary voting power, the founder may be contractually obligated to support the agreed board composition.

The agreement affects how the founder exercises voting rights associated with their shares.

These obligations may continue after closing, subject to the agreement's terms and termination provisions.

### Drag-Along Rights

Venture financing voting agreements also commonly contain *drag-along provisions*.

A drag-along provision may require specified stockholders to support a qualifying sale of the company once certain approval conditions have been satisfied.

For example, the agreement might require approval from the board, a specified percentage of preferred stockholders, and a designated group of common stockholders.

Once the conditions are satisfied, participating stockholders may be required to vote in favor of the transaction, sell their shares, or execute specified transaction documents.

Drag-along provisions can also contain protections concerning the consideration stockholders receive, the representations they must make, and the liabilities they may assume.

The specific obligations depend on the agreement. A drag-along provision does not eliminate corporate approvals otherwise required by law.

For founders, these provisions affect the circumstances in which they may be required to support or participate in a future acquisition.

## 5. How Can Investor Control Rights Change in Later Financing Rounds?

Governance arrangements established during a Series A financing may change as the company raises additional capital.

Series B or Series C investors may negotiate board representation, protective provisions, or contractual approval rights of their own.

These changes can affect the balance of authority among founders, earlier investors, and new investors.

### Changes to Board Composition

Suppose a company begins with a three-member board consisting of one founder designee, one Series A designee, and one independent director.

During its Series B financing, the company might expand the board to five members and grant the new lead investor a director designation right.

The parties would then need to negotiate the remaining seats and determine whether existing designation arrangements should be amended.

Although the founder may continue serving as CEO and retain substantial equity ownership, the founder's influence over board decisions may change.

### Multiple Investor Approval Rights

Successive financing rounds can also introduce additional approval requirements.

For example, an existing Series A protective provision may require approval before the company issues senior securities.

A proposed Series B financing might therefore require Series A approval.

After closing, the Series B Preferred Stock may carry protective provisions of its own.

Future transactions could require approval from multiple investor groups, depending on whether the preferred series vote together or separately and how their rights are drafted.

These requirements can complicate later financings, acquisitions, and amendments to corporate documents.

Counsel must review the existing governance arrangements to determine which consents, amendments, or waivers are needed.

## 6. What Should Founders Understand Before Agreeing to Investor Control Rights?

Investor governance rights are a conventional feature of venture capital financing. They generally protect investors against specified actions that could materially affect their investments and provide a role in corporate oversight.

The important question is how those rights are structured.

Before completing a financing, founders should understand the proposed board composition, the actions requiring investor consent, and any contractual restrictions on their stockholder voting rights.

These provisions should be evaluated together.

A founder might retain majority voting power while agreeing to a board structure that requires cooperation with other directors. The same financing might grant investors approval rights over future securities issuances and impose drag-along obligations governing a subsequent sale.

Each right serves a different purpose, but their combined effect determines the founder's practical authority.

Counsel can review the certificate of incorporation, voting agreement, and other investor contracts to identify the approvals required for significant corporate decisions.

Particular attention should be given to consent thresholds, exceptions, and whether the restrictions appropriately protect investor interests without unnecessarily limiting ordinary operations or future fundraising.

Founders should also understand how the arrangements may change in subsequent financing rounds.

## Conclusion

Raising venture capital can change how a company is governed even when its founders retain majority ownership.

Board representation gives investors a role in corporate oversight. Protective provisions may require investor approval for specified transactions, while voting agreements establish commitments concerning director elections and certain company sales.

These rights can protect investors, but they also affect the decisions founders can make independently.

Understanding the distinction between ownership, board authority, and investor approval rights helps founders evaluate the governance consequences of a proposed financing.
