A founder can now build a functioning software application in a weekend using artificial intelligence. With tools that generate code from ordinary-language instructions, entrepreneurs can create websites, connect databases, process payments, and launch products without extensive programming experience.

This approach, commonly called *vibe coding*, has made software development considerably more accessible.

But a website that looks finished may contain problems its creator cannot see.

A tracking pixel might transmit visitor information before someone accepts a cookie banner. A subscription feature might charge customers without adequately disclosing its renewal terms. A database might expose records belonging to other users. A website might also be inaccessible to visitors who rely on keyboards or screen readers.

These problems are not unique to AI-generated software. The concern with vibe coding is that founders may deploy sophisticated features without understanding how they operate or what legal obligations accompany them.

Before launching an AI-built product, founders should understand the risks that can arise even when the software appears to work.

## 1. Your Website May Be Collecting Information You Never Intended to Share

Websites commonly use analytics tools, advertising pixels, session-recording software, and other third-party services to monitor visitor activity.

An AI coding tool may integrate these technologies when asked to add conversion tracking, advertising analytics, or customer insights. The resulting implementation may transmit information that the founder has never examined.

### When Tracking Begins Before Consent

Suppose a founder launches a subscription platform with a cookie banner offering visitors the option to accept or reject nonessential tracking.

The banner appears when the website loads, suggesting that visitors control whether tracking occurs.

However, an advertising pixel begins transmitting information before the visitor makes a selection. Even clicking "Reject" may not stop the transmission if the consent mechanism has not been properly connected to the tracking scripts.

Whether prior consent is legally required depends on the jurisdiction, technology, and information involved.

In the European Union and United Kingdom, nonessential cookies and similar technologies generally require consent before they are used, subject to applicable exceptions.

The legal framework in the United States differs. There is no universal federal requirement that every website obtain affirmative consent before using ordinary analytics cookies. Nevertheless, federal and state privacy laws may impose disclosure, consent, or opt-out obligations in particular circumstances.

California's privacy laws, for example, require covered businesses to honor qualifying requests to opt out of certain uses of personal information, including applicable Global Privacy Control signals. Website tracking has also prompted litigation under California's Invasion of Privacy Act, although the application of that statute to particular technologies remains contested.

The relevant question is whether the website's actual tracking practices comply with applicable law and the choices presented to visitors.

### Tracking Has Produced Real Enforcement Actions

In 2022, California's Attorney General announced a $1.2 million settlement with Sephora over allegations that the company failed to disclose certain sales of personal information and honor qualifying opt-out signals.

In 2023, the Federal Trade Commission finalized an order requiring BetterHelp to pay $7.8 million to resolve allegations that it disclosed sensitive consumer information to advertising platforms despite its privacy promises.

Neither case involved a vibe-coded website. Both illustrate how ordinary advertising technologies can create significant legal exposure when their operation conflicts with applicable requirements.

The risks can be particularly serious when tracking technologies receive health information, financial details, or other sensitive data.

### Why a Privacy Policy May Not Be Enough

A privacy policy does not necessarily describe what an application actually does.

Suppose a policy states that customer information is used only to provide the service, but the website also transmits account identifiers and browsing activity to advertising providers.

That discrepancy may create legal problems regardless of whether the policy was written by an attorney, generated by AI, or copied from a template.

A meaningful privacy review requires identifying the information collected, the third parties receiving it, and the purposes for which it is used.

It may also require testing the deployed website to determine what information is transmitted before a visitor makes a consent decision, after tracking is rejected, and when an applicable opt-out signal is received.

A working cookie banner and an accurate privacy policy are related but separate matters.

## 2. Your Website May Be Inaccessible to People With Disabilities

AI coding tools can produce visually impressive websites without necessarily addressing accessibility for users with disabilities.

A website may function well with a mouse while presenting barriers to someone using a keyboard, screen reader, or other assistive technology.

Common problems include missing descriptions for meaningful images, unlabeled form fields, insufficient color contrast, and interactive elements that cannot be operated without a mouse.

### How Accessibility Becomes a Legal Issue

Title III of the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) prohibits disability discrimination by covered places of public accommodation.

Courts have addressed how these obligations apply to websites and mobile applications. The legal analysis can differ by jurisdiction, particularly for businesses operating exclusively online.

The Department of Justice nevertheless maintains that the ADA's accessibility requirements extend to goods and services that covered public accommodations offer through their websites.

Consider a founder who uses AI to build an e-commerce website.

Customers can browse products, create accounts, and complete purchases. However, the checkout form lacks labels that a screen reader can identify, and a navigation menu cannot be opened using a keyboard.

Those barriers may prevent customers with disabilities from using the service.

### Website Accessibility Lawsuits Are Not Hypothetical

In *Robles v. Domino's Pizza*, litigation over the accessibility of Domino's website and mobile application resulted in a federal district court ruling against the company in 2021. The court ordered accessibility remediation and awarded damages under California law. The dispute was subsequently resolved.

The case involved conventionally developed software, but its accessibility issues are equally relevant to AI-built products.

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) provide widely recognized technical standards addressing keyboard access, alternatives for images, accessible forms, and other features.

For private businesses subject to ADA Title III, however, federal law does not prescribe a single WCAG version as a universal compliance standard for every website.

Founders should also avoid assuming that an automated accessibility tool guarantees compliance.

In 2025, the FTC finalized an order requiring accessiBe to pay $1 million to settle allegations that it overstated its AI-powered accessibility product's ability to bring websites into WCAG compliance.

Automated testing can identify certain problems, but meaningful accessibility review may also require manual testing and evaluation using assistive technologies.

A website can pass an automated scan while still containing barriers that affect actual users.

## 3. Your Application May Expose Customer Data Even When Everything Works

An AI-generated application may accept registrations, process transactions, and display customer information correctly while allowing unauthorized access to its underlying data.

These vulnerabilities can be difficult to identify through ordinary product testing.

### Authentication Is Not the Same as Authorization

Suppose a founder builds an online invoicing application.

Customers register, log in, and access dashboards displaying their invoices.

The application checks whether a visitor is logged in before retrieving an invoice. However, it does not verify whether that visitor is authorized to access the particular record.

A customer who changes an invoice identifier in a request may be able to retrieve another customer's information.

The authentication system works, but the authorization controls are inadequate.

Other vulnerabilities may arise from publicly accessible storage, exposed API credentials, improperly configured databases, or insecure software dependencies.

### When Security Failures Create Legal Exposure

The FTC has emphasized that businesses should maintain security measures appropriate to the information they handle.

Security incidents may also implicate state privacy and breach-notification laws, contractual commitments, or industry-specific requirements.

California's Consumer Privacy Act, for example, provides a limited private right of action for certain incidents involving specified personal information when the statutory conditions are satisfied.

Not every vulnerability automatically gives rise to liability. The applicable law, nature of the information, security practices, and circumstances of any unauthorized access matter.

For applications handling customer accounts or sensitive information, appropriate technical review may include authorization testing, database permissions, credential security, encryption, and third-party dependencies.

Founders should not assume an AI coding assistant has performed that review merely because it successfully generated the application.

## 4. What Happens If Your Website Is Hit With a DDoS Attack?

Security problems can also affect a website's availability.

A distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack attempts to overwhelm a website or its supporting infrastructure with traffic or requests, potentially making the service unavailable.

Suppose a founder launches a paid online platform without evaluating how it handles unusually high traffic or malicious requests.

An attack could interrupt customer access. Poorly protected application endpoints might also allow automated requests to consume substantial computing resources or generate unexpected hosting or API charges.

Being the victim of a DDoS attack does not, by itself, establish that the website operator violated the law.

Legal exposure may arise, however, if the company has contractual obligations concerning uptime, availability, security, or incident response. A service-level agreement, for example, may specify remedies when the service fails to meet agreed performance standards.

The consequences depend on the applicable contract, including any exclusions and limitations of liability.

Founders operating commercial applications should understand their hosting arrangements, review promises made to customers, and consider appropriate protections such as traffic filtering, rate limiting, monitoring, and DDoS mitigation.

## 5. Your Checkout and Subscription Features May Violate Consumer Protection Laws

AI coding tools can quickly create payment pages, free trials, recurring subscriptions, and cancellation workflows.

These features may implicate consumer protection laws that are not apparent from their visual design.

### Free Trials and Automatic Renewals

Suppose a founder asks an AI tool to build a subscription service offering a seven-day free trial followed by a $19 monthly charge.

The application successfully collects payment information and begins charging customers after their trials expire.

However, the checkout page does not adequately disclose the recurring charges or explain how customers can cancel.

The Restore Online Shoppers' Confidence Act (ROSCA) regulates certain online negative-option transactions, including recurring billing arrangements.

Among its requirements are clear disclosure of material terms before obtaining billing information, express informed consent, and a simple mechanism for stopping recurring charges.

Applicable state automatic-renewal laws may impose additional requirements concerning disclosures, acknowledgments, cancellation methods, and renewal notices.

Although a federal appeals court vacated the FTC's expanded click-to-cancel rule in 2025, ROSCA and other applicable consumer protection laws remain in effect.

The appropriate cancellation process depends on the applicable requirements. There is no universal federal rule requiring every subscription to be canceled in exactly the same number of clicks used to enroll.

### Misleading Website Features

Consumer protection concerns extend beyond payment processing.

A founder might ask an AI coding assistant to make a landing page more persuasive. The resulting website could include a countdown timer, an inventory warning, or customer testimonials.

If the countdown falsely suggests an expiring offer, the inventory warning is fabricated, or the testimonials describe experiences that never occurred, the content may be deceptive.

In 2024, the FTC adopted a rule addressing specified deceptive practices involving consumer reviews and testimonials, including fabricated reviews attributed to nonexistent individuals.

AI-generated language is not automatically unlawful. The concern arises when a testimonial falsely represents a genuine customer's experience or otherwise violates applicable advertising rules.

Product claims also require attention. Describing software as secure, accurate, or capable of delivering particular results may require appropriate substantiation.

Those obligations apply regardless of whether the marketing content was created by a person or an AI system.

## 6. Small Features Can Trigger Additional Legal Requirements

Some legal obligations arise only when an application includes particular functions or serves particular users.

The ease of adding features through AI makes these requirements easy to overlook.

### Marketing Emails and Text Messages

A founder may ask an AI coding agent to send promotional emails after registration or automated text messages when a customer abandons checkout.

Those communications may be subject to the CAN-SPAM Act, the Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA), and other applicable laws.

Commercial emails generally must comply with requirements concerning sender information, subject lines, physical mailing addresses, and opt-out mechanisms.

Marketing text messages may involve different consent requirements depending on the communication method and applicable rules.

Operational messages, such as account verification codes, should also be distinguished from promotional communications.

### Children's Information

An application directed to children may trigger obligations under the Children's Online Privacy Protection Act (COPPA).

COPPA also applies to certain general-audience services with actual knowledge that they are collecting personal information from children under 13.

Covered operators may be required to provide notices, obtain verifiable parental consent, and comply with requirements governing children's information.

COPPA does not, however, require every general-audience website to install an age-verification screen.

The applicable obligations depend on the product, its intended audience, and its data collection practices.

### User-Generated Content

A founder who adds image uploads, public profiles, or user-generated posts should consider whether the service may host infringing material.

Section 512 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) provides qualifying online service providers with limitations on copyright liability when statutory conditions are satisfied.

For relevant hosting activities, obtaining the applicable safe-harbor protection generally requires designating and registering an agent to receive copyright infringement notices and complying with notice-and-takedown requirements, among other conditions.

Failure to qualify for a safe harbor does not automatically make a platform liable for every infringing upload. It means the platform may lack that statutory protection if an infringement claim arises.

Each additional feature can introduce obligations beyond the code needed to make it work.

## 7. Who Owns the AI-Generated Code?

The legal risks of vibe coding also extend to the software itself.

Founders should understand what intellectual property rights their companies possess and whether the code incorporates material belonging to others.

### Copyright Protection and Human Authorship

Many AI coding providers address ownership of generated outputs in their terms of service.

However, a contractual assignment of output rights does not necessarily establish that every generated element qualifies for copyright protection.

Under U.S. law, human authorship remains central to copyright eligibility.

The U.S. Copyright Office has explained that AI-assisted works may qualify for protection where sufficient expressive elements are attributable to human authors, including qualifying creative modifications or arrangements.

Purely AI-generated material without sufficient human authorship generally does not receive copyright protection.

The treatment of substantially AI-generated software remains fact-specific and developing.

Founders should also ensure their companies have appropriate rights to contributions made by employees, contractors, and cofounders.

### Third-Party Code and Software Licenses

AI coding tools may generate code resembling existing software or install third-party libraries.

Some components may carry licensing obligations, including attribution, preservation of notices, or other conditions.

Using an AI model trained on open-source software does not automatically subject its output to an open-source license. The relevant analysis concerns protected third-party code or licensed components actually incorporated into the application.

Founders should also consider what information they provide to AI coding services. Uploading proprietary source code, customer records, or third-party confidential information may raise contractual and trade-secret issues depending on the circumstances.

Unresolved ownership or licensing questions can become important during fundraising or acquisition due diligence.

## 8. What Should Founders Review Before Launching?

There is no universal checklist that establishes legal compliance for every website. The appropriate review depends on the application's functionality, the information it handles, the people using it, and the jurisdictions involved.

Several questions can help counsel and technical professionals identify the areas requiring attention:

**Privacy and tracking:** What information does the website collect or transmit? Which third parties receive it? Do consent mechanisms and applicable opt-out controls work as represented?

**Accessibility:** Can visitors navigate without a mouse? Are forms and interactive elements accessible to screen readers? Has the website undergone meaningful accessibility testing?

**Security:** Can users access information belonging to others? Are databases, credentials, and other sensitive resources appropriately protected? Has the application been tested for foreseeable vulnerabilities?

**Service availability:** What happens during excessive traffic or infrastructure failures? Has the company made contractual commitments concerning uptime or service performance?

**Payments and marketing:** Are recurring billing terms adequately disclosed? Can customers cancel as required? Are marketing communications, testimonials, and product claims compliant with applicable law?

**Intellectual property:** Does the company possess appropriate rights in its code and content? Have material third-party components, confidentiality commitments, and licensing obligations been reviewed?

An AI coding assistant can help identify potential issues, but a generated compliance report cannot conclusively establish that an application meets its legal obligations.

Many questions require evaluating how the deployed website actually behaves, the company's business practices, and the laws applicable to the product.

Depending on the circumstances, appropriate review may involve security testing, accessibility specialists, and legal counsel.

## Conclusion

Vibe coding has made it possible to build sophisticated websites and applications without the traditional barriers of software development.

But a functioning product may still transmit information improperly, exclude users with disabilities, expose customer records, or contain features that violate consumer protection requirements.

These risks do not mean founders should avoid AI-assisted development. Most of the relevant legal obligations apply regardless of whether software is written by human developers or generated with AI.

What matters is how the finished product operates.

Founders who understand that distinction can take advantage of AI-assisted development while addressing the legal and operational risks of bringing their products to market.
