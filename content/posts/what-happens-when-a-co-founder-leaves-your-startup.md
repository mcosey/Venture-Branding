Starting a company with someone often means sharing years of work, financial risks, and major business decisions. But not every founding relationship lasts as long as the business.

A co-founder might leave to pursue another opportunity, step away for personal reasons, or be removed following a disagreement. Whatever the circumstances, their departure can raise difficult questions about ownership, management, intellectual property, and the company's future.

What happens next depends largely on the agreements established when the business was formed and the rights the departing founder has acquired since then.

## **What Happens to a Departing Founder's Equity?**

One of the first questions is whether the departing founder gets to keep their ownership interest.

The answer depends on how the founder's equity was structured.

Many startups issue founders restricted stock subject to vesting. Under a typical arrangement, the founder receives shares when the company is formed, but the company retains the right to repurchase some of those shares if the founder leaves before completing a specified period of service.

A common vesting schedule runs for four years with a one-year cliff. After the first year, 25% of the shares vest, with the remainder vesting gradually over the following three years.

Consider a startup with two founders who each receive 500,000 shares, subject to four-year vesting.

If one founder leaves after two years, approximately 250,000 of their shares may have vested. The remaining 250,000 shares may be subject to repurchase by the company, depending on the stock purchase agreement.

The repurchase price is often based on the founder's original purchase price, although the agreement controls.

This distinction matters because founder equity commonly involves shares already issued to the founder. Unvested shares do not necessarily disappear when the founder leaves. The company generally must exercise its contractual repurchase right in accordance with the agreement, including any applicable deadlines and corporate-law requirements.

If the company fails to exercise that right properly, the departing founder may retain shares the company otherwise could have repurchased.

### **What If the Founder Has No Vesting Agreement?**

Without an enforceable vesting or repurchase arrangement, a founder who leaves may retain their entire ownership interest.

For example, if two founders each own 50% of a company without vesting restrictions, one generally cannot force the other to surrender their shares merely because they stop working for the business.

The departing founder could remain a significant shareholder even while the remaining founder performs the work necessary to grow the company.

This can complicate future financing, corporate governance, and decisions about bringing in new leadership.

Founders sometimes negotiate a voluntary buyout to resolve these concerns. However, the company or remaining founders must have an applicable legal right or obtain the departing founder's agreement to acquire the shares.

## **Can a Founder Be Forced to Sell Their Shares?**

Removing someone from an operational role does not ordinarily eliminate their rights as a shareholder.

A founder who is terminated as CEO or resigns from employment may still own shares and retain associated voting and economic rights.

The company's governing documents and shareholder agreements may contain provisions allowing or requiring a repurchase in specified circumstances, such as termination for cause or another defined departure event.

However, these rights are not automatic.

A right of first refusal, for example, commonly allows the company or designated shareholders to purchase shares before they are sold to a third party. It does not ordinarily require the departing founder to sell merely because they have left the business.

Likewise, vested shares generally remain the founder's property unless an applicable agreement or legal rule provides otherwise.

Any proposed buyout should account for contractual obligations, necessary corporate approvals, and restrictions on company repurchases under applicable state law.

## **Does a Departing Founder Lose Their Management Rights?**

A founder may hold several distinct positions within a startup.

They might simultaneously be an employee, an officer, a director, and a shareholder. Leaving one position does not necessarily mean leaving the others.

For example, a founder who resigns as CEO may remain on the company's board of directors. Similarly, removing a founder from the board does not automatically terminate their employment or eliminate their shares.

For Delaware corporations, the rules governing officers and directors are addressed separately under the Delaware General Corporation Law.

Officer appointments and removals are generally governed by the company's bylaws, applicable board authority, and statutory requirements. Directors are subject to different election and removal procedures, including limitations arising under applicable law and governing documents.

A founder's voting rights may also allow them to influence corporate decisions after leaving daily operations.

Consider a company in which two founders each hold 50% of the voting stock. If one stops working but retains their shares, the remaining founder may still need the departed founder's cooperation for certain stockholder decisions.

If the founders also occupy opposing positions on an evenly divided board, disagreements could create a governance deadlock.

Resolving these issues requires examining the company's capitalization, board composition, governing documents, and applicable shareholder agreements.

## **Who Owns the Intellectual Property Created by the Departing Founder?**

For many startups, their most valuable assets include software, inventions, product designs, trademarks, and other intellectual property.

A founder's departure can become particularly contentious if the company never properly established ownership of those assets.

Founders sometimes assume that anything developed for the business automatically belongs to the company. That is not always correct.

Intellectual property ownership depends on the applicable law, how the work was created, and whether appropriate assignments or other ownership arrangements were established.

For example, suppose a technical co-founder develops the software underlying a startup's principal product.

If the co-founder executed an appropriate intellectual property assignment, the company may already own the relevant rights, subject to the agreement's scope and applicable law.

If no assignment was executed, the company may face uncertainty over whether it owns the software or has sufficient rights to continue using and developing it.

The issue can become especially complicated when a founder created important technology before the company was incorporated.

A departing founder generally cannot reclaim intellectual property validly assigned to the company merely because the relationship has ended. Conversely, the company cannot assume that every contribution became company property simply because the individual was a founder.

These questions can affect fundraising and acquisitions, where investors and buyers often examine whether the company owns or controls the intellectual property essential to its operations.

## **What About Confidential Information and Company Accounts?**

Even when intellectual property ownership is clear, a departing founder may retain access to sensitive information and important business systems.

These might include customer information, source code repositories, financial records, domain registrations, cloud infrastructure, banking platforms, and social media accounts.

The company should identify which systems the founder can access and arrange an orderly transfer or termination of that access.

Confidentiality agreements may also impose continuing obligations concerning proprietary business information and trade secrets.

However, the enforceability of post-departure restrictions depends on their terms and applicable law. Noncompetition and nonsolicitation provisions, in particular, require separate legal analysis and cannot be assumed enforceable in every jurisdiction.

The company should also determine whether the departing founder personally controls any accounts, registrations, or business relationships that need to be transferred to authorized company representatives.

An orderly transition is especially important when the departure is contentious.

## **Does the Company Need a Separation Agreement?**

Not every founder departure requires a negotiated separation agreement. Existing contracts may already govern the relevant rights and obligations.

Nevertheless, a separation agreement can be useful when the parties need to resolve matters their original documents did not fully address.

Depending on the circumstances, an agreement might cover the founder's departure date, resignation from company positions, equity treatment, any agreed share repurchase, outstanding compensation, return of company property, and continuing confidentiality obligations.

It may also address unresolved intellectual property assignments or provide for mutual releases of specified claims.

The agreement should not be treated as a substitute for required corporate approvals or compliance with applicable employment, tax, and securities laws.

Documenting the agreed terms can reduce uncertainty and help the company move forward.

## **What If the Founders Cannot Agree?**

Founder departures sometimes involve disputes about performance, ownership, decision-making authority, or the circumstances of the separation.

When the parties disagree, the company's governing documents and existing agreements become particularly important.

Some agreements establish procedures for mediation, arbitration, or resolving specified ownership and management disputes. Others may contain buy-sell provisions or mechanisms designed to address governance deadlocks.

If the documents do not provide a workable solution, the parties may need to negotiate an arrangement or pursue available legal remedies.

The options depend on the company's legal structure, applicable state law, and the nature of the dispute.

Founders should also recognize that the company's interests may differ from their individual interests. An attorney representing the company does not necessarily represent each founder personally, particularly when a departure creates competing interests.

## **How Can Startups Prepare for a Co-Founder Departure?**

The easiest time to address a founder's potential departure is generally before it happens.

Appropriate documentation at formation can reduce uncertainty if a founder eventually leaves.

Among the matters founders should consider are:

- **Vesting and repurchase rights:** When founders' shares vest, what happens to unvested shares after departure, and how any repurchase rights must be exercised.

- **Governance:** How directors and officers are appointed or removed, which decisions require approval, and whether the company's structure creates deadlock risks.

- **Intellectual property:** Whether the company has obtained appropriate rights to the technology, branding, and other assets developed by its founders.

- **Transfer restrictions:** Whether founders can sell their shares freely or must comply with contractual restrictions.

- **Departure procedures:** How resignations, equity repurchases, company property, confidential information, and outstanding obligations will be handled.

These arrangements should reflect the company's actual structure and the founders' expectations rather than relying on informal understandings.

## **Moving Forward After a Founder Leaves**

A co-founder's departure does not necessarily threaten the future of a startup. Difficulties often arise when the departure exposes uncertainty about ownership, governance, or rights to essential business assets.

Leaving the company and surrendering ownership are legally distinct events. Resolving a departure may require separate action concerning equity, management positions, intellectual property, and continuing contractual obligations.

Addressing these issues early, and obtaining appropriate legal guidance when a departure occurs, can help startups reduce disputes and maintain continuity.
