You've settled on a name for your business, purchased the domain, and started developing your brand. Then, while researching the name, you discover another company already using it.

Does that mean you have to choose something else?

Not necessarily.

Two businesses can sometimes legally operate under identical or similar names. Trademark law does not automatically grant one company exclusive rights to a word or phrase across every industry.

Whether the names can coexist generally depends on the businesses' respective trademark rights and the likelihood of consumer confusion.

## Why Can Two Businesses Have the Same Name?

Trademarks identify the commercial source of particular goods or services. Their protection is therefore connected to the products or services associated with the mark.

Consider two hypothetical businesses named Willowridge.

One operates a neighborhood bakery. The other develops payroll management software.

Although their names are identical, their offerings may be sufficiently unrelated that consumers would not assume the businesses are connected.

Now imagine a third business opening a café under the Willowridge name.

Because bakeries and cafés may offer related products, serve overlapping customers, and operate through similar channels, using the same name could raise different trademark concerns.

Neither example establishes a definitive legal outcome. The analysis depends on the parties' rights, commercial activities, and how consumers are likely to encounter their marks.

## What Is Likelihood of Confusion?

*Likelihood of confusion* is a central concept in trademark law. Under Section 2(d) of the Lanham Act, the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) may refuse registration of a proposed mark if it is likely to cause confusion with an existing registered trademark.

Likelihood of confusion also plays an important role in trademark infringement disputes.

The question is whether consumers might mistakenly believe that goods or services originate from the same business or that the businesses are affiliated, sponsored, or otherwise connected.

Consumers do not necessarily have to mistake one product for another.

For example, someone encountering a Willowridge café might recognize that it is a different establishment from the Willowridge bakery but still assume the businesses share an owner.

Trademark attorneys evaluate several factors, including:

- **Similarity of the marks:** Their appearance, sound, meaning, and overall commercial impression.

- **Relationship between goods or services:** Whether consumers might reasonably assume the offerings come from a common source.

- **Customers and marketing channels:** How and to whom the businesses offer their products or services.

- **Strength of the existing mark:** Its distinctiveness and recognition in the marketplace.

- **Other relevant circumstances:** Depending on the dispute, evidence of actual confusion, purchasing conditions, or existing coexistence may also matter.

The factors and their relative importance depend on the circumstances.

During federal trademark examination, the similarity of the marks and the relationship between the goods or services identified in the applications and registrations are generally central considerations. In infringement disputes, courts may also examine evidence of actual marketplace conditions.

## What If the Businesses Operate in Different Industries?

Operating in different industries may reduce the likelihood of confusion, but it does not automatically eliminate a conflict.

For example, a company selling fitness equipment and another offering exercise instruction might describe themselves as operating in different industries.

Nevertheless, their goods and services may be sufficiently related that customers could believe the businesses share a commercial connection. Similarly, downloadable accounting software and online accounting software services may be closely related despite their different delivery methods.

The analysis concerns the relationship between the offerings, not simply how the businesses describe their industries.

### What If the Trademarks Are in Different Classes?

Federal trademark applications organize goods and services into numbered categories called *trademark classes* .

Different classes do not necessarily mean two marks can coexist.

For example, downloadable software generally falls within Class 9, while software as a service generally falls within Class 42.

Businesses using similar marks in those classes may still present a likelihood of confusion.

Conversely, the fact that two businesses operate within the same class does not automatically establish a conflict.

Trademark classification serves an administrative purpose. Class numbers do not independently determine the scope of trademark rights.

## What If the Businesses Operate in Different States?

Geographic separation can matter, particularly when businesses rely on unregistered trademark rights.

In the United States, common-law trademark rights generally arise through qualifying use and are often tied to the geographic markets where those rights have been established.

Two businesses using the same name in separate markets may therefore have developed rights without initially encountering one another.

However, their priority dates, commercial activities, geographic reach, and subsequent expansion can complicate that arrangement.

Federal trademark registration introduces additional considerations.

Registration on the Principal Register generally provides nationwide priority rights for the identified goods or services, subject to applicable limitations and earlier rights. Under federal law, those priority rights may relate back to the application filing date, contingent on registration. Consequently, a business operating exclusively in Tennessee is not necessarily free from a trademark conflict merely because a federally registered trademark owner is located in California.

Likewise, federal registration does not automatically extinguish every earlier common-law right established in a particular geographic market.

An attorney evaluating geographically separated businesses may examine their histories of use, relevant filing dates, established markets, and applicable legal protections.

Different business addresses do not, by themselves, resolve the question.

For more information, see *[Don't Overlook Common-Law and State Trademarks](dont-overlook-common-law-and-state-trademarks.html)* .

## Does Registering an LLC Name Mean It's Available to Use?

Not necessarily.

A business's legal name and its trademark serve different purposes.

A legal business name identifies the entity registered with a state. A trademark identifies the commercial source of goods or services.

Sometimes the names are identical, but registering one does not necessarily establish rights in the other.

Suppose an entrepreneur forms Willowridge Technologies, LLC, after the Secretary of State approves the proposed entity name.

That approval generally establishes that the name satisfies the state's applicable business-registration requirements.

It does not determine whether using Willowridge as a trademark would conflict with another business's rights.

A company can therefore have a properly registered legal name while encountering separate trademark issues when marketing its products or services.

The same general distinction applies to assumed-name and DBA filings.

## Can Famous Trademarks Receive Broader Protection?

Yes. Certain famous trademarks receive protection beyond ordinary likelihood-of-confusion principles. Federal law recognizes claims involving *trademark dilution* , which can arise when use of another mark is likely to weaken a famous mark's distinctiveness or harm its reputation.

Unlike a traditional likelihood-of-confusion claim, federal dilution does not require proof that consumers are likely to believe the businesses are connected.

However, this protection is reserved for marks that satisfy demanding statutory requirements, including widespread recognition among the general consuming public of the United States.

A mark's popularity within a particular industry does not necessarily meet that standard.

The possibility of dilution is another reason unrelated products do not always eliminate potential trademark conflicts.

## What If Two Businesses Are Already Using the Same Name?

Two businesses may be using identical or similar names without necessarily infringing each other's trademark rights.

In some situations, businesses enter into *coexistence agreements* addressing how their marks may be used.

These agreements may establish limitations concerning goods or services, markets, or other measures intended to reduce consumer confusion.

However, a coexistence agreement does not automatically resolve every trademark concern or guarantee that the USPTO will approve a registration.

Its legal significance depends on the agreement's terms, the parties' rights, and the surrounding circumstances.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Evaluate Similar Business Names?

Evaluating potentially conflicting names typically involves both trademark clearance research and legal analysis.

An attorney may examine federal applications and registrations, state trademark records, business-name databases, and evidence of common-law trademark use.

The attorney then considers the similarity of the marks, the relationship between the relevant goods or services, priority, geographic rights, distinctiveness, and the likelihood of consumer confusion. Finding an identical business name does not automatically establish that a proposed trademark is unavailable.

Conversely, the absence of an identical federal registration does not guarantee that a proposed name is free from potential conflicts.

The analysis depends on the legal significance of existing trademark rights, not simply whether another business uses the same words.

## The Bottom Line

Two businesses can sometimes legally use the same name, even when their trademarks are identical.

Whether they can coexist depends on their respective rights, the nature of their goods or services, the likelihood of consumer confusion, and other relevant circumstances.

Operating in different industries, using different trademark classes, or conducting business in different states does not automatically eliminate a potential conflict. Likewise, registering a legal business name with a state does not independently establish that the name is available for trademark use.

Trademark rights are evaluated in connection with commercial activity and the relevant legal circumstances, rather than names considered in isolation.
