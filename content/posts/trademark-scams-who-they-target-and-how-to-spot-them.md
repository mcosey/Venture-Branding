You file a trademark application with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). A few days later, you receive an official-looking email informing you that an additional payment is required to complete your registration.

The message includes your trademark's name, application number, and business information. It may even display the USPTO's logo.

But the message isn't necessarily from the USPTO.

Trademark scams have become an increasingly serious problem for business owners. Scammers impersonate government officials, send misleading invoices, invent registration requirements, and pressure applicants into paying for services they do not need.

Recognizing these schemes is particularly important because trademark applications contain publicly available information that scammers can use to make their communications appear legitimate.

## How Common Are Trademark Scams?

The USPTO has reported a substantial increase in trademark-related scams.

In a July 2026 presentation, the agency reported that **trademark scam reports received by** **the USPTO increased 1,034% between fiscal years 2023 and 2025** .

The agency identified USPTO impersonation as the most prevalent type of reported trademark scam.

That figure measures the increase in reports received by the agency, not the total number of scams committed or the amount of money lost. Nevertheless, it illustrates the growing volume of suspicious activity being brought to the USPTO's attention.

The agency has responded by publishing educational resources, identifying fraudulent solicitations, and working with other federal authorities to address trademark-related fraud.

## Who Do Trademark Scammers Target?

Trademark scammers target businesses at nearly every stage of the registration process. **New applicants.** Businesses that recently filed applications may receive fake invoices, notices demanding additional fees, or communications claiming their applications require immediate attention.

**Applicants with pending applications.** Scammers may impersonate examining attorneys or claim that additional payments are necessary to avoid refusal or abandonment.

**Registered trademark owners.** Businesses with existing registrations may receive misleading renewal notices or requests for payment to preserve their trademark rights.

**Business owners who haven't filed.** Some scams begin with advertisements offering unusually inexpensive trademark registration or logo design services. These offers may lead to demands for additional payments or requests for sensitive information.

First-time applicants can be particularly vulnerable because they may be unfamiliar with the USPTO's procedures and official correspondence.

### How Do Scammers Know You've Filed a Trademark?

Federal trademark applications become public records.

Information associated with an application, including identifying and correspondence information, may be accessible through USPTO databases.

Scammers can use these records to identify applicants and personalize their communications.

For example, a fraudulent invoice might include the applicant's business name, trademark, filing date, and serial number.

Because those details are accurate, the recipient may assume the invoice is legitimate.

However, **accurate information about your trademark does not establish that the sender is** **affiliated with the USPTO** .

## What Are the Most Common Trademark Scams?

Trademark scams take several forms, but many rely on impersonation, fabricated obligations, or misleading offers.

### 1. Fake USPTO Invoices and Payment Demands

A business receives an invoice that appears to originate from a government agency or trademark registration office. The notice might claim that payment is required for publication, certification, registration verification, or another administrative service.

Some solicitations imitate government documents by using official-looking seals, legal terminology, or names resembling federal agencies.

Others advertise optional private services, such as inclusion in a trademark directory, in ways that may suggest payment is mandatory.

The USPTO does charge legitimate fees at specific stages of the registration process. However, applicants should not assume that an unsolicited invoice represents an official government obligation.

### 2. Impersonation of USPTO Employees

Scammers may contact applicants by telephone, email, or text message while pretending to be USPTO personnel.

They sometimes use the names of actual examining attorneys and may manipulate caller ID information to display a legitimate USPTO telephone number.

This technique is known as *spoofing* .

A caller might claim that an application cannot proceed unless the owner immediately provides credit card information or pays an outstanding fee.

The USPTO warns that its employees do not request personal or payment information by telephone, email, or text message.

### 3. False Warnings About Losing Trademark Rights

Some scammers exploit the fear of losing a valuable business name.

A message may claim that another company is attempting to register the same trademark and that the recipient must act immediately to preserve its rights.

Others falsely state that an existing application has been abandoned or that a registration is about to expire.

These communications may include unusually short deadlines, threats of penalties, or demands for immediate payment.

Actual trademark deadlines can have serious legal consequences, but a threatening message is not proof that such a deadline exists.

### 4. Fake Trademark Attorneys and Registration Services

Not every trademark scam involves someone pretending to be the USPTO.

Some businesses advertise inexpensive trademark registration services while misleading customers about their qualifications, fees, or relationship with the federal government.

The USPTO has warned about companies that impersonate law firms or falsely suggest that licensed attorneys will handle customers' applications.

Other schemes begin with inexpensive logo design or trademark services before introducing substantial additional charges.

For matters requiring attorney representation before the USPTO, the relevant practitioner must satisfy the USPTO's professional eligibility requirements.

A company's website, advertising claims, or professional-looking materials do not independently establish that it employs a qualified trademark attorney.

### 5. Fake Websites and Requests for Account Information

Some scammers create websites resembling official USPTO pages.

These websites may seek account credentials, payment information, or other sensitive details.

The USPTO also warns about unauthorized attempts to change contact information in trademark records, potentially interfering with an owner's control over its applications or registrations.

These schemes demonstrate that trademark-related fraud can involve more than unnecessary payments. In some cases, scammers seek access to accounts or identifying information.

## How Can You Recognize a Trademark Scam?

The USPTO identifies several warning signs commonly associated with fraudulent or misleading solicitations.

| Warning sign | Why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| Urgent demands for payment | Scammers often create artificial deadlines to discourage verification |
| Threats of immediate cancellation | The message may invent or misrepresent a legal consequence |
| Requests for passwords or sensitive information | The USPTO does not require applicants to disclose their account passwords to third parties |
| Unusual payment methods | Demands for wire transfers, gift cards, or payments to unfamiliar third parties are serious warning signs |
| Official-looking documents from unfamiliar organizations | Private companies may use names, seals, or formatting resembling government communications |
| Unexpected contact from a supposed USPTO representative | Names, email addresses, and caller ID information can be impersonated |

None of these factors should be evaluated in isolation.

For example, a legitimate private company may offer an optional trademark-related service. Such an offer is not necessarily fraudulent, but it should not be mistaken for an official USPTO requirement.

Similarly, an email appearing to originate from an @uspto.gov address is not conclusive proof of authenticity because email addresses can be spoofed.

## How Can Official USPTO Communications Be Verified?

One of the most useful resources is the USPTO's Trademark Status and Document Retrieval system (TSDR).

TSDR allows users to review the status and official documents associated with federal trademark applications and registrations.

Formal USPTO correspondence, including office actions, is generally available in the application's electronic record.

For example, if a message claims that the USPTO issued an office action requiring immediate attention, the application's document history can help determine whether such an action was actually issued.

The USPTO also provides a Trademark Assistance Center to address questions about trademark procedures and communications.

Because scammers sometimes include fraudulent links or telephone numbers in their messages, independently accessing official USPTO resources is more reliable than relying on the contact information supplied in a suspicious solicitation.

When an applicant is represented by a trademark attorney, that attorney can also review the correspondence and assess whether it reflects an authentic filing requirement.

## What If You've Already Paid a Trademark Scammer?

The USPTO publishes guidance for individuals and businesses that believe they have been targeted by trademark fraud.

Depending on the circumstances, potential steps include preserving the suspicious correspondence, contacting the relevant financial institution, and reporting the incident to appropriate authorities.

The USPTO accepts reports of suspected trademark scams at **TMScams@uspto.gov** .

The Federal Trade Commission also accepts fraud reports through ReportFraud.ftc.gov.

If the incident involved access to a USPTO account, unauthorized filings, or changes to application information, additional measures may be necessary.

The consequences depend on what information was disclosed, whether payments were made, and whether the scam affected an application or registration. A trademark attorney may help evaluate those issues.

## The Value of Knowing Who You're Working With

Trademark registration involves government correspondence, procedural deadlines, and legal requirements that may be unfamiliar to many business owners.

Scammers exploit that unfamiliarity by making invented fees and obligations appear routine.

Working with a qualified trademark attorney can provide a reliable point of contact for understanding official communications, evaluating unexpected requests, and distinguishing genuine legal requirements from questionable solicitations.

## The Bottom Line

Trademark scams are a growing concern, and their effectiveness often depends on appearing legitimate.

Scammers may possess accurate information about your application, use official-looking documents, or impersonate actual USPTO employees.

A communication's appearance, however, does not establish its authenticity. Understanding how these schemes operate, recognizing common warning signs, and knowing how official USPTO communications can be verified can help business owners distinguish legitimate trademark requirements from fraudulent or misleading solicitations.
