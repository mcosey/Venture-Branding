Startups commonly raise capital through simple agreements for future equity (SAFEs), convertible promissory notes, and preferred stock. Companies may also use venture debt and warrants as part of their financing arrangements.

These instruments differ in the rights they create, how investors receive returns, and what happens when the company raises additional capital or is acquired. For emerging companies and venture capital (ECVC) attorneys, understanding those distinctions is essential to structuring financings, calculating ownership, and reviewing transaction documents.

## 1. Categories of Venture Financing Instruments

Venture financing instruments generally fall into three categories:

- **Equity:** Securities representing existing ownership, such as common or preferred stock.

- **Debt:** Obligations to repay borrowed money, including convertible promissory notes and venture loans.

- **Contractual rights to future equity:** Agreements providing for the issuance of equity upon specified events, principally SAFEs.

A preferred stockholder already owns shares. A convertible noteholder is generally a creditor until conversion. A SAFE holder has contractual rights to receive equity or specified payments upon a triggering event, but does not initially hold stock or conventional debt.

These distinctions affect investor rights, payment priorities, and the company's obligations before a subsequent financing.

## 2. Simple Agreements for Future Equity (SAFEs)

A SAFE is a contract under which an investor provides capital in exchange for the right to receive equity upon a future financing or another specified event.

Y Combinator introduced SAFEs in 2013 as an alternative to convertible notes and adopted its post-money SAFE structure in 2018.

Unlike conventional convertible debt, a standard SAFE does not accrue interest or have a maturity date. The investor does not receive shares when the SAFE is executed. Instead, the agreement establishes the terms governing future conversion or payment.

### Valuation Caps and Discounts

A SAFE may provide favorable conversion economics through a valuation cap or discount.

A **valuation cap** establishes a ceiling on the valuation used to calculate the investor's conversion price. If the company's subsequent financing occurs at a sufficiently higher valuation, the cap allows the SAFE holder to convert at a lower price than the new investors.

A **discount** reduces the price paid by new investors by a negotiated percentage.

For example, suppose Series A investors purchase preferred stock at $2.00 per share. A SAFE providing a 20% discount would convert at $1.60 per share.

The resulting number of shares is calculated as follows:

**Conversion Shares = Investment Amount ÷ Conversion Price**

A $100,000 investment converting at $1.60 per share would therefore produce 62,500 shares.

Y Combinator currently publishes three principal U.S. post-money SAFE forms:

- Valuation cap without a discount.

- Discount without a valuation cap.

- Most-favored-nation (MFN) provisions without an initial cap or discount.

An MFN provision generally permits the holder to adopt the terms of a qualifying subsequent SAFE, subject to the agreement's amendment procedures. Customized SAFEs may include both a valuation cap and discount, with conversion determined by the contractual method more favorable to the investor.

### Pre-Money and Post-Money Valuations

A **pre-money valuation** is the negotiated value of a company immediately before a financing. A **post-money valuation**includes the capital invested in that financing.

**Post-Money Valuation = Pre-Money Valuation + New Investment**

Suppose a startup raises $2 million at an $8 million pre-money valuation.

| **Component** | **Amount** |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-money valuation | $8,000,000 |
| Investment | $2,000,000 |
| Post-money valuation | $10,000,000 |
| New investors' ownership | 20% |

The new investors acquire 20% because their $2 million investment represents 20% of the company's $10 million post-money valuation.

This simplified example assumes no additional capitalization adjustments, such as conversions of outstanding instruments or changes to the equity incentive pool.

In a priced financing, the agreed pre-money valuation is ordinarily divided by the contractually defined pre-financing capitalization to determine the new investors' price per share.

### Pre-Money and Post-Money SAFEs

The distinction between pre-money and post-money SAFEs concerns how their valuation caps and capitalization definitions determine the investor's conversion economics.

Under Y Combinator's original **pre-money SAFE**, the capitalization used to calculate the cap-based conversion price generally excluded other outstanding convertible securities. Additional convertible financing could therefore dilute the ownership ultimately attributable to earlier SAFE investments.

The **post-money SAFE** was designed to make the ownership sold through a capped SAFE more readily calculable.

For example, assume an investor contributes $500,000 under a post-money SAFE with a $5 million valuation cap.

**Implied Ownership = Investment Amount ÷ Post-Money Valuation Cap**

$500,000 ÷ $5,000,000 = **10%**

Assuming the cap-based conversion applies, the SAFE generally represents 10% of the relevant capitalization immediately before the new money in the subsequent priced financing.

If the company issues another $500,000 post-money SAFE at the same cap, the two SAFEs generally represent a combined 20% at that measurement point.

That percentage is not necessarily the investors' ownership after the Series A closes. The new investment and applicable equity incentive pool adjustments can produce additional dilution.

The post-money cap includes the SAFE financing according to the agreement's capitalization provisions. It does not include the new money raised in the subsequent priced round.

### Calculating the SAFE Conversion Price

Under YC's standard post-money valuation-cap SAFE, the cap-based conversion price is:

**SAFE Price = Post-Money Valuation Cap ÷ Company Capitalization**

The operative conversion provisions generally determine whether the investor receives shares based on that SAFE Price or the price paid by new investors in the equity financing.

The agreement's definition of *Company Capitalization* is critical. It generally includes specified outstanding equity, equity awards, reserved shares, and converting securities, while excluding specified new financing-related option pool increases.

Because converting securities are included, determining the share count may require interdependent calculations when multiple SAFEs or other instruments are outstanding.

An attorney should therefore use the definitions in the executed SAFE rather than simply dividing the valuation cap by the company's outstanding common shares.

### Conversion and Liquidity Events

Under YC's standard post-money SAFE, an equity financing generally triggers automatic conversion into preferred stock without requiring a minimum amount of new capital.

Depending on the conversion terms, the investor may receive the preferred stock issued to new investors or a separate series of SAFE preferred stock with adjusted per-share economic rights.

If the company is acquired before conversion, a liquidity event provision generally entitles the SAFE holder to the greater of its purchase amount or an amount determined under the agreement's as-converted calculation, subject to payment priorities and available proceeds.

A dissolution event may also trigger payment rights. Under the standard YC structure, SAFE claims generally rank behind outstanding indebtedness and creditor claims.

## 3. Convertible Promissory Notes

A convertible promissory note is a debt instrument that permits or requires outstanding indebtedness to convert into equity upon specified events.

Convertible notes are commonly used in seed and bridge financings when the parties wish to defer establishing a definitive equity price.

Unlike SAFEs, convertible notes ordinarily create a debtor-creditor relationship until conversion.

### Principal, Interest, and Maturity

Convertible notes typically establish four principal terms:

- **Principal:** The amount borrowed.

- **Interest:** The rate at which additional indebtedness accrues.

- **Maturity:** The date on which repayment or another contractual consequence may be required.

- **Conversion:** The circumstances under which outstanding debt converts into equity.

Accrued interest frequently converts alongside principal.

For example, a $100,000 note bearing 6% simple annual interest would have an outstanding balance of $106,000 after one year, assuming no payments or adjustments.

If the note requires conversion of principal and accrued interest, $106,000 would be used to determine the shares issuable.

### Two Methods for Determining the Conversion Price

Convertible notes often provide two alternative methods for calculating the conversion price: a valuation cap and a discount.

Where both apply, the note commonly provides for conversion at the lower price.

**Method 1: Valuation Cap**

A conventional pre-money valuation cap establishes a maximum valuation used to determine the conversion price.

**Cap Price = Valuation Cap ÷ Applicable Capitalization**

Suppose a company has 5 million shares in its contractually defined capitalization and an outstanding note with a $5 million valuation cap.

$5,000,000 ÷ 5,000,000 = **$1.00 per share**

The cap-based conversion price is $1.00.

The applicable capitalization is determined by the note's terms and may include outstanding stock, options, warrants, and reserved equity, while excluding certain convertible securities.

**Method 2: Conversion Discount**

The discount method applies a negotiated reduction to the price paid by new investors.

**Discount Price = New Financing Price × (1 − Discount Percentage)**

If Series A investors pay $2.00 per share and the note provides a 20% discount:

$2.00 × 80% = **$1.60 per share**

The discounted conversion price is $1.60.

Where the note provides the benefit of the lower price:

| **Method** | **Conversion price** |
| --- | --- |
| Valuation cap | $1.00 |
| Discount | $1.60 |
| **Applicable price** | **$1.00** |

The lower price results in more shares for the same amount of converting indebtedness.

### Worked Example: Conversion During a Series A Financing

Assume a company completes a Series A financing under the following terms:

| **Term** | **Amount** |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-money valuation | $10,000,000 |
| Applicable pre-financing capitalization | 5,000,000 shares |
| Series A purchase price | $2.00 per share |
| Convertible note principal | $100,000 |
| Annual simple interest | 6% |
| Time outstanding | 1 year |
| Principal and accrued interest | $106,000 |
| Valuation cap | $5,000,000 |
| Discount | 20% |

The financing price is $2.00 per share because the $10 million pre-money valuation is divided by 5 million shares.

**Step 1: Calculate the cap price.**

$5,000,000 ÷ 5,000,000 = **$1.00**

**Step 2: Calculate the discount price.**

$2.00 × 80% = **$1.60**

**Step 3: Determine the applicable conversion price.**

Assuming the note provides for the lower of the two prices, the applicable conversion price is **$1.00**.

**Step 4: Calculate the conversion shares.**

Conversion Shares = Outstanding Principal and Interest ÷ Conversion Price

$106,000 ÷ $1.00 = **106,000 shares**

Had the discount price applied instead:

$106,000 ÷ $1.60 = **66,250 shares**

The valuation cap therefore produces 39,750 additional shares.

This hypothetical assumes that the note's capitalization definition excludes the converting note, the Series A purchase price is calculated using the stated capitalization, and no other adjustments are required. Real transactions may require additional calculations involving multiple securities, equity incentive pools, or negotiated financing definitions.

### Qualified Financing Thresholds

Convertible notes often provide for automatic conversion upon a *qualified financing*, generally defined as an equity financing meeting a minimum new-money threshold.

For example, a note might require the company to raise at least $2 million in new capital before automatic conversion occurs.

A smaller financing may permit optional conversion or leave the note outstanding.

Counsel should review whether the financing threshold excludes amounts attributable to converting debt and whether noteholder consent is required for nonqualified financings.

### Maturity, Default, and Priority

If a qualifying financing does not occur before maturity, the note may require repayment, permit conversion, or allow an extension under its terms.

Amendments may require the consent of each noteholder or a specified majority, depending on the applicable agreements.

Convertible notes may also contain events of default, acceleration rights, and restrictive covenants. They may be secured or unsecured.

A secured note requires additional consideration of collateral, perfection, and priority. Unsecured noteholders generally rank ahead of equity holders in liquidation, although recovery depends on available assets and competing claims.

These creditor rights distinguish convertible notes from standard SAFEs.

## 4. Preferred Stock and Priced Equity Financings

In a priced equity financing, investors purchase stock at a negotiated price per share. Institutional venture investors commonly receive preferred stock.

Preferred stock may carry several rights that differ from common stock:

- **Liquidation preferences:** Priority in specified distributions upon liquidation or deemed liquidation events.

- **Conversion rights:** Rights or obligations to convert preferred stock into common stock.

- **Anti-dilution provisions:** Adjustments to conversion prices following certain subsequent issuances.

- **Protective provisions:** Consent rights over specified corporate actions.

- **Dividend rights:** Preferences concerning distributions.

The certificate of incorporation ordinarily establishes the stock's principal rights and preferences. Contractual protections may appear in an investors' rights agreement, voting agreement, or other financing documents.

The NVCA publishes model documents commonly used as starting points for these transactions.

### Conversion into Shadow Preferred Stock

SAFEs and convertible notes may convert into a separate series of preferred stock, commonly called *shadow preferred stock*.

For example, new investors might receive Series A-1 Preferred Stock, while existing SAFE or noteholders receive Series A-2 Preferred Stock.

The two series may carry substantially similar governance rights but different per-share economic terms reflecting their respective purchase or conversion prices.

This distinction can prevent a converting investor from receiving an unintended liquidation preference greater than the amount invested or converted.

Counsel should review the certificate of incorporation and conversion documents to determine the precise rights attached to the shares issued.

## 5. Venture Debt

Venture debt is financing provided to startups by banks or specialized lenders, often alongside or following an equity financing.

Unlike convertible notes structured primarily for future equity conversion, venture debt generally contemplates repayment.

Typical terms include interest, scheduled payments, security interests, restrictive covenants, events of default, and acceleration rights. Lenders may also receive warrants.

Venture debt can restrict later financings. For example, a loan agreement may prohibit additional indebtedness without lender consent. A subsequent convertible note financing could therefore require an amendment, waiver, or determination that the notes constitute permitted indebtedness.

The borrowing documents must be reviewed alongside the company's existing financing arrangements.

## 6. Warrants and Other Equity-Linked Instruments

A warrant grants the holder a contractual right to acquire specified securities at an established exercise price.

Warrants may accompany venture loans, equity financings, or strategic investments.

Unlike SAFEs, conventional warrants generally require payment of an exercise price, although some permit cashless exercise.

Important terms include the exercise price, expiration date, securities issuable, transfer restrictions, and adjustments for stock splits or other corporate transactions.

Convertible preferred stock is another equity-linked security. Its holder already owns equity but may convert those preferred shares into common stock under specified circumstances.

## 7. Comparing Venture Financing Instruments

| **Instrument** | **Legal character** | **Interest** | **Maturity or expiration** | **Existing equity ownership** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| SAFE | Contractual future-equity right | Generally none | Generally none | No |
| Convertible note | Debt | Generally yes | Generally yes | No, before conversion |
| Preferred stock | Equity | Not applicable | Generally none | Yes |
| Venture debt | Debt | Generally yes | Generally yes | No, absent separate equity rights |
| Warrant | Contractual purchase right | Generally none | Exercise deadline | No, before exercise |

These are customary characteristics. Individual agreements may provide materially different terms.

## 8. Practical Considerations for ECVC Attorneys

### Capitalization and Conversion Modeling

Before a priced financing, counsel should identify outstanding SAFEs, convertible notes, warrants, equity awards, and other securities affecting capitalization.

Each instrument must be reviewed for applicable conversion provisions, valuation caps, discounts, capitalization definitions, accrued interest, and conversion triggers.

Where multiple instruments convert simultaneously, the calculations may be interdependent. The resulting capitalization should be reconciled against the company's stock ledger, capitalization table, and governing agreements.

### Corporate Approvals and Existing Rights

Issuing financing instruments requires appropriate corporate authorization.

For Delaware corporations, counsel should evaluate the DGCL, certificate of incorporation, bylaws, and applicable financing agreements.

New issuances may require board approval, stockholder votes, preferred-stockholder consent, or waivers of contractual participation rights.

A subsequent financing may also trigger MFN provisions, conversion obligations, or restrictions under existing credit agreements.

### Securities Law Compliance

SAFEs, convertible notes, preferred stock, and warrants generally implicate federal and state securities laws.

Private offerings frequently rely on exemptions from Securities Act registration, including Rule 506(b) or Rule 506(c) of Regulation D.

The applicable exemption determines requirements concerning investor eligibility, solicitation, verification, disclosures, and filings.

Rule 506(b) generally prohibits general solicitation. Rule 506(c) permits general solicitation if all purchasers are accredited investors and the issuer takes reasonable steps to verify that status. Form D and applicable state notice filing requirements must also be considered.

## Conclusion

SAFEs, convertible notes, preferred stock, venture debt, and warrants provide different methods of financing emerging companies.

For ECVC attorneys, understanding these instruments requires examining the rights they create, calculating conversion prices and resulting shares, and identifying how each interacts with the company's capitalization and existing agreements.

The governing documents ultimately determine the securities issued, approvals required, and economic consequences of a financing.
