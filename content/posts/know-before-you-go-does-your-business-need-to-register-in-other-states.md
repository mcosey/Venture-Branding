Forming an LLC or corporation establishes a company's legal existence, but it does not necessarily satisfy all the requirements for operating a business.

Depending on where a company operates and the nature of its activities, it may also need to register in other states, obtain business licenses, or register with state and local tax authorities.

## 1. What Is Foreign Qualification?

Foreign qualification is the process through which a company obtains authority to transact business in a state other than its state of formation.

In this context, *foreign* does not necessarily mean that the company was formed outside the United States. It simply means that the company was formed under the laws of another jurisdiction.

Suppose a founder incorporates a company in Delaware but operates the business from Tennessee.

Delaware considers the company a domestic corporation, while Tennessee considers it a foreign corporation. Depending on its activities in Tennessee, the company may need to obtain authority to transact business there.

Foreign qualification does not ordinarily create a separate legal entity. The company remains the same Delaware corporation but becomes authorized to transact business in another state.

## 2. When Might a Business Need to Foreign Qualify?

States generally require foreign corporations and LLCs to register before engaging in activities that constitute transacting business within their jurisdictions. However, the applicable standards vary by state.

Activities that may raise foreign qualification questions include:

- Maintaining an office, storefront, warehouse, or other business facility.

- Employing individuals who regularly work within the state, including remote employees.

- Conducting ongoing business operations from a founder's residence.

- Regularly providing services or entering into contracts within the state.

Not every business activity requires foreign qualification.

Many states identify activities that do not, by themselves, constitute transacting business for registration purposes. These may include maintaining bank accounts, conducting certain isolated transactions, holding shareholder or board meetings, and engaging in interstate commerce.

Tennessee, for example, expressly excludes several of these activities under its corporate and LLC statutes.

Attorneys evaluating foreign qualification requirements typically consider the nature, frequency, and extent of a company's activities within the state, together with applicable statutes and judicial decisions.

## 3. What If Your Business Operates Entirely Online?

Online businesses can serve customers nationwide without maintaining traditional business premises.

Suppose a founder operates a Delaware software company from their home in Tennessee and has paying customers in California, Texas, and Florida.

Having customers in those states does not necessarily mean the company must foreign qualify in each one. However, employing workers or conducting regular business operations in those jurisdictions may change the analysis.

Tax obligations can also arise independently of foreign qualification.

In its 2018 decision in *South Dakota v. Wayfair, Inc.*, the U.S. Supreme Court rejected the rule that a business must have a physical presence in a state before the state can impose sales tax collection obligations.

An online business may therefore establish sufficient economic connections with a state, commonly called *economic nexus*, to trigger sales tax obligations under applicable state law.

The standards governing taxation and foreign qualification are separate. A company may be subject to one requirement without being subject to the other.

## 4. What Does Foreign Qualification Involve?

Although requirements vary by jurisdiction and entity type, foreign qualification commonly involves the following:

**Application for authority.** A filing identifying the company, its state of formation, registered agent, and other required information. The resulting authorization may be called a certificate of authority or foreign registration.

**Certificate of good standing.** Some states require documentation from the company's state of formation confirming its legal status. This may also be called a certificate of existence or certificate of status.

**Registered agent.** A foreign-qualified company generally must maintain a registered agent in the state where it registers, in addition to maintaining one in its state of formation.

**Ongoing compliance.** Foreign qualification may introduce additional annual reports, filing fees, and other administrative obligations.

A company that ceases operating in a state may also need to formally withdraw its registration. Discontinuing business activities does not necessarily terminate the obligations associated with an existing registration.

## 5. State and Local Business Licenses

Foreign qualification is separate from business licensing. Even a company operating exclusively within its state of formation may need licenses or permits from state, county, or municipal authorities.

### General Business Licenses

Some jurisdictions require businesses to obtain general business licenses.

For example, Tennessee maintains a business licensing framework under which applicable requirements may depend on a company's gross receipts, activities, and location. Qualifying businesses may need licenses issued by county or municipal authorities.

A company may therefore encounter licensing requirements from more than one local government.

### Industry-Specific Licenses and Permits

Certain business activities require specialized government authorization. Examples include:

- Restaurants and food establishments.

- Construction and contracting businesses.

- Childcare facilities.

- Businesses selling alcoholic beverages.

- Certain healthcare and professional services.

These requirements may apply independently of a company's registration with the Secretary of State. Some regulated activities may also require federal authorization.

### Home-Based Businesses

Operating a business from home does not necessarily eliminate local regulatory requirements.

Municipalities may regulate home-based businesses through zoning ordinances, home occupation permits, or other restrictions.

The applicable requirements may depend on customer visits, employee activity, signage, inventory storage, and the nature of the business.

A software company operated from a residence may present different considerations from a business that regularly receives customers at the same location.

## 6. Trade Names and DBA Registrations

A company may conduct business under a name other than its legal entity name.

For example, an LLC legally named Atlas Technologies, LLC might market its products under the name Atlas Software.

Depending on applicable law and how the name is used, the company may need to register an assumed name, fictitious name, or *doing business as* (DBA) name.

These registrations may be administered at the state or local level.

A DBA registration generally does not create a separate legal entity or, by itself, establish exclusive trademark rights in the registered name.

Business name registration and trademark protection serve different legal purposes.

## 7. State and Local Tax Registrations

Companies may also need to register with state or local tax authorities.

Common examples include registrations associated with:

- Sales and use taxes.

- State income and franchise taxes.

- Employment tax withholding.

- State unemployment taxes.

- State and local business taxes.

Obtaining authority to transact business in a state does not necessarily establish every tax account a company may need.

Accountants and tax attorneys typically evaluate these requirements based on the company's activities, revenue, employees, and connections to the relevant jurisdictions.

## 8. Understanding the Different Registration Requirements

Although these requirements frequently arise together, they serve distinct purposes.

| **Registration or Filing** | **General Purpose** |
| --- | --- |
| Entity formation | Establishes the company's legal existence. |
| Foreign qualification | Obtains authority to transact business outside the company's state of formation. |
| Business license | Authorizes applicable commercial activities within a jurisdiction. |
| Specialized permit | Authorizes particular regulated activities or uses. |
| DBA registration | Registers a name under which the company conducts business. |
| Tax registration | Establishes applicable tax reporting, collection, or payment accounts. |

Completing one registration does not necessarily satisfy the others.

## 9. What Happens If a Business Fails to Register?

The consequences of operating without a required registration or license vary by jurisdiction.

Potential consequences include financial penalties, unpaid fees or taxes, restrictions on business operations, and limitations on the company's ability to pursue claims in state courts.

For example, Tennessee generally prohibits foreign corporations and LLCs that lack required authority from maintaining proceedings in its courts until they obtain that authority. However, failure to register does not, by itself, invalidate their corporate or company acts or prevent them from defending lawsuits.

Under Tennessee's revised LLC statute, members also do not become personally liable for company debts solely because the LLC failed to obtain required authority.

Missing registrations may become relevant during financing or acquisition due diligence, when attorneys examine a company's compliance in the jurisdictions where it operates.

## Conclusion

A company's registration requirements depend on more than where it was formed. Its operating locations, employees, commercial activities, and industry can introduce additional legal and tax obligations.

Understanding the differences among foreign qualification, business licensing, trade name registration, and tax registration can help founders recognize when those questions arise.

Attorneys and tax professionals can evaluate the applicable requirements as a company begins operating or expands into new markets.
