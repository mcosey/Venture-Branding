A priced venture financing involves more than a single investment agreement. When a startup raises capital by issuing preferred stock, the transaction is typically documented through several agreements addressing the investment, the rights attached to the shares, and the ongoing relationships among the company and its stockholders.

The National Venture Capital Association (NVCA) publishes model documents widely used in U.S. venture financings. Five documents form the core of that framework: the Stock Purchase Agreement, Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation, Investors' Rights Agreement, Voting Agreement, and Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement.

Each serves a distinct purpose. Understanding how they fit together is essential to understanding the transaction itself.

## 1. The Five Core Venture Financing Documents

A venture financing often begins with a term sheet outlining the principal economic and governance terms. Once the parties reach an agreement, those terms are implemented through definitive documentation.

The five core documents divide the transaction into separate legal components.

| Document | Primary function |
| --- | --- |
| Stock Purchase Agreement (SPA) | Governs the purchase and issuance of preferred stock, representations and warranties, and closing conditions |
| Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation | Establishes the preferred stock's rights, preferences, and limitations |
| Investors' Rights Agreement (IRA) | Provides contractual information, registration, and future financing participation rights |
| Voting Agreement | Establishes voting commitments, including board composition arrangements and drag-along provisions |
| Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement (ROFR) | Regulates certain transfers of existing shares by specified stockholders |

The SPA governs the investment transaction. The certificate of incorporation establishes rights attached to the corporation's stock. The remaining agreements create contractual rights and obligations among their parties.

The NVCA forms provide a starting point rather than mandatory documentation. Their provisions are negotiated, and the appropriate structure depends on the financing. Smaller or earlier-stage investments may use simplified agreements.

## 2. The Stock Purchase Agreement: Governing the Investment

The Stock Purchase Agreement, often called the Preferred Stock Purchase Agreement, is the principal contract through which investors agree to purchase newly issued preferred stock.

The SPA identifies the securities being sold, their purchase price, the participating investors, and the conditions governing closing.

### Purchase and Sale Provisions

The SPA typically specifies the series of preferred stock, the price per share, and the aggregate investment amount. A schedule of purchasers identifies the number of shares allocated to each investor.

Suppose a company raises $5 million by issuing Series A Preferred Stock at $2.00 per share.

The SPA would provide for the sale of 2.5 million Series A shares and identify each investor's purchase commitment.

The rights attached to those shares, however, are principally established in the certificate of incorporation.

### Representations and Warranties

The SPA contains representations and warranties concerning the company and its business.

Common subjects include corporate organization, authority, capitalization, intellectual property, litigation, material contracts, financial statements, and legal compliance.

Investors also generally make representations regarding their authority, investment intent, and matters relevant to the securities offering.

Company representations may be qualified by a disclosure schedule, sometimes called a schedule of exceptions. For example, the company might represent that its capitalization is accurately described in the agreement, subject to disclosed outstanding options, SAFEs, or convertible notes.

The disclosure schedule must be reviewed alongside the representations it qualifies. An issue identified during due diligence should not be assumed to have been adequately disclosed merely because the parties discussed it.

### Closing Conditions

The SPA also establishes conditions that must be satisfied or waived before the applicable party is obligated to close.

Common conditions include required corporate approvals, filing the amended and restated certificate of incorporation, execution of the other financing agreements, and delivery of closing certificates.

The SPA may provide for an initial closing followed by additional closings. Some transactions also contemplate funding in tranches based on dates, milestones, or other conditions.

These provisions make the SPA the central document for coordinating the closing, although many of the investor's continuing rights are established elsewhere.

## 3. The Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation: Establishing Preferred Stock Rights

The certificate of incorporation serves a different function from the SPA.

For a Delaware corporation, it is a governing corporate instrument filed with the Delaware Secretary of State. A priced financing commonly requires an amended and restated certificate establishing the new preferred stock and its rights.

Under Section 151 of the Delaware General Corporation Law (DGCL), a corporation may establish classes and series of stock with differing rights and preferences through its certificate of incorporation or other authorized statutory mechanisms.

Amending the charter generally requires compliance with DGCL Section 242 and applicable corporate approval requirements.

### Economic Rights

The certificate commonly establishes several important preferred stock rights.

**Liquidation preferences.** These provisions determine the preferred stockholders' priority in distributions upon specified liquidation or other covered events. Under a conventional 1x nonparticipating preference, an investor generally receives its applicable preference before common stockholders receive distributions, or may elect to convert into common stock when doing so produces a better result.

**Dividend rights.** The certificate may establish dividend preferences, including whether dividends are cumulative or noncumulative.

**Conversion rights.** Preferred stock is commonly convertible into common stock. The certificate specifies the conversion mechanics and applicable adjustments.

**Anti-dilution protection.** Price-based anti-dilution provisions may adjust the conversion price following certain stock issuances below a specified threshold.

**Redemption rights.** Some preferred stock includes redemption provisions exercisable under specified circumstances, subject to applicable law.

These provisions often rely on defined terms such as *Original Issue Price* , which may affect liquidation preferences, dividends, and conversion calculations.

### Voting Rights and Protective Provisions

The certificate may also grant preferred stockholders voting rights beyond those held by common stockholders.

For example, *protective provisions* may require approval from holders of a specified percentage of preferred stock before the corporation issues senior securities, amends certain charter provisions, or undertakes other designated actions.

These approval requirements must be distinguished from statutory voting requirements.

A proposed transaction may require board approval, a general stockholder vote, a separate class or series vote, and approval under the preferred stock's protective provisions.

The charter is not the exclusive source of investor governance rights. Delaware law also permits certain contractual governance arrangements, including under DGCL Section 122(18), subject to applicable limitations. The relevant distinction is between rights attached to the securities and rights created by agreement.

**Why this matters:** Describing a liquidation preference or voting right in the SPA does not substitute for properly establishing the intended stock rights through the corporation's governing instruments.

## 4. The Investors' Rights Agreement: Information, Registration, and Participation Rights

The Investors' Rights Agreement establishes contractual rights that generally continue after the financing closes.

Its provisions commonly fall into three categories.

### Information and Inspection Rights

Investors may negotiate rights to receive periodic financial statements, annual budgets, and other company information.

The IRA may also provide contractual rights to inspect records or meet with management, subject to specified conditions and limitations.

These rights are frequently limited to investors satisfying a defined ownership threshold, often described by the term *Major Investor* .

Consequently, two investors holding the same series of preferred stock may have different contractual reporting rights.

Contractual information rights are also distinct from statutory inspection rights under DGCL Section 220.

### Registration Rights

Registration rights concern the potential resale of securities through registered offerings under federal securities laws.

Common provisions include:

- **Demand registration rights:** Allow qualifying investors to request that the company register eligible securities, subject to negotiated requirements.

- **Piggyback registration rights:** Permit eligible investors to include their securities in certain registrations initiated by the company or other holders.

- **Form S-3 registration rights:** Provide additional registration opportunities when the company becomes eligible to use that form and the agreement's conditions are satisfied.

The IRA also commonly addresses registration expenses, underwriting limitations, transferability, and termination of these rights.

### Participation Rights in Future Financings

The IRA may give qualifying investors the right to purchase a portion of securities issued in future financings.

These rights are commonly called *pro rata participation rights* or *rights of first offer* . Suppose an investor owns 10% of the company on the contractual basis used to calculate participation rights. The IRA might give that investor an opportunity to purchase a corresponding portion of a future qualifying issuance.

The right depends on the agreement's definition of covered securities, investor eligibility, exceptions, notice procedures, and exercise requirements.

Employee equity awards and certain other issuances may be excluded. Participation rights may also be limited to Major Investors.

**An important distinction:** Participation rights in the IRA generally concern new securities issued by the company. Rights of first refusal under the ROFR Agreement generally concern existing shares that a stockholder proposes to transfer.

## 5. The Voting Agreement: Board Composition and Stockholder Commitments

The Voting Agreement establishes contractual commitments concerning how specified stockholders will exercise their voting rights.

DGCL Section 218(c) recognizes written agreements among stockholders governing the voting of their shares.

Two common subjects are director elections and drag-along obligations.

### Board Composition

Investors frequently negotiate board representation as part of a preferred stock financing.

For example, the parties might agree to a five-member board consisting of two founder designees, two investor designees, and one independent director selected through an agreed process.

The Voting Agreement may require participating stockholders to vote their shares in favor of the designated nominees.

It may also address director removal, replacement, and the circumstances under which designation rights terminate.

These provisions should be coordinated with the certificate of incorporation, which may separately grant a class or series of stock the right to elect particular directors.

The Voting Agreement creates obligations among its parties. It does not, merely by naming a director designee, eliminate applicable corporate voting and governance requirements.

### Drag-Along Rights

Voting Agreements commonly contain *drag-along provisions* designed to facilitate certain approved sales of the company.

A drag-along provision may require participating stockholders to support a qualifying transaction once specified approval conditions are satisfied.

Depending on the agreement, those obligations may include voting in favor of the transaction, selling shares, executing transaction documents, or complying with other agreed requirements.

For example, the drag-along provision might become operative only after approval by the board, a specified percentage of preferred stockholders, and a designated group of common stockholders.

The required approvals and contractual obligations must be examined separately. A drag-along provision does not itself establish that every statutory approval for a transaction has been obtained.

The specific provisions also determine which stockholders are bound, what transactions qualify, and what limitations apply to their obligations.

## 6. The Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement: Regulating Stock Transfers

The Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement governs certain proposed transfers of existing shares by specified stockholders, often founders and other key holders.

Its principal provisions protect negotiated interests in secondary transfers.

### Right of First Refusal

A right of first refusal, or ROFR, generally gives designated parties an opportunity to purchase shares before a covered stockholder transfers them to a third party.

Under a common structure, the company receives an initial purchase right, followed by a secondary right for eligible investors.

Suppose a founder proposes to sell 100,000 common shares to a third party.

The agreement may require the founder to deliver a transfer notice identifying the proposed purchaser, price, number of shares, and other material terms. The company then has an opportunity to purchase the shares under the agreement. If it does not exercise its right in full, eligible investors may have an opportunity to purchase the remaining covered shares.

Any subsequent third-party transfer remains subject to the agreement's procedures and restrictions.

The applicable exercise periods, allocations, and permitted transfer conditions depend on the negotiated terms.

### Co-Sale Rights

Co-sale rights, also called *tag-along rights* , may allow eligible investors to sell their own shares alongside a covered stockholder.

Continuing the example, suppose the company and investors decline to purchase the founder's shares through the ROFR process.

Eligible investors may then have a right to participate in the proposed third-party sale by including a proportionate amount of their own shares, subject to the agreement.

This allows participating investors to share in certain secondary liquidity opportunities obtained by key holders.

### Permitted Transfers and Enforceability

The agreement typically includes exceptions for specified transactions, such as certain estate-planning or affiliate transfers. It also addresses notice, exercise periods, and termination.

DGCL Section 202 governs restrictions on stock transfers and includes requirements relevant to their enforceability. Particular care is required when imposing restrictions on previously issued shares or ensuring that restrictions are properly reflected in certificates or notices for uncertificated stock.

Separate transfer restrictions may also appear in founder stock purchase agreements, bylaws, or other documents.

## 7. How the Five Documents Work Together

Consider Atlas Labs, Inc., a Delaware corporation completing a $5 million Series A financing.

Assume the company will issue new Series A Preferred Stock, convert several outstanding SAFEs, grant its lead investor board representation, and provide certain investors with information and participation rights. The founders will also become subject to negotiated transfer restrictions. Each element implicates different documents.

| Transaction issue | Principal document or documents |
| --- | --- |
| Purchase of the new Series A shares | SPA |
| Authorization and rights of Series A Preferred Stock | Certificate of Incorporation |
| Conversion of outstanding SAFEs | SAFE instruments; Certificate of Incorporation; capitalization records |
| Investor financial reporting and future participation rights | IRA |
| Board designation and voting commitments | Voting Agreement; potentially Certificate of Incorporation |
| Founder share transfer restrictions | ROFR and Co-Sale Agreement |
| Drag-along obligations in a future sale | Voting Agreement |
| Financing approvals and closing requirements | Corporate consents; SPA; applicable existing agreements |

Three aspects of this transaction illustrate why the documents must be reviewed together.

### SAFE Conversions and Capitalization

Suppose one SAFE converts at a lower price than the Series A purchase price.

Depending on its terms, the SAFE may convert into a separate series of preferred stock with adjusted economic rights, sometimes called *shadow preferred stock* .

The company's charter must provide for the securities being issued, and the capitalization records must correctly reflect their number and terms.

The SPA's capitalization representations and disclosure schedules must also account for the outstanding SAFE and its conversion.

A share-count discrepancy can therefore affect several documents simultaneously.

### Investor Eligibility

Suppose a SAFE holder receives preferred stock at closing.

That holder does not necessarily receive every contractual right granted to new investors. The IRA might limit information and participation rights to Major Investors, while the Voting Agreement and ROFR Agreement use different eligibility definitions.

The relevant agreements must identify which investors receive each right and which holders must sign the agreements or applicable joinders.

Owning a particular series of preferred stock does not automatically confer every contractual right associated with the financing.

### Corporate Approvals and Closing

Atlas Labs may need board authorization for the stock issuance, stockholder approval of its amended charter, and separate preferred stockholder approvals under existing protective provisions.

Earlier investor agreements may also grant participation or consent rights requiring action before closing.

These requirements must be considered independently. A stockholder vote does not necessarily waive a contractual participation right, and an investor's contractual consent does not necessarily satisfy a statutory approval requirement.

The SPA coordinates these requirements through its closing conditions. The charter filing, execution of definitive agreements, delivery of consents, and receipt of funds must occur in the appropriate sequence.

## 8. Documents Beyond the Core Five

The five principal documents establish much of the financing's legal structure, but they do not represent the entire closing package.

**Corporate approvals.** Board and stockholder consents or resolutions may authorize the financing, charter amendment, stock issuances, and related actions. Existing investor approvals may also be required.

**Ancillary agreements.** Depending on the transaction, these may include management rights letters, director indemnification agreements, investor questionnaires, and negotiated side letters.

**Closing deliverables.** Common examples include officer or secretary certificates, executed signature pages, capitalization schedules, evidence of required filings, payment instructions, and legal opinions when negotiated.

**Existing agreements.** Earlier investor agreements may need to be amended, replaced, terminated, or waived. Outstanding participation rights and other contractual protections must be addressed where applicable. **Post-closing records and filings.** The company should update its stock ledger and capitalization table, document the issuances, and complete applicable securities notices and other regulatory filings.

The precise documentation depends on the transaction. The NVCA model collection includes several ancillary forms in addition to the five principal agreements.

## 9. Common Issues in Financing Document Review

Even when each agreement is individually well drafted, problems can arise from inconsistencies among them.

**Defined terms.** Terms such as *Major Investor* , *Registrable Securities* , and *Preferred Stock* may have different meanings across agreements. Counsel must determine whether those differences are intentional.

**Capitalization.** The SPA, charter, conversion calculations, and closing capitalization table must reconcile to the intended securities issuance.

**Existing rights.** Prior participation rights, protective provisions, voting agreements, and transfer restrictions may require waivers, amendments, or other actions.

**Approval requirements.** Board approval, statutory stockholder approval, charter-based protective provisions, and contractual consent rights must be evaluated separately.

**Parties and signatures.** The intended stockholders and investors must be properly bound by the relevant agreements. Existing holders and converting investors may require additional signatures or joinders.

**Closing sequence.** The SPA's closing conditions must align with the required corporate actions, charter filing, agreements, and funds flow.

A financing review therefore requires more than identifying the meaning of each provision. Counsel must establish where each right originates, who holds it, who is bound by it, and whether all the documents implement the negotiated transaction consistently.

## Conclusion

The five core venture financing documents allocate different parts of a priced equity financing among distinct corporate and contractual instruments.

The Stock Purchase Agreement governs the investment. The certificate of incorporation establishes preferred stock rights. The Investors' Rights Agreement provides ongoing contractual protections. The Voting Agreement governs specified voting commitments, and the ROFR and Co-Sale Agreement regulates certain share transfers. Their effectiveness depends on how well they operate together. Understanding each document's function is the starting point; understanding their interaction is what makes a financing review complete.
