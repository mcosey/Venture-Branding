Selling a startup for $20 million does not necessarily mean its stockholders receive $20 million in proportion to their ownership percentages.

A founder who owns 30% of a company might expect to receive 30% of its acquisition proceeds. However, if the company has raised venture capital, preferred stockholders may be entitled to receive certain amounts before founders and other common stockholders receive anything.

These rights are known as *liquidation preferences*. They are among the most important economic terms in a venture capital financing because they affect how proceeds from a sale or other covered transaction are distributed.

Understanding liquidation preferences helps founders evaluate financing proposals and anticipate how different acquisition outcomes could affect their ownership interests.

## 1. What Is a Liquidation Preference?

A liquidation preference gives specified stockholders priority over other stockholders when a company distributes proceeds from a liquidation or another event covered by its governing documents.

In venture-backed companies, investors commonly purchase preferred stock, while founders and employees typically hold common stock.

Preferred stock generally carries economic rights that common stock does not, including liquidation preferences.

Suppose an investor contributes $3 million to a startup in exchange for preferred stock carrying a *1x liquidation preference*.

If the company is subsequently sold in a transaction covered by that preference, the investor generally has the right to receive up to $3 million before common stockholders receive proceeds, subject to other securityholders' rights and available funds.

The preference does not guarantee repayment. If insufficient proceeds remain after satisfying creditors and other obligations, the investor may recover less than its investment.

### When Does a Liquidation Preference Apply?

Despite its name, a liquidation preference is not limited to situations where a company shuts down and liquidates its assets.

Venture financing documents commonly extend these rights to specified mergers, acquisitions, and sales of substantially all corporate assets. Such transactions are often addressed as *deemed liquidation events*.

The company's certificate of incorporation establishes which transactions trigger the preference and how the resulting proceeds are distributed.

Company debts and other obligations ordinarily must be addressed before proceeds can be distributed to equity holders. Consequently, a company's headline acquisition price may differ substantially from the amount ultimately available to stockholders.

## 2. What Does a 1x Nonparticipating Liquidation Preference Mean?

A *1x nonparticipating liquidation preference* generally entitles an investor to the greater of its original investment amount or the proceeds attributable to its shares on an as-converted basis.

In other words, the investor receives the economic benefit of its preference or the amount it would receive as a common stockholder, but not both. The governing documents determine how that comparison is implemented.

Consider a company with the following capitalization:

| **Term** | **Amount** |
| --- | --- |
| Investor's original investment | $3 million |
| Investor's ownership on an as-converted basis | 25% |
| Common stockholders' ownership | 75% |
| Liquidation preference | 1x nonparticipating |

For simplicity, assume there is one series of preferred stock, no accrued preferred dividends, and no other securities affecting the distribution. All acquisition amounts below represent proceeds available to stockholders after satisfying applicable debts, expenses, and other obligations.

### Scenario A: The Company Is Sold for $8 Million

The investor's 1x liquidation preference is $3 million.

Alternatively, its shares would represent 25% of the available proceeds on an as-converted basis, or $2 million.

Because the preference produces the greater return, the investor receives $3 million, leaving $5 million for common stockholders.

| **Recipient** | **Distribution** |
| --- | --- |
| Preferred investor | $3 million |
| Common stockholders | $5 million |
| **Total** | **$8 million** |

Although common stockholders own 75% of the company, they receive only 62.5% of the distributable proceeds.

### Scenario B: The Company Is Sold for $20 Million

Now suppose the same company is sold for $20 million.

The investor's 1x preference remains $3 million. However, its 25% as-converted interest would be worth $5 million.

The as-converted amount therefore produces the better return.

| **Recipient** | **Distribution** |
| --- | --- |
| Preferred investor | $5 million |
| Common stockholders | $15 million |
| **Total** | **$20 million** |

In this scenario, the proceeds are distributed according to the parties' ownership percentages.

A nonparticipating liquidation preference is therefore especially significant when the company's sale proceeds are insufficient to provide the investor with a greater return on an as-converted basis.

## 3. What Is the Difference Between 1x, 2x, and Participating Preferred Stock?

Not all liquidation preferences provide the same economic protections.

Two important distinctions are the *liquidation preference multiple* and whether the preferred stock is participating or nonparticipating.

### Liquidation Preference Multiples

The multiple determines the amount an investor is entitled to receive in preference to common stockholders.

A 1x preference generally corresponds to the investor's original investment. A 2x preference generally corresponds to twice that amount.

For an investor who contributes $3 million:

| **Preference** | **Aggregate preference** |
| --- | --- |
| 1x | $3 million |
| 1.5x | $4.5 million |
| 2x | $6 million |

Under a 2x nonparticipating preference, the investor generally receives the greater of its $6 million preference or its as-converted share of the proceeds.

If the company has $8 million available for distribution, the investor receives $6 million, leaving $2 million for common stockholders.

Under a 1x nonparticipating preference, the investor would receive $3 million, leaving $5 million for common stockholders.

The multiple can therefore significantly affect the amount founders receive in a sale.

### Participating Preferred Stock

Participating preferred stock provides an additional economic benefit.

Under a conventional *1x participating liquidation preference*, the investor first receives its liquidation preference and then participates alongside common stockholders in the remaining proceeds, generally on an as-converted basis.

Return to the investor who contributed $3 million for 25% ownership.

If the company has $8 million available for distribution, the investor first receives its $3 million preference.

That leaves $5 million. The investor then receives 25% of the remaining proceeds, or another $1.25 million.

The investor's total distribution is:

**$3 million + $1.25 million = $4.25 million.**

Common stockholders receive the remaining $3.75 million.

Under a 1x nonparticipating preference, the investor would have received only $3 million.

### Comparing the Different Structures

The following table illustrates how the same investment produces different outcomes depending on the preferred stock's rights.

Assume the investor contributed $3 million for 25% ownership, all other shares are common stock, and no additional securities affect the distribution.

| **Preferred stock terms** | **Investor receives on $8M sale** | **Common receives on $8M sale** | **Investor receives on $20M sale** | **Common receives on $20M sale** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1x nonparticipating | $3M | $5M | $5M | $15M |
| 2x nonparticipating | $6M | $2M | $6M | $14M |
| 1x participating, uncapped | $4.25M | $3.75M | $7.25M | $12.75M |

The differences are substantial even though the investor's original investment and ownership percentage remain unchanged.

A higher liquidation preference multiple provides greater protection when sale proceeds are limited. Participating preferred stock may allow the investor to receive additional proceeds after recovering its original investment.

### What Is Capped Participating Preferred Stock?

Some financing agreements limit the amount an investor can receive through participation.

For example, a provision might cap the investor's aggregate distributions under the preference and participation formula at three times its original investment.

The investor receives its preference and participates in additional proceeds until reaching the negotiated cap.

Depending on the conversion provisions, the investor may still receive more by converting into common stock.

The effect of a participation cap therefore depends on the governing documents.

## 4. What Happens When a Company Has Multiple Preferred Stock Series?

As startups raise successive rounds of venture capital, they may issue multiple series of preferred stock.

A company might have Series A Preferred Stock from its first priced financing and Series B Preferred Stock from a subsequent round.

Each series can carry its own liquidation preference. The company's certificate of incorporation also establishes how those preferences rank relative to one another.

The principal possibilities are senior, junior, and *pari passu* preferences.

### Senior and Junior Preferences

A series with a *senior liquidation preference* receives its applicable preference before a junior series receives its preference.

For example, Series B investors may negotiate a preference that ranks senior to the existing Series A Preferred Stock.

In that case, Series B's preference must generally be satisfied before Series A receives distributions under its preference.

### Pari Passu Preferences

*Pari passu* is a Latin phrase meaning "on equal footing."

When Series A and Series B liquidation preferences rank pari passu, neither series receives priority over the other.

If available proceeds are insufficient to satisfy both preferences in full, the proceeds allocated to that priority level are generally shared proportionately according to the series' respective preference entitlements.

### Example: Senior vs. Pari Passu

Suppose a company has the following preferred stock outstanding:

| **Series** | **Aggregate 1x preference** |
| --- | --- |
| Series A | $4 million |
| Series B | $2 million |
| **Total** | **$6 million** |

The company is sold, with only $3 million available for distribution to stockholders.

Assume neither series would benefit from conversion into common stock and both series have nonparticipating preferred stock.

Their distributions depend on the ranking established in the certificate of incorporation.

| **Distribution structure** | **Series A receives** | **Series B receives** | **Common receives** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Series B senior to Series A | $1M | $2M | $0 |
| Series A and B pari passu | $2M | $1M | $0 |
| Series A senior to Series B | $3M | $0 | $0 |

Under the pari passu structure, Series A holds two-thirds of the aggregate preference entitlement and receives two-thirds of the available proceeds. Series B receives the remaining one-third.

Under the senior structures, the senior series receives its preference first, with any remaining proceeds available to the junior series.

Common stockholders receive nothing in these examples because the available proceeds are insufficient to satisfy the preferred stock preferences.

This is why the relationship among preferred stock series matters, particularly when a company has raised several rounds of financing and later sells for less than anticipated.

## 5. Why Can Liquidation Preferences Change a Founder's Exit Proceeds?

A capitalization table shows ownership percentages, but those percentages do not necessarily determine how much each stockholder receives when the company is sold.

Several additional considerations can affect the distribution.

### Multiple Financing Rounds

Each new preferred stock financing may introduce additional liquidation preferences.

Over time, the aggregate amount payable to preferred stockholders before common stockholders receive distributions can become substantial.

Suppose a company has raised $25 million through preferred stock financings carrying aggregate 1x preferences of $25 million.

If an acquisition produces only $20 million for stockholder distributions, common stockholders may receive nothing, assuming the preferred holders retain their preferences rather than benefiting from conversion.

The company's valuations during prior financing rounds do not eliminate these rights.

### Accrued Dividends

Some preferred stock provisions incorporate accrued or declared but unpaid dividends into the liquidation amount.

If an investor's preferred stock carries cumulative dividends, unpaid amounts may increase the aggregate preference over time, depending on the certificate of incorporation.

Dividends can therefore affect the distribution even when the preference multiple remains unchanged.

### Amounts Available for Distribution

A $30 million acquisition does not necessarily produce $30 million for stockholders.

Outstanding debt, transaction expenses, and other obligations may reduce the amount available to equity holders. Purchase price adjustments, escrows, contingent consideration, and other transaction terms can affect when and how consideration is received.

A liquidation waterfall must account for the actual transaction terms rather than relying solely on the announced purchase price.

## 6. What Should Founders Understand Before Agreeing to a Liquidation Preference?

Liquidation preferences are commonly negotiated when investors purchase preferred stock in a priced financing.

The principal terms may first appear in a venture capital term sheet. They are then implemented through the company's certificate of incorporation and coordinated with the other financing documents.

Founders should understand the proposed preference multiple, whether the preferred stock participates in additional proceeds, and how the new series ranks against existing preferred stock.

They should also understand which transactions trigger the preference and whether dividends or other amounts increase the investor's entitlement.

The implications are best evaluated through a *liquidation waterfall*, a calculation showing how available proceeds would be distributed among the company's securityholders under different acquisition values.

For example, counsel might model distributions assuming the company is sold for $10 million, $25 million, or $50 million. The calculations would reflect the capitalization table, conversion rights, liquidation preferences, and other applicable terms.

This analysis can reveal circumstances in which an investor receives a substantial portion of the proceeds despite owning a relatively small percentage of the company.

It can also help founders evaluate the economic consequences of a proposed financing beyond the ownership percentages shown on the capitalization table.

## Conclusion

Liquidation preferences determine how certain proceeds are distributed among preferred and common stockholders when a company is sold or experiences another covered event.

Their effect depends on the preference multiple, participation rights, ranking among preferred stock series, and the amount available for distribution.

A founder's ownership percentage is therefore only part of the picture when evaluating the potential proceeds from a future sale.

Understanding liquidation preferences helps founders assess the economic consequences of raising venture capital.
