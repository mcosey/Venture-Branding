When applying for federal trademark registration, you may be required to submit a *trademark specimen*.

A specimen shows how your trademark is actually used in connection with your goods or services. It allows the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) to examine how customers encounter your mark in the marketplace.

For example, a clothing company might submit a photograph of its trademark on a product label. A software company might submit a screenshot showing its trademark on a webpage where customers can download its application.

The appropriate evidence depends on what the business offers and how the mark is used.

## What Is the Difference Between a Trademark Drawing and a Specimen?

Every federal trademark application must include a representation of the mark the applicant seeks to register. This is called the *drawing*.

A drawing might consist of a business name in standard characters or a particular logo design.

A specimen serves a different purpose. It shows the mark in actual commercial use.

Suppose a startup develops a logo for its productivity software. The company might submit that logo as the drawing in its trademark application.

However, uploading the logo file itself generally would not establish trademark use. The USPTO ordinarily requires evidence showing how the mark appears in connection with the goods or services identified in the application.

The specimen must also correspond to the applied-for mark. Material differences between the mark in the drawing and the mark shown on the specimen can create registration issues.

For more information about the distinction between word marks and logos, see *[Should I Trademark My Business Name or Logo First?](should-i-trademark-my-business-name-or-logo-first.html)*

## What Qualifies as a Specimen for Goods?

For goods, acceptable specimens commonly show the trademark on the product itself, its packaging, labels, tags, or qualifying displays associated with the goods.

For example, a clothing company might submit a photograph of a label sewn into a shirt. A beverage company might submit a photograph of its branded packaging.

A webpage may also qualify if it directly associates the mark with the goods and provides an appropriate means of ordering or purchasing them.

Merely advertising goods generally is not sufficient. The evidence must demonstrate the type of trademark use required for goods actually sold or transported in commerce.

### What About Downloadable Software?

Downloadable software presents additional considerations because the product is digital.

An acceptable specimen might be a software launch screen displaying the trademark or a webpage identifying the software and providing a means to download it.

The USPTO evaluates whether the evidence demonstrates use of the mark in connection with the downloadable product rather than simply advertising software that is not yet available.

## What Qualifies as a Specimen for Services?

Services are treated differently because they generally are not physical products that carry labels or packaging.

A service mark specimen may consist of advertising, a website, promotional materials, signage, or other evidence showing the mark used in connection with services actually being rendered.

The specimen must demonstrate a *direct association* between the mark and the identified services.

For example, a consulting firm's website might display its trademark alongside a description of the consulting services it provides.

A trademark appearing on an unrelated webpage, without an adequate connection to the services identified in the application, may not satisfy the requirement.

## What Would a Software Specimen Look Like?

Consider TempoGarden, a hypothetical company offering productivity software.

TempoGarden provides a browser-based subscription platform and a downloadable mobile application.

Although both offerings use the same brand, they may require different specimen evidence.

For the downloadable application, the evidence would demonstrate use of the mark with software as a product.

For the SaaS platform, it would demonstrate use in connection with the online service.

A screenshot taken from within the software may also qualify in appropriate circumstances, but displaying the company name somewhere in an application does not automatically establish acceptable trademark use.

An attorney must consider how consumers encounter the mark and whether it functions as an identifier of the particular goods or services claimed.

## Why Does the USPTO Reject Trademark Specimens?

Specimen refusals arise for several reasons. Some concern the evidence itself, while others raise questions about whether the mark is functioning as a trademark.

Common issues include:

**Mockups or digitally altered images.** The specimen depicts proposed packaging, a simulated website, or another representation that does not reflect actual marketplace use.

**A different mark.** The specimen displays a mark that differs materially from the one identified in the application.

**Incorrect goods or services.** The evidence does not demonstrate use in connection with the offerings identified in the application.

**Insufficient trademark use.** The mark appears merely as decoration or otherwise fails to identify commercial source.

**Inappropriate evidence.** The applicant submits advertising for goods without showing the type of use required for a goods-related specimen.

These issues are particularly relevant for businesses preparing to launch.

For example, a founder might commission a realistic rendering of branded product packaging before the product is manufactured.

Although the rendering may be useful for product development, it generally cannot serve as evidence of actual trademark use.

The distinction is between a genuine photograph or screenshot documenting marketplace use and an image created or altered to depict how a mark might eventually appear.

### Do Website Specimens Need a URL and Date?

Yes. The USPTO requires webpage specimens to include the page's URL and the date it was accessed or printed.

That information can appear on the specimen itself or be supplied in the appropriate fields or verified response materials, as applicable.

Because websites can change, these requirements help document the source and timing of the evidence.

## When Must a Trademark Specimen Be Submitted?

The timing depends on the application's filing basis.

### Use-Based Applications

For applications filed under Section 1(a) of the Lanham Act, the applicant generally submits a specimen with the initial application.

The specimen must reflect qualifying use that existed by the application filing date.

### Intent-to-Use Applications

Applicants filing under Section 1(b) generally submit specimens later, after establishing qualifying use.

Depending on the application's stage, this occurs through an Amendment to Allege Use or a Statement of Use.

The USPTO generally requires at least one acceptable specimen per class, although a single specimen may sometimes support multiple classes.

Submitting one specimen per class does not eliminate the requirement that the applicant's use allegations be supportable for the goods or services claimed in that class.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Evaluate a Specimen?

Reviewing a specimen involves more than determining whether the trademark is visible.

A trademark attorney examines the relationship between the mark, the submitted evidence, and the goods or services identified in the application.

The attorney may consider whether customers would perceive the mark as identifying commercial source, whether the specimen demonstrates the required association with the identified offering, and whether the use satisfies the applicable timing requirements.

For software companies, this review may involve examining how the product is distributed, how users access the service, and where the trademark appears during those activities.

Timing is especially important. A specimen submitted with a Section 1(a) application must reflect use existing by the application filing date. For an Amendment to Allege Use or Statement of Use, different statutory timing requirements apply.

Evidence created after a relevant deadline cannot simply be used to establish use that did not exist when required.

## What Happens If a Specimen Is Rejected?

If the USPTO determines that a specimen is unacceptable, the examining attorney may issue an office action explaining the refusal.

A rejected specimen does not necessarily mean the applicant has never made qualifying trademark use.

For example, a business may be using its mark appropriately but have submitted evidence that fails to demonstrate that use.

Depending on the circumstances, an attorney may evaluate whether the original specimen can be defended, whether an acceptable substitute specimen exists, or whether a change in filing basis is legally available.

A substitute specimen generally must have been in actual use by the applicable required date and must be accompanied by the necessary verification.

The relevant date depends on whether the specimen supports an initial use-based application, an Amendment to Allege Use, or a Statement of Use.

If qualifying use did not exist when required, creating new promotional materials after receiving a refusal generally will not cure the earlier deficiency.

Some specimen refusals can be resolved, while others reveal more fundamental problems with the application's claimed use.

## The Bottom Line

A trademark specimen demonstrates how a mark is actually used with the goods or services identified in a federal trademark application.

Acceptable evidence varies by offering. A product label may support an application for physical goods, while a webpage may demonstrate use for an online service.

For software businesses, the distinction between downloadable applications and SaaS can be particularly important.

Because the USPTO evaluates the evidence's authenticity, the mark's function, its relationship to the identified goods or services, and the timing of use, specimen review is an important part of trademark prosecution.
