Understanding a venture financing requires more than knowing the company's valuation and the amount investors intend to contribute. Transactional attorneys must determine how those figures translate into share issuances, conversion prices, and ownership percentages.

The calculations become more complicated when a financing involves outstanding SAFEs, convertible notes, or an increase to the company's equity incentive pool. Each can affect fully diluted capitalization and the resulting allocation of ownership.

This article examines the mathematics underlying a priced venture financing, beginning with basic valuation calculations and progressing through a hypothetical Series A transaction involving a post-money SAFE, a convertible note, and an option pool increase.

## 1. Pre-Money Valuation, Post-Money Valuation, and Price Per Share

The starting point for a priced equity financing is the company's agreed pre-money valuation.

*Pre-money valuation* represents the negotiated value attributed to the company's equity immediately before the new financing, using the capitalization conventions agreed upon by the parties.

*Post-money valuation* generally equals the pre-money valuation plus the new equity investment.

**Post-Money Valuation = Pre-Money Valuation + New Investment**

Suppose a company raises $5 million at a $15 million pre-money valuation.

Its post-money valuation is:

$15,000,000 + $5,000,000 = $20,000,000

Assuming the agreed capitalization includes all relevant pre-financing equity interests, the new investors acquire:

$5,000,000 ÷ $20,000,000 = 25%

The remaining 75% represents the ownership attributed to the company's pre-financing equity holders and reserves under the agreed fully diluted capitalization.

### Calculating Price Per Share

The financing price per share is generally calculated by dividing the pre-money valuation by the applicable fully diluted pre-money capitalization.

**Price Per Share = Pre-Money Valuation ÷ Fully Diluted Pre-Money Shares**

If the company has 10 million fully diluted shares:

$15,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = $1.50 per share

The $5 million investment purchases approximately 3,333,333 shares.

The new investors consequently acquire approximately 25% of the post-closing fully diluted capitalization.

These calculations depend on which shares and equity rights the transaction includes in the pricing denominator.

## 2. Understanding Fully Diluted Capitalization

Fully diluted capitalization generally includes more than shares that have been issued and are outstanding.

Depending on the transaction documents, the calculation may include:

- Issued and outstanding common stock.

- Preferred stock on an as-converted basis.

- Shares issuable upon exercise of outstanding options and warrants.

- Shares reserved for future issuance under an equity incentive plan.

- Shares issuable upon conversion of SAFEs, convertible notes, and other securities.

- Increases to the equity incentive pool negotiated in connection with the financing.

These categories are not treated identically in every transaction.

The parties must establish which securities and reserves are included when determining the Series A purchase price. Outstanding convertible instruments may also contain their own capitalization definitions for calculating conversion prices.

For example, a post-money SAFE may exclude a newly negotiated option pool increase from its conversion calculation even though that increase is included in the Series A pricing capitalization.

Accordingly, attorneys must distinguish the capitalization used to price the new financing from the capitalization used to convert existing securities.

## 3. How Option Pool Increases Affect the Financing

Before completing a priced financing, investors frequently negotiate an increase to the company's equity incentive pool.

The pool represents shares reserved for equity compensation. Investors may require a sufficient reserve to support hiring and retention after closing.

Whether the increase is included in the pre-money capitalization affects which shareholders bear the resulting dilution.

### The Pre-Money Option Pool

Suppose a company has 8 million fully diluted shares, including its existing equity incentive pool.

Investors agree to contribute $4 million at a $12 million pre-money valuation.

Without a pool increase:

$12,000,000 ÷ 8,000,000 = $1.50 per share

The investors receive approximately:

$4,000,000 ÷ $1.50 = 2,666,667 shares

Now suppose the investors require a 2 million-share pool increase, included in the pre-money capitalization.

The pricing denominator becomes 10 million shares:

$12,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = $1.20 per share

The same $4 million investment purchases:

$4,000,000 ÷ $1.20 = 3,333,333 shares

The new investors still acquire 25% of the post-closing fully diluted capitalization. However, the company's existing shareholders retain a smaller percentage because the additional pool shares are included before the investors' ownership is calculated.

This is sometimes called the *option pool shuffle*.

The economic burden of a pre-money pool expansion generally falls on existing equity holders, including holders of convertible securities to the extent provided by their conversion terms.

If the increase is instead structured to dilute both existing holders and incoming investors, the allocation changes.

Attorneys should therefore examine the negotiated pool size, the capitalization used to determine the purchase price, and the specific treatment of outstanding convertible securities.

## 4. How Convertible Notes Convert Into Equity

A convertible note is a debt instrument that may convert into equity upon a qualifying financing.

Unlike a conventional SAFE, a convertible note generally accrues interest and has a maturity date.

The amount converting into equity may include both principal and accrued interest, depending on the note's terms.

Convertible notes frequently provide a valuation cap, a discount, or both.

### Valuation Cap

A valuation cap establishes a contractual valuation benchmark used to calculate the note's conversion price.

Suppose a note has an $8 million valuation cap, and the capitalization specified for the cap calculation is 8 million shares.

The cap price is:

$8,000,000 ÷ 8,000,000 = $1.00 per share

If the new investors purchase shares at $1.50 per share, the noteholder may be entitled to convert at the lower $1.00 price.

The actual result depends on the note's capitalization definition and conversion provisions.

### Discount Rate

A discount allows the noteholder to convert at a specified percentage of the financing price.

For a 20% discount:

**Discount Price = Financing Price × 80%**

If the financing price is $1.50:

$1.50 × 80% = $1.20 per share

Assume the note has $500,000 in principal and $100,000 in accrued interest.

If the entire $600,000 converts:

$600,000 ÷ $1.20 = 500,000 shares

### What If the Note Has Both a Cap and a Discount?

Many notes provide for conversion at the lower of the applicable cap price and discount price.

If the cap price is $1.00 and the discount price is $1.20, the note generally converts at $1.00 under that structure.

The $600,000 balance would then convert into:

$600,000 ÷ $1.00 = 600,000 shares

The noteholder receives 100,000 additional shares compared with conversion at the discounted price.

Attorneys must examine the instrument's actual provisions, including its valuation cap, discount, interest treatment, conversion triggers, and capitalization definition.

In some financings, the note's conversion price depends on the Series A price, while the Series A price itself depends on the shares issued upon note conversion. Those calculations must be reconciled.

## 5. How SAFEs Convert Into Equity

A Simple Agreement for Future Equity, or SAFE, generally provides an investor with a contractual right to receive equity upon a future triggering event.

Unlike convertible debt, a conventional SAFE does not accrue interest or have a maturity date.

SAFE conversion mechanics depend on the instrument's form.

### Pre-Money SAFEs

Under the original pre-money SAFE structure, conversion prices were calculated using capitalization definitions that generally excluded shares issued upon conversion of the SAFEs themselves.

Consequently, the ownership ultimately acquired by a SAFE investor could depend on other convertible securities issued before the priced financing.

The investor's eventual ownership percentage was not necessarily fixed when the SAFE was signed.

### Post-Money SAFEs

Y Combinator introduced the post-money SAFE in 2018 to provide greater certainty about the ownership sold through SAFE financings.

For a post-money valuation-cap SAFE that converts at its cap, the investor's ownership on the capitalization basis specified by the instrument can generally be expressed as:

**SAFE Ownership = SAFE Investment ÷ Post-Money Valuation Cap**

Suppose an investor purchases a $1 million post-money SAFE with a $10 million valuation cap.

$1,000,000 ÷ $10,000,000 = 10%

Assuming the valuation cap governs conversion, the investor generally receives 10% of the applicable capitalization immediately before the new priced-round investment and associated option pool increase.

That does not mean the investor will retain 10% after the Series A. The new investment and the pool increase negotiated for that financing may dilute the SAFE investor.

### Calculating SAFE Conversion Shares

The number of conversion shares depends on the SAFE's defined *Company Capitalization*.

Under the standard Y Combinator post-money valuation-cap structure, that definition generally includes outstanding equity, the existing equity incentive pool, and other converting securities, while excluding the new Series A investment and the option pool increase adopted in connection with that financing.

The actual conversion formula must be taken from the applicable SAFE.

The investor's implied ownership percentage cannot simply be applied to the company's currently outstanding common shares. The calculation must account for the additional securities included in the contractual denominator.

## 6. Worked Example: A Series A Financing

Consider Atlas Labs, Inc., a Delaware corporation preparing to complete a Series A preferred stock financing.

The transaction includes founder common stock, an existing equity incentive pool, a post-money SAFE, a convertible note, a pool increase, and a new Series A investment.

### Transaction Assumptions

| **Item** | **Amount** |
| --- | --- |
| Founder common stock | 6,500,000 shares |
| Existing equity incentive pool, including outstanding awards and unissued reserves | 2,000,000 shares |
| Existing fully diluted capitalization | 8,500,000 shares |
| Post-money SAFE investment | $1,000,000 |
| SAFE post-money valuation cap | $10,000,000 |
| Convertible note principal | $500,000 |
| Accrued note interest | $100,000 |
| Convertible note discount | 20% |
| Additional pre-money option pool shares | 1,000,000 |
| Series A pre-money valuation | $16,500,000 |
| New Series A investment | $4,500,000 |

For this example, assume:

- The SAFE converts at its valuation cap.

- The note converts at its 20% discount rather than a valuation cap.

- The entire $600,000 note balance converts.

- The SAFE capitalization includes the converting note shares but excludes the new option pool increase.

- The Series A pricing capitalization includes the SAFE and note conversion shares and the new option pool increase.

These assumptions are illustrative contractual terms, not universal financing conventions.

Fractional shares and transaction-specific rounding are disregarded.

### Step 1: Calculate the Convertible Note Shares

The note has $600,000 outstanding, including principal and interest.

Its conversion price equals 80% of the Series A price.

Assuming a Series A price of $1.50:

$1.50 × 80% = $1.20

The noteholder receives:

$600,000 ÷ $1.20 = 500,000 shares

We will verify the assumed Series A price through the combined capitalization calculations.

### Step 2: Calculate the SAFE Conversion Shares

The SAFE investor contributed $1 million under a $10 million post-money valuation cap.

Its ownership percentage under the assumed cap conversion is:

$1,000,000 ÷ $10,000,000 = 10%

The SAFE capitalization includes the existing 8.5 million fully diluted shares, the 500,000 note conversion shares, and the SAFE's own conversion shares.

Let S represent the SAFE conversion shares.

S ÷ (8,500,000 + 500,000 + S) = 10%

Solving:

S = 1,000,000 shares

The SAFE conversion price is:

$1,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = $1.00 per share

This is lower than the assumed Series A price of $1.50, consistent with the valuation cap governing conversion.

### Step 3: Calculate the Pre-Money Capitalization

The negotiated Series A pricing capitalization includes:

| **Component** | **Shares** |
| --- | --- |
| Founder common stock | 6,500,000 |
| Existing equity incentive pool | 2,000,000 |
| New option pool increase | 1,000,000 |
| SAFE conversion shares | 1,000,000 |
| Convertible note conversion shares | 500,000 |
| **Total pre-money fully diluted capitalization** | **11,000,000** |

With a $16.5 million pre-money valuation:

$16,500,000 ÷ 11,000,000 = $1.50 per share

This matches the assumed financing price used to calculate the note conversion.

### Step 4: Resolve the Circular Calculations

Although the preceding steps use an assumed Series A price for readability, the SAFE, note, and financing price must be calculated consistently.

Let:

- P = Series A price per share.

- N = note conversion shares.

- S = SAFE conversion shares.

The note conversion formula is:

N = $600,000 ÷ (0.80 × P)

The SAFE ownership formula is:

S ÷ (8,500,000 + N + S) = 10%

The Series A price formula is:

P = $16,500,000 ÷ (9,500,000 + N + S)

The 9.5 million fixed shares in the last equation consist of the existing 8.5 million fully diluted shares plus the 1 million-share pool increase.

Solving the three equations simultaneously produces:

| **Variable** | **Result** |
| --- | --- |
| Series A price per share | $1.50 |
| Note conversion shares | 500,000 |
| SAFE conversion shares | 1,000,000 |

The calculations reconcile.

The distinction between the two capitalization denominators is important. The SAFE calculation excludes the Series A pool increase, while the Series A pricing calculation includes it.

In an actual financing, counsel must confirm that the mathematical model reflects the relevant contractual definitions.

### Step 5: Calculate the New Series A Shares

The new investors contribute $4.5 million at $1.50 per share.

$4,500,000 ÷ $1.50 = 3,000,000 shares

The post-closing fully diluted capitalization becomes:

11,000,000 + 3,000,000 = 14,000,000 shares

### Step 6: Calculate the Post-Money Valuation

The post-money valuation is:

$16,500,000 + $4,500,000 = $21,000,000

The new Series A investors' ownership is:

3,000,000 ÷ 14,000,000 = 21.43%

This agrees with:

$4,500,000 ÷ $21,000,000 = 21.43%

### Step 7: Reconcile the Post-Closing Cap Table

| **Shareholder or Reserve Category** | **Fully Diluted Shares** | **Ownership** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founders | 6,500,000 | 46.43% |
| Existing equity incentive pool | 2,000,000 | 14.29% |
| Additional option pool | 1,000,000 | 7.14% |
| SAFE investor | 1,000,000 | 7.14% |
| Convertible noteholder | 500,000 | 3.57% |
| New Series A investors | 3,000,000 | 21.43% |
| **Total** | **14,000,000** | **100.00%** |

The founders' ownership declines from approximately 76.47% of the original fully diluted capitalization to 46.43% after closing.

The dilution reflects the SAFE conversion, note conversion, option pool increase, and new Series A issuance.

The SAFE investor's ownership also declines from 10% of the capitalization specified by the SAFE to 7.14% of the post-closing fully diluted capitalization.

The equity incentive pool rows represent a fully diluted convention. Shares reserved for future awards are not necessarily outstanding or owned by existing shareholders.

## 7. Why the Headline Valuation Can Be Misleading

The announced valuation of a financing does not, by itself, establish how much ownership existing shareholders will retain.

In the Atlas Labs example, the $16.5 million pre-money valuation and $4.5 million investment imply that new investors acquire 21.43% of the post-closing fully diluted capitalization.

However, the founders' dilution is not limited to the new investors' ownership percentage.

Their interests are also affected by the outstanding convertible securities and the negotiated option pool increase.

The capitalization used to determine the financing price allocates the company's negotiated pre-money value across existing holders, converting investors, and equity reserves.

Different capitalization definitions can therefore produce different prices per share and ownership percentages even when the headline valuation and investment amount remain unchanged.

## 8. How Transactional Attorneys Review Financing Calculations

Attorneys reviewing venture financing calculations typically examine the company's capitalization records, outstanding securities, and proposed financing documents together.

Several issues require particular attention.

**Capitalization definitions.** Does the financing price include outstanding options, unissued equity reserves, convertible securities, and the new pool increase? Do the SAFE and note agreements use different definitions?

**Conversion mechanics.** Which valuation caps and discounts govern? Does accrued interest convert? Are the correct capitalization denominators used?

**Equity incentive pool.** Is the increase included before or after the financing for pricing purposes? Does the company have sufficient authorized shares and the required corporate approvals?

**Share classes.** Are converting investors receiving the same preferred shares as the new investors or a separate series with different economic terms?

**Rounding and capitalization records.** Do the calculated share issuances reconcile with the governing agreements, actual outstanding securities, and corporate records?

For example, a post-money SAFE may convert into a separate series of preferred stock commonly called *shadow preferred stock*. Its original issue price, liquidation preference per share, and conversion price may differ from the securities purchased by new Series A investors.

Accordingly, the capitalization table must reconcile both the number of shares issued and the specific securities being issued.

A model that produces ownership percentages totaling 100% is necessary, but it does not establish that the financing has been documented correctly.

## Conclusion

Venture financing mathematics translates negotiated economic terms into actual ownership.

Pre-money valuations determine share prices only after the applicable capitalization has been established. Option pool increases can shift dilution toward existing holders, while convertible securities may convert at prices below those paid by new investors.

These calculations can also interact. The financing price may affect convertible note conversions, and the resulting shares may affect the capitalization used to determine that price.

For transactional attorneys, the objective is to ensure that the capitalization model implements the governing agreements and accurately reflects the intended share issuances.

The final capitalization table should reconcile investment amounts, conversion mechanics, financing price, and post-closing ownership before the transaction closes.
