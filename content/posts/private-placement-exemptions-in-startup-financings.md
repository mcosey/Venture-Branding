When a startup raises capital by issuing stock, convertible notes, or other securities, the transaction is subject to federal securities laws. The fact that the company is privately held, or that its investors are sophisticated venture capital funds, does not automatically exempt the offering from registration.

Most startup financings proceed without registering securities with the Securities and Exchange Commission (SEC). Instead, issuers rely on exemptions under the Securities Act of 1933, particularly Section 4(a)(2) and Regulation D.

For venture financing attorneys, the chosen exemption affects which investors may participate, how the company can solicit capital, what information must be provided, and which federal and state compliance requirements apply.

## 1. The Securities Act's Registration Framework

Section 5 of the Securities Act generally requires securities offerings to be registered unless an exemption applies.

Registration ordinarily involves extensive disclosures concerning the issuer's business, finances, management, and securities. For most early-stage startups, the expense and regulatory burden would make conventional venture fundraising impractical.

The Securities Act therefore provides exemptions for qualifying securities and transactions.

A *transaction exemption* applies to particular offers or sales. Section 4(a)(2), for example, exempts certain transactions by issuers that do not involve a public offering.

A *securities exemption* applies to securities meeting specified statutory requirements, such as certain governmental securities under Section 3.

The distinction matters because an exemption covering a startup's original issuance does not automatically exempt subsequent resales.

For example, a corporation may issue preferred stock to a venture capital fund in an exempt private placement. If the fund later sells those shares, the secondary transaction requires a separate registration analysis or applicable exemption.

Exempt offerings also remain subject to federal securities antifraud provisions. An issuer cannot avoid liability for materially misleading statements merely because its offering was not registered.

## 2. Section 4(a)(2): The Statutory Private Placement Exemption

Section 4(a)(2) exempts transactions by issuers not involving a public offering.

Although the statutory language is brief, determining whether an offering is private requires a fact-specific analysis.

The Supreme Court addressed this question in *SEC v. Ralston Purina Co.*, 346 U.S. 119 (1953).

Ralston Purina offered securities to employees it characterized as key personnel without registering the offerings. The Court rejected the proposition that their employment status alone established a private placement.

Instead, the Court examined whether the offerees needed the protections of registration, emphasizing their access to information necessary to make an informed investment decision.

The decision established an important principle: the availability of the private placement exemption depends substantially on the circumstances and informational position of the offerees, rather than simply their number or relationship to the issuer.

### What Makes an Offering Private?

A Section 4(a)(2) analysis may consider:

- The number and characteristics of persons receiving offers.

- Their financial sophistication and ability to evaluate the investment.

- Their access to information concerning the issuer.

- Their relationships with the issuer.

- The manner in which securities are offered.

- Whether the offering involves public solicitation.

Section 4(a)(2) establishes no fixed dollar limit or numerical cap on purchasers. However, public advertising and general solicitation are generally incompatible with the traditional statutory exemption.

The issuer bears the burden of establishing that its offering qualifies.

Because these standards are principles-based, issuers frequently rely on the more defined requirements of Regulation D.

## 3. Regulation D: The Principal Exemptions

Regulation D provides regulatory exemptions and safe harbors for unregistered securities offerings.

Rules 506(b) and 506(c) are particularly important in venture financings. Both operate within the Section 4(a)(2) framework, but they impose different conditions concerning solicitation and investor qualification.

Rule 504 provides an additional exemption for certain smaller offerings.

### Rule 506(b): Traditional Private Placements

Rule 506(b) is a nonexclusive safe harbor under Section 4(a)(2).

An issuer satisfying its requirements can rely on the statutory private placement exemption without independently establishing compliance under the broader judicial analysis of Section 4(a)(2).

Rule 506(b) permits an unlimited offering amount and sales to an unlimited number of accredited investors. It also permits up to 35 qualifying non-accredited purchasers within any applicable 90-calendar-day period.

Non-accredited purchasers must possess, individually or through a purchaser representative, sufficient financial and business knowledge to evaluate the investment's merits and risks.

The rule prohibits general solicitation and general advertising.

For example, a startup generally cannot advertise an opportunity to purchase its Series A preferred stock on an unrestricted website while relying on Rule 506(b).

Private communications with investors with whom the issuer has established appropriate preexisting substantive relationships may be consistent with the exemption.

A substantive relationship involves sufficient knowledge of a prospective investor's financial circumstances and sophistication to evaluate their suitability. Merely obtaining a name or having someone complete an investor questionnaire does not necessarily establish such a relationship.

A preexisting substantive relationship is an important recognized basis for demonstrating the absence of general solicitation, but it is not a universal prerequisite for every private communication.

#### Disclosure Requirements

When all purchasers are accredited investors, Rule 506(b) generally affords issuers flexibility concerning the information provided, subject to applicable antifraud obligations.

When non-accredited investors participate, Rule 502(b) imposes specified disclosure requirements, including applicable financial information.

Participating non-accredited investors must also receive information provided to accredited investors, as required by the rule.

Consequently, venture financings frequently limit participation to accredited investors even though Rule 506(b) permits certain non-accredited purchasers.

Rule 506(b) offerings are also subject to the bad actor disqualification provisions of Rule 506(d).

### Rule 506(c): Offerings With General Solicitation

Rule 506(c) permits general solicitation and advertising if the issuer satisfies specified requirements.

An issuer may raise an unlimited amount, provided that:

1. Every purchaser is an accredited investor.

1. The issuer takes reasonable steps to verify each purchaser's accredited investor status.

1. The offering satisfies the other applicable conditions of Regulation D.

Unlike Rule 506(b), Rule 506(c) does not permit sales to non-accredited investors.

The verification requirement is particularly important. An investor's unsupported representation that they are accredited ordinarily does not establish that the issuer has taken reasonable verification steps.

Rule 506(c) provides nonexclusive verification methods, including reviewing specified financial records or obtaining appropriate written confirmations from attorneys, accountants, registered broker-dealers, or SEC-registered investment advisers.

However, the rule does not universally require issuers to obtain tax returns or similar documents.

Verification is governed by a principles-based standard that considers the investor's characteristics, information available to the issuer, and the circumstances of the offering.

In March 2025, SEC staff issued a no-action response addressing certain offerings involving substantial minimum investments. Under the circumstances described in the request, minimum investments of at least $200,000 for individuals or $1 million for entities, together with specified written representations and the absence of contrary information, could support a conclusion that reasonable verification steps had been taken.

That position is fact-specific staff guidance, not a separate regulatory safe harbor.

Rule 506(c) offerings are also subject to bad actor disqualification provisions.

### Rule 504: Smaller Exempt Offerings

Rule 504 permits eligible issuers to offer and sell up to $10 million of securities during a 12-month period.

Unlike Rule 506(c), it does not categorically require purchasers to be accredited investors.

However, the exemption is unavailable to certain issuers, including Exchange Act reporting companies, investment companies, and companies without a specific business plan or whose stated plan involves acquiring an unidentified company.

General solicitation is permitted only under specified circumstances involving state registration or qualifying exemptions.

Rule 504 offerings are subject to bad actor disqualification provisions and generally remain subject to applicable state securities registration or exemption requirements.

For startups raising capital from investors in several states, Rule 506 may therefore provide a more convenient framework.

## 4. Comparing the Available Exemptions

The principal distinctions can be summarized as follows:

| **Feature** | **Section 4(a)(2)** | **Rule 506(b)** | **Rule 506(c)** | **Rule 504** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Legal basis | Statutory exemption | § 4(a)(2) safe harbor | Rule 506 exemption treated under § 4(a)(2) | Regulatory exemption |
| Offering amount | No fixed limit | Unlimited | Unlimited | $10 million in 12 months |
| General solicitation | Generally incompatible | Prohibited | Permitted | Limited circumstances |
| Accredited purchasers only | No categorical requirement | No | Yes | No |
| Non-accredited purchasers | Fact-specific | Up to 35 qualifying purchasers | None | Permitted |
| Form D | Not required solely under § 4(a)(2) | Required | Required | Required |
| State registration preemption | Not generally | Yes | Yes | Not generally |

These distinctions provide a starting point. Each exemption has additional conditions that must be satisfied.

## 5. Accredited Investors and Investor Qualification

Rule 501(a) defines accredited investors through several categories based on financial resources, professional qualifications, or institutional status.

Common qualifications for individuals include:

- A net worth exceeding $1 million, individually or jointly with a spouse or spousal equivalent, excluding the primary residence under applicable rules.

- Individual annual income exceeding $200,000, or joint income exceeding $300,000, during each of the two most recent years, together with a reasonable expectation of reaching the applicable threshold in the current year.

- Certain qualifying professional certifications or credentials, including specified securities licenses.

Entities may qualify under separate standards, including categories for regulated financial institutions, investment funds, entities satisfying applicable asset thresholds, and entities whose equity owners are all accredited investors.

Investor qualification affects both exemption selection and the procedures used to document an offering.

### Reasonable Belief vs. Verification

Under Rule 506(b), an investor may be treated as accredited if the issuer reasonably believes that the investor satisfies the applicable definition.

That determination depends on the circumstances, including the issuer's relationship with the investor and information available about the investor.

Rule 506(c) imposes an additional procedural requirement: the issuer must take reasonable steps to verify accredited status, and all purchasers must actually be accredited.

An investor questionnaire may help support the reasonable-belief determination under Rule 506(b), particularly when accompanied by other relevant information.

The same questionnaire, without more, ordinarily will not satisfy Rule 506(c)'s verification requirement.

Attorneys therefore evaluate investor qualification in light of the specific exemption rather than treating accreditation as a uniform administrative exercise.

## 6. State Securities Laws and Federal Preemption

Federal securities exemptions do not necessarily eliminate state securities-law obligations, commonly called *blue sky laws*.

The National Securities Markets Improvement Act of 1996 (NSMIA) substantially altered state securities regulation by establishing federal preemption for certain covered securities.

Under Section 18 of the Securities Act, securities sold in qualifying Rule 506 offerings generally qualify as covered securities.

States therefore cannot require registration or qualification of those offerings.

However, states may still require notice filings and associated fees and retain authority to investigate and enforce applicable antifraud laws.

### Practical Consequences

Suppose a Delaware startup conducts a Rule 506(b) financing involving investors in California, New York, and Texas.

The company generally does not need to register the offering separately in those states.

Nevertheless, it may need to submit state notices and pay applicable filing fees.

The relevant deadlines and procedures vary by jurisdiction.

Accordingly, counsel typically evaluates the locations of offerees and purchasers, the relevant state requirements, and any necessary post-closing filings.

Standalone Section 4(a)(2) and Rule 504 offerings generally do not benefit from the same broad federal preemption of state registration requirements.

## 7. Form D and Transactional Compliance

Issuers relying on Rules 504, 506(b), or 506(c) generally must file Form D electronically with the SEC.

Form D provides notice of an exempt offering and includes specified information concerning the issuer, offering amount, exemption relied upon, and certain related persons.

Under Rule 503, the issuer generally must file Form D within 15 calendar days after the first sale.

For this purpose, the first sale generally occurs when the first investor becomes irrevocably contractually committed to invest, which may precede the transfer of funds.

If the filing deadline falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or holiday, the deadline generally moves to the next business day.

### Does a Late Form D Invalidate the Exemption?

Not automatically.

The SEC has clarified that timely Form D filing is not itself a condition to the availability of the Rule 504 or Rule 506 exemptions.

However, failure to comply with Rule 503 can have regulatory consequences, including those addressed by Rule 507.

The filing obligation therefore remains important even though a late filing does not automatically destroy an otherwise available exemption.

### Other Transactional Requirements

Private financings commonly involve several additional compliance measures.

**Investor questionnaires** help establish investor eligibility and document the issuer's exemption analysis.

**Subscription agreements** establish investment commitments, representations, acknowledgments, and contractual obligations.

**Bad actor diligence** evaluates whether persons covered by the applicable rules have experienced disqualifying events.

**Disclosure materials** address applicable information requirements and the issuer's antifraud obligations.

**Transfer restrictions** reflect the fact that securities acquired in private placements are commonly restricted and cannot freely be resold without registration or an available exemption.

**State notices** address filing and fee requirements in relevant jurisdictions.

These matters should be coordinated with corporate authorizations and the financing's closing procedures.

## 8. Integration of Securities Offerings

Startups frequently raise capital through several transactions over a relatively short period.

A company might issue SAFEs to angel investors, complete a convertible note financing several months later, and subsequently conduct a priced Series A round.

Each offering may independently qualify for an exemption. However, securities counsel must consider whether multiple offerings should be analyzed together under the integration doctrine.

Integration matters because combining offerings can affect the availability of the claimed exemptions.

For example, an issuer cannot necessarily avoid restrictions on general solicitation by dividing a fundraising campaign into transactions with different labels.

### Rule 152

Rule 152 establishes the federal integration framework.

Its general principle considers whether each offering independently complies with Securities Act registration requirements or an available exemption.

For offerings that prohibit general solicitation, the analysis may require the issuer to establish a reasonable belief that purchasers were not solicited through general solicitation used for another offering or that appropriate substantive relationships existed before the relevant offering commenced.

Rule 152 also provides four nonexclusive safe harbors.

One generally addresses offerings separated by at least 30 calendar days, subject to additional conditions when an offering prohibiting general solicitation follows one permitting it.

Other safe harbors address specified employee benefit plan and offshore offerings, certain registered offerings, and offerings permitting general solicitation that follow completed or terminated offerings.

The safe harbors do not mean that every offering separated by 30 days is automatically exempt from integration concerns.

For startup counsel, the analysis requires attention to fundraising chronology, investor communications, the exemptions relied upon, and when each offering commenced or terminated.

## 9. Worked Example: Atlas Labs' Series A Financing

Consider Atlas Labs, Inc., a Delaware corporation preparing to raise $8 million through a Series A preferred stock financing.

The company expects investments from three venture capital funds and several angel investors. It previously issued SAFEs to early investors.

Atlas Labs has not registered the proposed Series A securities with the SEC.

### Step 1: Identify the Offering

Atlas Labs is issuing newly created preferred stock directly to investors in exchange for capital.

The transaction is a primary securities issuance and must be registered or qualify for an exemption.

### Step 2: Evaluate the Available Exemptions

Atlas Labs proposes to approach a limited group of venture funds and angel investors without public advertising.

Assuming the remaining conditions are satisfied, Rule 506(b) may provide an appropriate exemption.

The company could analyze the offering directly under Section 4(a)(2), but Rule 506(b) offers more defined requirements.

The proposed $8 million financing does not exceed an offering limit under Rule 506(b), which permits unlimited capital raising.

### Step 3: Determine Investor Eligibility

Assume all three venture funds qualify as accredited investors under applicable Rule 501(a) categories.

The angel investors require separate analysis.

Some may qualify based on income or net worth, while others may qualify through professional credentials or applicable entity categories.

If Atlas Labs limits the offering to accredited investors, it avoids the additional Rule 502(b) disclosure requirements triggered by non-accredited purchasers.

If a non-accredited purchaser participates, counsel must evaluate the applicable sophistication standard, purchaser limitation, and disclosure requirements.

### Step 4: Review Solicitation Practices

Suppose Atlas Labs approaches venture funds with which its founders have established appropriate investment relationships.

Those communications may be consistent with Rule 506(b), assuming the offering satisfies the prohibition on general solicitation.

Now suppose Atlas Labs publishes an unrestricted webpage inviting the public to invest in its Series A.

That communication may constitute general solicitation, requiring counsel to reconsider the proposed exemption.

Rule 506(c) might permit general solicitation, but every purchaser would need to be accredited, and Atlas Labs would need to take reasonable verification steps.

The exemption analysis therefore depends partly on how the company solicits investors, not merely the qualifications of its ultimate purchasers.

### Step 5: Examine the Prior SAFE Financings

Atlas Labs issued SAFEs several months before the Series A.

Counsel must identify when the prior offerings commenced and terminated, which exemptions applied, and whether the earlier solicitation practices affect the proposed financing.

Rule 152 may support treating the offerings separately, depending on the chronology and facts.

The conversion of outstanding SAFEs into preferred stock also requires analysis of the instruments and the securities-law treatment of the resulting issuance.

### Step 6: Coordinate Compliance and Closing

Assuming Atlas Labs proceeds under Rule 506(b), counsel would typically evaluate investor qualification, disclosure obligations, general solicitation restrictions, and bad actor diligence.

The financing documents should accurately reflect the exemption and the representations needed to support the analysis.

Following the first sale, Atlas Labs generally must file Form D within 15 calendar days, subject to the applicable deadline rules.

Counsel must also determine whether state notice filings and fees are required.

The result is an exempt financing supported by the appropriate documentary and regulatory framework.

## 10. Practical Considerations for Venture Financing Attorneys

The applicable private placement exemption should be evaluated before an issuer begins soliciting investors rather than treated solely as a closing determination.

Attorneys ordinarily examine the issuer, securities, proposed purchasers, solicitation methods, and any related offerings to determine which exemption fits the transaction.

They also coordinate the required disclosures, investor representations, bad actor diligence, federal filings, and state compliance.

These questions are interconnected. An issuer may have accredited investors but still encounter an exemption issue because of its solicitation practices. Likewise, a properly structured federal offering may require state notices or separate analysis of related securities transactions.

The objective is to ensure that the issuer's actual conduct, financing documents, and compliance procedures satisfy the requirements of the exemption relied upon.

## Conclusion

Private placement exemptions form the legal foundation of most startup financings.

Section 4(a)(2) provides the statutory exemption for qualifying nonpublic issuer transactions. Regulation D supplies defined pathways through Rules 506(b), 506(c), and 504.

The differences among these provisions affect investor eligibility, solicitation, disclosure, and transaction execution.

A properly structured financing requires more than identifying an exemption in a subscription agreement. The issuer's offering activities must satisfy the applicable requirements, and the transaction must be documented and administered accordingly.
