A venture-backed company that needs additional capital may find itself negotiating a financing at a lower valuation than its previous round. The company must account for the economic consequences of issuing new shares at a reduced price, the rights of existing preferred stockholders, and any proposed changes to its capital structure.

A down round can be completed through a conventional preferred stock financing. More complicated transactions may involve a recapitalization that modifies existing securities or a pay-to-play arrangement that conditions certain investor rights on participation in the new financing.

These transactions require counsel to evaluate the company's existing obligations before determining how the investment can be implemented. The analysis extends beyond dilution calculations to corporate approvals, charter amendments, contractual rights, and potential conflicts of interest.

## 1. What Is a Down Round?

A *down round* generally occurs when a company issues equity at a lower price per share than in a prior financing.

Suppose a company previously issued Series A Preferred Stock at $2.00 per share. Eighteen months later, it needs additional capital and proposes issuing Series B Preferred Stock at $1.00 per share.

Assuming the share prices are comparable and appropriate adjustments have been made for stock splits or similar events, the Series B would constitute a down round.

The lower price may reflect changes in the company's performance, market conditions, financing alternatives, or negotiating leverage.

Counsel should distinguish the actual issuance price from the company's stated valuation. Differences in capitalization assumptions, option pool treatment, and the rights attached to different securities can complicate comparisons between financing rounds.

A down round may also involve more favorable terms for new investors, including senior liquidation preferences or additional governance rights.

### Why Down Rounds Require Additional Legal Analysis

Issuing shares at a lower price can have several consequences.

First, existing stockholders are diluted when the company issues new equity.

Second, the lower issuance price may trigger anti-dilution protections held by existing preferred stockholders.

Third, existing investor agreements may require participation opportunities, notices, waivers, or approvals.

Finally, the company may seek to modify its existing capital structure to make the investment acceptable to new investors.

A conventional down round does not necessarily require changing existing preferred stock rights. A recapitalization may involve precisely those changes.

## 2. Reviewing Existing Rights Before Structuring the Financing

Counsel's starting point is the company's capitalization and existing financing documentation.

The certificate of incorporation, investors' rights agreement, outstanding convertible instruments, and relevant side letters may each affect the proposed transaction.

### Anti-Dilution Protection

Preferred stock frequently includes price-based anti-dilution protection designed to address subsequent issuances below an established conversion price.

Two common approaches are broad-based weighted-average and full-ratchet anti-dilution protection.

Under a broad-based weighted-average provision, the adjustment generally depends on the size and price of the new issuance relative to the company's capitalization.

Full-ratchet protection generally produces a larger adjustment by reducing the applicable conversion price to the lower issuance price, subject to the governing terms.

These provisions ordinarily increase the number of common shares into which existing preferred stock can convert. They do not necessarily involve issuing additional preferred shares or increasing the existing liquidation preference.

Counsel must review the formula, the definition of consideration received, and the securities excluded from adjustment.

For example, the charter may exempt certain employee equity issuances, conversions of previously outstanding securities, or other specified transactions.

A financing described commercially as a down round does not automatically establish that an anti-dilution adjustment is required. The applicable charter provisions control.

### Protective Provisions

Existing preferred stockholders may have approval rights over specified corporate actions.

A proposed financing could implicate protective provisions governing:

- The creation or issuance of senior or pari passu securities.

- Amendments to preferred stock rights.

- Changes to authorized share capital.

- Certain amendments to the certificate of incorporation.

- Other transactions subject to negotiated investor consent requirements.

The approval threshold may be based on all preferred stock voting together or on a particular series.

These distinctions become especially important when the financing alters existing preferences or introduces a new series with senior rights.

### Participation Rights and Outstanding Securities

The Investors' Rights Agreement may grant qualifying investors rights to purchase a portion of newly issued securities.

These rights may be described as pro rata participation rights or rights of first offer.

Counsel should examine the notice procedures, eligibility requirements, calculation methods, and excluded securities.

Existing SAFEs, convertible notes, warrants, and options must also be reviewed. The financing may trigger conversion obligations or affect the capitalization calculations.

The ownership analysis must reflect the new issuance and any adjustments or conversions occurring in connection with closing.

## 3. How Is a Recapitalization Different From a Conventional Down Round?

A *recapitalization* involves changing a company's existing capital structure or the rights associated with its outstanding securities.

Although recapitalizations may accompany down rounds, the concepts are not interchangeable.

A company may complete a down round by issuing Series B Preferred Stock while leaving its existing Series A Preferred Stock outstanding under substantially unchanged terms.

In a more extensive restructuring, investors may require changes to the existing capitalization before providing additional financing.

A recapitalization may involve:

- Converting an existing preferred stock series into common stock.

- Exchanging outstanding preferred shares for a newly created preferred series.

- Reducing or eliminating existing liquidation preferences.

- Changing preferred stock conversion ratios.

- Modifying anti-dilution or voting rights.

- Reorganizing the company's authorized and outstanding shares.

Some transactions also use reverse stock splits or other share reclassifications to implement the negotiated capitalization.

### Why Would Investors Require a Recapitalization?

A company that has completed several financing rounds may have substantial liquidation preferences outstanding.

Suppose investors previously contributed an aggregate $25 million in exchange for preferred stock carrying 1x liquidation preferences.

The company later seeks financing at a valuation below the amount of those outstanding preferences.

A new investor may be reluctant to contribute capital if existing preferred stockholders retain substantial priority over future sale proceeds.

One possible solution is to negotiate a restructuring of those rights as a condition to the financing.

The transaction might reduce the existing liquidation preference overhang, establish different distribution priorities, or otherwise reallocate risks and potential returns among existing and new investors.

These changes can materially affect existing stockholders and must be implemented through appropriate corporate procedures.

## 4. What Are Pay-to-Play Provisions?

A *pay-to-play provision* conditions specified benefits of an investor's existing securities on participation in a future financing.

Its purpose is generally to encourage existing investors to contribute additional capital rather than retaining all their existing preferred stock protections while other investors fund the company.

The consequences of failing to participate vary.

Under one structure, a preferred stockholder who does not purchase its required allocation of new securities may have its existing preferred shares converted into common stock.

Other arrangements may convert nonparticipating investors into a different preferred stock series carrying reduced economic or governance rights.

Some provisions impose narrower consequences, such as the loss of specified anti-dilution protections or participation rights.

The operative terms must establish who must participate, how the required investment is calculated, and what happens if the investor does not satisfy the condition.

### Prospective and Transaction-Specific Pay-to-Play Provisions

A pay-to-play obligation may already exist in the company's certificate of incorporation.

For example, Series A investors may agree that failure to participate in a qualifying future financing will trigger specified consequences for their Series A shares.

Alternatively, a company negotiating a down round may seek to introduce a pay-to-play arrangement through a recapitalization.

The second situation can be more complicated because the existing securities may not already be subject to those conditions.

The company cannot assume that existing preferred stock rights may be eliminated merely because the proposed financing includes a pay-to-play requirement.

Counsel must determine whether the contemplated treatment is authorized by the governing documents or requires a charter amendment, exchange agreement, or other corporate action.

### Example: Converting Nonparticipating Preferred Stockholders

Assume a company has 4 million shares of Series A Preferred Stock outstanding.

The Series A investors originally paid $2.00 per share, and the stock carries a 1x nonparticipating liquidation preference.

The aggregate initial liquidation preference is $8 million.

The company subsequently negotiates a Series B financing that includes a recapitalization.

Under the proposed arrangement, qualifying Series A holders who purchase their required allocation in the new round retain their preferred stock. Those who do not participate have their shares converted into common stock at a negotiated one-for-one ratio.

Assume the holders of 60% of the Series A shares satisfy the participation requirement, while holders of the remaining 40% do not.

The resulting treatment would be:

| **Treatment** | **Series A shares affected** | **Aggregate initial preference retained** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Participating holders retain preferred stock | 2,400,000 | $4,800,000 |
| Nonparticipating holders convert to common stock | 1,600,000 | $0 |
| **Total** | **4,000,000** | **$4,800,000** |

The recapitalization reduces the aggregate initial Series A liquidation preference from $8 million to $4.8 million.

Nonparticipating holders retain equity through their resulting common shares, but those shares no longer carry the Series A liquidation preference.

The example assumes that the conversion mechanism has been validly adopted and that the one-for-one ratio is the agreed treatment under the recapitalization. Any otherwise applicable anti-dilution adjustments must be accounted for or appropriately waived.

The example addresses only the previously outstanding Series A shares. Participating investors separately receive the new securities they purchase in the Series B financing.

### Distinguishing Pay-to-Play From Pro Rata Rights

Pay-to-play obligations should not be confused with ordinary pro rata participation rights.

A pro rata right generally gives an eligible investor an opportunity to purchase securities in a subsequent financing.

A pay-to-play provision imposes consequences if an investor does not participate as required.

An investor may therefore have a contractual opportunity to purchase additional shares without being obligated to do so.

The mechanisms should be reviewed separately, even when both apply to the same financing.

## 5. Corporate Approvals and Implementing the Recapitalization

Approval requirements depend on whether the transaction involves a new stock issuance, amendments to existing stock rights, or a broader reclassification of outstanding shares.

For a Delaware corporation, several provisions of the Delaware General Corporation Law (DGCL) may be relevant.

### Board Approval and Stock Issuance

DGCL Section 141 generally places management of the corporation under the direction of its board of directors, subject to applicable statutory and charter provisions.

Under DGCL Sections 152 and 153, the board generally determines the consideration for newly issued shares, subject to statutory requirements and the certificate of incorporation.

The board must authorize the proposed stock issuance and related transactions.

Counsel should confirm that the corporation has sufficient authorized shares and that the issuance complies with applicable requirements.

### Amendments to the Certificate of Incorporation

DGCL Section 151 permits corporations to establish classes and series of stock with differing economic and voting rights through the applicable charter mechanisms.

A financing that creates a new preferred stock series or changes existing preferences may require a charter amendment.

Under DGCL Section 242, charter amendments generally require appropriate board action and stockholder approval, subject to statutory exceptions.

Section 242(b)(2) also grants class voting rights, and in certain circumstances separate series voting rights, for amendments affecting specified stock rights.

For example, an amendment that adversely changes the powers, preferences, or special rights of a particular preferred stock series may require a separate statutory vote by the affected holders under the conditions established by Section 242(b)(2).

The statutory analysis depends on what rights the amendment changes. An issuance that is economically dilutive does not necessarily trigger a separate statutory class or series vote merely because existing stockholders are adversely affected economically.

The certificate of incorporation may separately impose protective provisions requiring preferred stockholder approval.

These statutory and charter-based requirements must be evaluated independently. An amendment may require approval under both.

### Implementing a Pay-to-Play Conversion

Where a pay-to-play arrangement converts existing preferred shares into common stock or another series, counsel must confirm the legal basis for the conversion.

The documents should establish the conversion conditions, exchange ratios, timing, and treatment of holders who satisfy or fail the participation requirement.

If the existing certificate does not authorize the proposed conversion, an amendment or other valid restructuring mechanism may be necessary.

The required stockholder approvals and contractual consents must be obtained before the relevant changes become effective.

Where several securityholder groups or preferred series are involved, the approval analysis can be considerably more complicated than in a conventional new-money financing.

## 6. Fiduciary Duties and Conflicts in Insider-Led Down Rounds

Down rounds and recapitalizations can present significant fiduciary duty issues, particularly when existing investors negotiate and approve transactions from which they receive benefits unavailable to other stockholders.

Consider a company whose board includes directors designated by venture capital funds.

The company needs additional capital, and those funds propose investing in a new preferred stock series at a substantially reduced valuation.

The same investors may benefit from the lower share price, enhanced preferences, or a recapitalization that adversely affects nonparticipating stockholders.

The presence of investor-designated directors does not automatically establish a disabling conflict. The analysis depends on the directors' relationships, interests, and involvement in the transaction.

Nevertheless, counsel should identify potential conflicts before the board authorizes the financing.

### Delaware Fiduciary Duty Considerations

Delaware courts have examined recapitalizations in which existing investors obtained increased ownership or other benefits at the expense of nonparticipating stockholders.

In *In re Nine Systems Corp. Shareholders Litigation*, the Delaware Court of Chancery considered a recapitalization involving conflicted directors and investors.

Applying the entire fairness standard under the law governing that case, the court concluded that the transaction was not entirely fair despite finding the price fair, because the process was seriously deficient.

The decision illustrates the importance of examining how a conflicted recapitalization is negotiated, approved, and disclosed.

Current Delaware law must also be considered.

In 2025, Delaware amended DGCL Section 144 to establish statutory protections for specified transactions involving interested directors, officers, controlling stockholders, and control groups.

Depending on the transaction, Section 144 provides procedures involving disinterested director or stockholder approval, as well as statutory provisions addressing fairness. The requirements differ for interested director or officer transactions, controlling stockholder transactions, and controlling stockholder going-private transactions.

The Delaware Supreme Court upheld the constitutionality of the 2025 amendments in 2026.

Counsel must therefore evaluate whether a particular transaction falls within the statute and whether the applicable conditions have been satisfied. Compliance with Section 144 also does not eliminate the need for proper corporate authorization or compliance with governing documents.

### Structuring the Approval Process

Where material conflicts exist, counsel should consider appropriate procedures for evaluating and negotiating the transaction.

Depending on the circumstances, those measures may include:

- Identifying directors and investors with material interests in the transaction.

- Evaluating whether disinterested directors or an appropriately constituted committee should negotiate or approve the financing.

- Developing a supportable record for the proposed valuation and economic terms.

- Considering available financing alternatives.

- Evaluating participation opportunities for existing stockholders.

- Providing material information necessary for stockholders asked to approve the transaction to make an informed decision.

A rights offering, for example, may provide eligible existing stockholders with an opportunity to participate in the financing.

That approach can be relevant to the fairness analysis, but it is not automatically required or sufficient. The opportunity, eligibility restrictions, disclosures, and economic terms matter.

Counsel should evaluate the approval process under applicable fiduciary duty principles and the statutory protections available for the particular transaction.

## 7. Worked Transaction: From Down Round to Recapitalization

Consider Atlas Labs, Inc., a Delaware corporation seeking $3 million in additional capital.

Its existing capitalization includes:

| **Security** | **Outstanding shares** | **Relevant terms** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common Stock | 8,000,000 | Held by founders and other common stockholders |
| Series A Preferred Stock | 4,000,000 | Originally issued at $2.00 per share; 1x nonparticipating preference |
| **Total before financing** | **12,000,000** | Before applicable conversion adjustments |

Assume each Series A share initially converts into one common share and there are no additional securities or option reserves affecting this example.

Atlas Labs proposes issuing 3 million Series B Preferred shares at $1.00 per share, raising $3 million.

Because the Series B issuance price is below the previous Series A price, the transaction is a down round.

### Step 1: Determine the Effect of the New Issuance

Without anti-dilution adjustments or other capitalization changes, the issuance would increase outstanding shares from 12 million to 15 million.

The new investors would acquire 3 million shares.

However, these figures may not represent the final fully diluted capitalization.

If the Series A anti-dilution provisions are triggered, the existing preferred stock's conversion ratio may change.

Counsel must apply the relevant formula and reconcile the resulting common-equivalent shares with the financing capitalization table.

### Step 2: Review Existing Investor Rights

The Series B financing may require Series A approval if the new stock carries senior liquidation preferences or other rights covered by existing protective provisions.

Existing investors may also possess contractual participation rights.

Counsel must identify the necessary notices, approvals, and waivers.

### Step 3: Evaluate a Proposed Recapitalization

Assume the new investor will proceed only if Atlas Labs adopts the pay-to-play restructuring described earlier.

Under that arrangement, holders of 60% of the Series A shares satisfy the participation requirement and retain their preferred stock.

The remaining 40% are converted into common stock under a validly adopted one-for-one conversion mechanism.

For this example, also assume the requisite holders approve a waiver of any price-based Series A anti-dilution adjustment that would otherwise result from the Series B issuance.

The aggregate initial Series A preference falls from $8 million to $4.8 million.

The new Series B shares carry a $3 million aggregate initial liquidation preference, assuming a 1x preference based on the $1.00 purchase price.

The parties must negotiate whether Series B ranks senior to or pari passu with the remaining Series A Preferred Stock.

Under these assumptions, the post-closing capitalization is:

| **Security** | **Shares outstanding** | **As-converted ownership** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Common Stock, including converted Series A shares | 9,600,000 | 64% |
| Remaining Series A Preferred Stock | 2,400,000 | 16% |
| New Series B Preferred Stock | 3,000,000 | 20% |
| **Total** | **15,000,000** | **100%** |

This table illustrates the simplified capitalization resulting from the negotiated conversion and new issuance. The common stock figure includes 1.6 million shares received by nonparticipating Series A holders.

Actual financing calculations must also address any outstanding options, convertible securities, different conversion ratios, or other capitalization adjustments.

### Step 4: Implement the Transaction

Counsel must coordinate the approved capitalization changes with the definitive financing documents.

The amended charter must establish the new securities and changes to existing rights.

The Stock Purchase Agreement must reflect the authorized shares, purchase price, representations, and closing conditions.

The Investors' Rights Agreement, Voting Agreement, and other existing agreements may require amendment, termination, waiver, or joinders from applicable holders.

Corporate consents and the necessary charter filing must become effective in the appropriate sequence.

The closing capitalization table should reconcile the new Series B issuance, existing preferred shares retained or converted, liquidation preferences, voting rights, and applicable conversion adjustments.

The company must also update its stock ledger and other corporate records and address applicable securities-law requirements.

## 8. Common Issues in Down-Round Documentation

Several issues merit attention during document preparation and closing.

**Incorrect capitalization assumptions.** A financing spreadsheet may omit anti-dilution adjustments, outstanding instruments, or changes to the employee equity pool.

**Incomplete consent analysis.** Required statutory class or series votes may differ from charter-based protective provisions and contractual approval rights.

**Inconsistent treatment of preferred stock.** A recapitalization may change liquidation preferences, conversion rights, or voting rights without those changes being reflected consistently across the financing documents.

**Unclear pay-to-play mechanics.** The documents must identify qualifying investments, participation thresholds, affected securities, and the consequences of nonparticipation.

**Undocumented conflicts.** Insider-led financings require attention to interested parties, the approval process, disclosures, and applicable fiduciary duty requirements.

**Closing sequence.** Charter amendments, security conversions, investor consents, new share issuances, and funds flow must be coordinated so that each step occurs with the required legal authority.

The closing checklist should address both ordinary financing deliverables and the additional actions required by any restructuring.

## Conclusion

Down rounds require corporate counsel to evaluate more than the price at which new shares will be issued.

Existing preferred stock rights, anti-dilution provisions, investor approval requirements, and capitalization adjustments may affect the structure and execution of the financing.

Where the transaction involves a recapitalization or pay-to-play arrangement, counsel must also consider changes to outstanding securities, required corporate approvals, and potential conflicts among investors and other stockholders.

The central task is ensuring that the financing documents, capitalization records, and corporate actions implement the negotiated transaction consistently and lawfully.
