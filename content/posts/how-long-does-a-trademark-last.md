When a business registers a trademark with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO), how long does that protection last?

Unlike patents and copyrights, trademarks do not have a fixed maximum term of protection. Trademark rights can potentially continue indefinitely, provided the applicable legal requirements remain satisfied.

Federal trademark registrations, however, require periodic maintenance. Registration does not permanently secure a mark without further action.

Understanding the distinction between trademark rights and federal registration helps explain what happens after registration and why continued use matters.

## Do Trademarks Expire?

Trademark rights and trademark registrations are related, but they are not the same thing.

In the United States, trademark rights generally arise through qualifying use of a distinctive mark to identify the commercial source of goods or services.

Those rights can potentially continue for as long as the mark remains legally protectable and the requirements for maintaining those rights are satisfied.

A federal trademark registration, meanwhile, operates in ten-year terms and can be renewed for successive ten-year periods without a predetermined limit.

A business could therefore maintain the same federal trademark registration for decades, provided it satisfies the applicable use, maintenance, and renewal requirements.

## What Are the Trademark Renewal Requirements?

For most federal registrations, the USPTO requires maintenance filings at specific intervals measured from the registration date.

| **Time after registration** | **Required filing** |
| --- | --- |
| Between years 5 and 6 | Section 8 Declaration of Use or Excusable Nonuse |
| Between years 9 and 10 | Combined Section 8 Declaration and Section 9 Renewal |
| Every 10 years thereafter | Combined Section 8 Declaration and Section 9 Renewal |

### The First Maintenance Filing: Years 5–6

Between the fifth and sixth anniversaries of registration, the owner generally must submit a Section 8 declaration.

This filing includes a verified statement concerning continued trademark use, supporting evidence, and the required government fees.

An acceptable specimen generally must be submitted for each class of goods or services covered by the maintenance filing.

A qualifying claim of excusable nonuse may be available in limited circumstances.

### Renewal: Years 9–10

Between the ninth and tenth anniversaries, the owner generally must submit a combined Section 8 declaration and Section 9 renewal application.

This filing addresses continued trademark use and renewal of the registration for another ten-year term.

### Subsequent Renewals

The same combined filing requirements recur during the year preceding each successive ten-year anniversary.

For example, a registration maintained through its initial term would ordinarily face additional renewal periods between years 19 and 20, 29 and 30, and so forth.

These deadlines are generally calculated from the registration date, not the original application filing date.

The requirements described above apply to most domestic federal registrations. Registrations obtained through the Madrid Protocol involve different maintenance procedures.

## What Happens If a Renewal Deadline Is Missed?

The USPTO provides a six-month grace period after the regular deadlines for required Section 8 and Section 9 filings.

Filings submitted during the grace period are subject to additional government fees.

If the required documents are not filed within the applicable period, including the grace period, the USPTO generally cancels the registration or allows it to expire.

Losing a federal registration means losing the legal benefits associated with that registration.

However, cancellation or expiration does not necessarily extinguish every underlying trademark right.

Depending on the circumstances, a business may retain common-law rights arising from qualifying trademark use, even without an active federal registration.

Those rights are separate from the registration itself. A canceled or expired registration generally cannot simply be renewed after the applicable deadlines have passed, and obtaining another federal registration ordinarily requires a new application.

For more information about unregistered trademark rights, see *[Don't Overlook Common-Law and State Trademarks](dont-overlook-common-law-and-state-trademarks.html)*.

## Does a Business Have to Keep Using Its Trademark?

Generally, yes.

Continued qualifying use is central to maintaining federal trademark registration.

Consider TempoGarden, a hypothetical company that registers its name in connection with an online productivity software service.

Several years later, the company approaches its first maintenance deadline.

The USPTO generally requires evidence showing that the trademark continues to be used in commerce with the relevant service.

For a software service, that evidence might include an acceptable webpage displaying the mark in connection with the service being provided.

However, trademark registrations often cover multiple goods or services.

Suppose TempoGarden's registration covers both downloadable software and its online productivity platform. The company subsequently discontinues the downloadable product but continues offering the online service.

That change may affect which goods and services remain properly covered by the registration.

The USPTO requires accurate maintenance filings and expects registration records to reflect qualifying use of the identified goods or services, subject to applicable exceptions.

The agency may also request additional evidence through its post-registration audit program.

An attorney preparing maintenance filings therefore examines the registration's identification of goods and services alongside the business's current commercial activities.

For more information about evidence of trademark use, see *[What Is a Trademark Specimen?](what-is-a-trademark-specimen.html)*

## What Happens If a Business Stops Using Its Trademark?

Discontinuing trademark use can raise questions about *abandonment*.

Under Section 45 of the Lanham Act, a trademark may be abandoned when its use is discontinued with an intent not to resume.

The statute provides that **three consecutive years of nonuse constitute prima facie evidence of abandonment**.

*Prima facie evidence* generally means evidence sufficient to establish a presumption unless rebutted.

This does not mean every trademark automatically becomes abandoned after three years of nonuse. The determination depends on the applicable legal standards and circumstances.

For example, an attorney evaluating a discontinued software product may examine when use stopped, why the business discontinued the product, and evidence concerning its intent to resume use.

A trademark may also be abandoned if it loses its significance as an identifier of commercial source, including when it becomes the generic name for the relevant goods or services.

These issues are distinct from filing maintenance documents. A registration's active status does not conclusively resolve every question concerning continued trademark rights.

## What If Trademark Use Is Temporarily Interrupted?

Federal law recognizes limited circumstances in which nonuse may be excused for registration maintenance purposes.

Under Section 8 of the Lanham Act, an owner may establish that nonuse results from special circumstances that excuse the interruption and does not reflect an intention to abandon the mark.

The USPTO generally requires specific supporting facts concerning the reasons for nonuse and the owner's efforts to resume use.

For example, reduced customer demand, by itself, does not necessarily constitute excusable nonuse.

An attorney evaluating an interruption may consider its cause, expected duration, and the circumstances surrounding the business's operations.

The availability of excusable nonuse for maintenance purposes is also distinct from whether a trademark has been abandoned under the applicable law.

## What Happens After Five Years of Trademark Registration?

Another concept frequently arises around the fifth anniversary of registration: *incontestability*.

Under Section 15 of the Lanham Act, certain marks registered on the Principal Register may qualify for additional statutory protections after at least five consecutive years of qualifying use following registration.

An owner may claim those protections through an optional Declaration of Incontestability, provided the applicable legal requirements are satisfied, including restrictions involving adverse decisions and pending proceedings.

An incontestable registration receives enhanced evidentiary protection concerning specified aspects of the registration and trademark rights.

However, incontestability does not prevent every legal challenge.

Importantly, **a Section 15 declaration is not the same as the required Section 8 maintenance filing**.

Section 8 concerns maintaining the registration. Section 15 is optional and concerns additional legal protections available to qualifying registrations.

The two declarations may be filed together when the relevant requirements are satisfied.

## How Do Trademark Attorneys Handle Registration Maintenance?

Trademark maintenance involves more than reviewing a registration's anniversary date.

An attorney may examine the current registration record, the identified goods or services, evidence of continued use, and changes in the business's operations.

For example, a company may have discontinued one product while continuing to use its trademark with several other offerings. That change may affect the information and evidence required in its maintenance filing.

Ownership changes, licensing arrangements, and corporate reorganizations can also raise questions relevant to the registration record.

The attorney's role includes evaluating these circumstances and preparing filings that accurately reflect the owner's rights and commercial activities.

## The Bottom Line

A trademark can potentially remain protected indefinitely, but federal registration requires continued attention.

Most federal registrations require maintenance filings between years five and six, renewal between years nine and ten, and subsequent renewal filings every ten years.

Continued qualifying use remains important throughout the trademark's life. Extended nonuse, changes in business operations, and missed maintenance deadlines can affect trademark rights or registration status.

Federal registration therefore does not necessarily expire after ten years, but obtaining registration is only one stage in maintaining a business's trademark protection.
