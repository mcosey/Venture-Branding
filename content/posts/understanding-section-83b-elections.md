*An introduction to Section 83(b) elections for law students and attorneys entering emerging companies practice.*

Suppose two founders form a Delaware corporation and purchase common stock at a nominal price. Their shares are subject to four-year vesting arrangements, allowing the company to repurchase unvested shares if a founder leaves.

At issuance, the shares are worth very little. If the company succeeds, however, their value may increase substantially before they finish vesting.

This creates an important federal income tax question: **When does the appreciation become taxable?**

Section 83 of the Internal Revenue Code governs property transferred in connection with the performance of services, including certain compensatory stock issuances. Its general rule can cause restricted stockholders to recognize ordinary income as their shares vest.

A Section 83(b) election allows an eligible recipient to recognize the compensation element of the transfer immediately, potentially avoiding ordinary income recognition on subsequent appreciation.

For corporate attorneys, the issue arises frequently in founder stock issuances, early-exercised stock options, and other equity compensation arrangements.

## 1. The General Rule Under Section 83(a)

Section 83(a) generally addresses property transferred in connection with services.

When property is subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture and is not transferable within the meaning of the statute, compensation income is ordinarily recognized when the property becomes substantially vested.

Property generally becomes substantially vested when it is transferable or no longer subject to a substantial risk of forfeiture, whichever occurs first.

A substantial risk of forfeiture may exist when the recipient must continue providing substantial services to retain the property.

For example, a founder might purchase 100,000 shares under an agreement permitting the corporation to repurchase unvested shares at their original cost if the founder stops working for the company.

That arrangement may create a substantial risk of forfeiture because the founder must continue providing services to retain the shares free of the repurchase right.

Under Section 83(a), compensation income is generally calculated as:

**Ordinary income = Fair market value at vesting − Amount paid for the shares**

If shares vest in installments, the calculation generally applies separately to each tranche, using the fair market value when that tranche becomes substantially vested.

Consequently, appreciation during the vesting period can increase the recipient's ordinary compensation income.

Not every contractual restriction creates a substantial risk of forfeiture. The applicable analysis depends on the nature and enforceability of the restriction under the Treasury Regulations.

## 2. How Does a Section 83(b) Election Change the Tax Treatment?

Section 83(b) permits an eligible service provider to elect to recognize the compensation element of substantially nonvested property when it is transferred rather than when it vests.

The election changes the timing of income recognition.

**Ordinary income under Section 83(b) = Fair market value at transfer − Amount paid for the property**

Fair market value is generally determined without regard to restrictions that will lapse, as provided in the applicable regulations.

Suppose a founder purchases restricted stock for $1,000 when its fair market value is also $1,000.

A timely election produces:

$1,000 − $1,000 = **$0 of ordinary income**

Although no immediate compensation income results, the election can still have significant consequences.

Treasury Regulation § 1.83-2 expressly permits an election when the recipient pays full fair market value for the property.

With a valid election, subsequent appreciation generally does not become compensation income when the shares vest. Instead, the appreciation may be recognized as capital gain when the shares are eventually sold.

The election does not change the contractual vesting schedule or eliminate the corporation's repurchase rights.

## 3. A Numerical Example: With and Without an 83(b) Election

Consider TempoGarden, Inc., a hypothetical Delaware software company.

A founder purchases 100,000 restricted common shares for $0.01 per share, paying $1,000 in total.

Assume:

| **Event** | **Value per share** | **Total fair market value** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Initial issuance | $0.01 | $1,000 |
| Vesting after two years | $2.00 | $200,000 |
| Subsequent sale | $5.00 | $500,000 |

For simplicity, assume all shares vest at once after two years and are sold more than one year later. Actual founder stock commonly vests in installments.

Also assume the shares are capital assets in the founder's hands and no special capital gains exclusions apply.

### Scenario A: The Founder Makes a Timely Election

At issuance, the founder pays $1,000 for shares worth $1,000.

The compensation income recognized under the election is:

$1,000 − $1,000 = **$0**

Two years later, the stock vests when its value has increased to $200,000.

Because the founder made a valid election, the vesting ordinarily produces no additional compensation income.

When the founder sells the shares for $500,000, the capital gain is:

**Capital gain = Sale proceeds − Adjusted tax basis**

$500,000 − $1,000 = **$499,000**

Assuming the applicable holding-period requirements are satisfied, the gain is long-term capital gain.

### Scenario B: The Founder Does Not Make an Election

Without the election, the founder generally recognizes compensation income when the shares become substantially vested.

At vesting, their fair market value is $200,000.

The ordinary income is:

$200,000 − $1,000 = **$199,000**

This income may be taxable even though the founder has not sold the shares and received no cash proceeds.

The founder's tax basis increases to $200,000, reflecting the purchase price and compensation income recognized.

When the founder subsequently sells the stock for $500,000:

$500,000 − $200,000 = **$300,000 of capital gain**

The founder has recognized $199,000 of ordinary income and $300,000 of capital gain.

### Comparing the Outcomes

Assume illustrative federal tax rates of 35% for ordinary compensation income and 20% for long-term capital gains.

| **Tax consequence** | **With election** | **Without election** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary income | $0 | $199,000 |
| Ordinary income tax at 35% | $0 | $69,650 |
| Long-term capital gain | $499,000 | $300,000 |
| Capital gains tax at 20% | $99,800 | $60,000 |
| **Total illustrative federal tax** | **$99,800** | **$129,650** |

Under these assumptions, making the election results in **$29,850 of federal tax savings**.

The example excludes state and local taxes, employment taxes, the net investment income tax, and other adjustments. It assumes the founder satisfies the vesting conditions and ultimately sells the shares at a gain.

The potential benefit comes from recognizing little or no compensation income at transfer while allowing subsequent appreciation to receive capital gains treatment, subject to the applicable tax rules.

## 4. What Happens If the Stock Declines in Value or Is Forfeited?

An election is not necessarily advantageous in every circumstance.

Suppose an employee pays $100,000 for restricted stock worth $200,000.

A timely election generally results in:

$200,000 − $100,000 = **$100,000 of compensation income**

If the employee subsequently forfeits the stock before vesting, the compensation income previously recognized generally cannot be deducted merely because the shares were forfeited.

Treasury Regulation § 1.83-2 provides a limited loss rule.

**Loss upon forfeiture = Amount paid for property − Amount realized upon forfeiture**

If the property is a capital asset, the resulting loss is generally a capital loss.

The amount previously included as compensation is not added to the amount recoverable under this forfeiture rule.

A decline in the stock's value creates a related risk. The taxpayer may have paid tax based on a higher initial value and cannot simply reverse that income inclusion when the stock depreciates.

A Section 83(b) election generally cannot be revoked without IRS consent, which is available only in limited circumstances. A mistaken prediction about future value does not ordinarily qualify.

These risks become more significant when the stock has substantial value at transfer or the recipient may not satisfy the vesting conditions.

## 5. Which Types of Startup Equity Are Eligible?

The distinction between restricted stock, stock options, and restricted stock units is particularly important.

### Restricted Founder Stock

Founders frequently acquire actual shares shortly after incorporation.

The shares may be subject to *reverse vesting*, under which the founder owns the stock immediately but the corporation retains a repurchase right over unvested shares.

Where that arrangement creates substantially nonvested property for tax purposes, an 83(b) election may be available.

The relevant deadline begins with the transfer of the stock, not necessarily the date the corporation was incorporated, the board approved the issuance, or the stock purchase agreement was signed.

Determining the actual transfer date requires examining the transaction and applicable tax rules.

### Early-Exercised Nonqualified Stock Options

A stock option generally gives its holder the right to purchase shares at a specified exercise price.

An ordinary option grant does not itself transfer the underlying shares for Section 83(b) purposes.

However, some options permit *early exercise* before vesting.

Upon early exercise, the holder purchases actual stock, which may remain subject to forfeiture or repurchase restrictions.

For a nonqualified stock option, an eligible recipient may make an election concerning the restricted shares acquired through early exercise.

Suppose an employee exercises an option to purchase 10,000 unvested shares at $1 per share when the shares' fair market value is also $1.

The initial taxable spread is zero.

A timely election generally prevents subsequent appreciation from becoming compensation income as the shares vest.

**The 30-day election period generally begins when the restricted shares are transferred upon exercise, not when the option was originally granted.**

### Incentive Stock Options

Incentive stock options (ISOs) are governed by separate statutory tax rules.

For an early-exercised ISO, a Section 83(b) election may affect the alternative minimum tax (AMT) calculation.

The election generally causes the applicable AMT adjustment to be determined using the spread at exercise rather than the spread when the shares vest.

However, the election operates differently from one involving nonqualified options. In particular, it does not start the ordinary income tax holding period for determining capital gain treatment on a disqualifying disposition of the ISO shares.

The ISO rules also impose holding-period requirements for favorable treatment, generally involving both the grant date and exercise date.

These distinctions warrant separate analysis by tax counsel.

### Restricted Stock Units

Restricted stock units (RSUs) generally represent contractual rights to receive shares or cash upon future settlement.

Because an RSU grant ordinarily does not transfer property to the recipient, an 83(b) election generally is not available upon grant.

An RSU should therefore not be confused with an actual restricted stock issuance.

We'll explore these instruments further in *Stock Options, Restricted Stock, and RSUs in Startup Compensation*.

## 6. Tax Basis, Holding Periods, and Qualified Small Business Stock

An election also affects the recipient's tax basis and capital gains holding period.

### Tax Basis

The initial tax basis for property subject to a valid Section 83(b) election generally equals:

**Tax basis = Amount paid + Compensation income recognized**

Suppose an employee pays $10,000 for restricted shares worth $15,000.

The election produces $5,000 of ordinary income.

The employee's initial tax basis is:

$10,000 + $5,000 = **$15,000**

That basis is used to determine gain or loss upon a later disposition, subject to subsequent adjustments.

### Capital Gains Holding Period

A valid election generally causes the capital gains holding period for restricted stock to begin at transfer.

Without the election, the holding period ordinarily begins as the stock becomes substantially vested.

This difference can affect whether a subsequent sale produces short-term or long-term capital gain.

### Qualified Small Business Stock

The election may also affect the holding period relevant to qualified small business stock (QSBS) under Section 1202.

Under the rules applicable to qualifying stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the federal gain exclusion generally increases with the holding period:

| **Holding period** | **Potential federal gain exclusion** |
| --- | --- |
| At least 3 years | 50% |
| At least 4 years | 75% |
| At least 5 years | 100% |

Different rules apply to stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025.

QSBS eligibility also depends on requirements concerning the issuing corporation, original issuance, qualifying business activities, asset limitations, and other statutory conditions.

A Section 83(b) election does not itself establish QSBS eligibility or guarantee a gain exclusion. It may, however, affect when the relevant holding period begins for restricted stock.

Given the potential tax consequences, the Section 1202 analysis should be conducted separately.

## 7. How Is a Section 83(b) Election Filed?

Section 83(b)(2) requires the election to be made **no later than 30 days after the property is transferred**.

The deadline is statutory and generally cannot be extended through ordinary discretionary filing relief.

The service provider makes the election, even when the corporation prepares the associated stock documents.

### IRS Form 15620

The IRS provides [Form 15620, Section 83(b) Election](https://www.irs.gov/pub/irs-pdf/f15620.pdf), which requests information including:

- The taxpayer's identifying information.

- The type and quantity of property transferred.

- The transfer date and applicable taxable year.

- The restrictions affecting the property.

- Fair market value at transfer.

- The amount paid and resulting compensation income.

A taxpayer may also make the election using a signed written statement satisfying Treasury Regulation § 1.83-2.

The IRS's current mobile-friendly forms program lists Form 15620 as available through an online submission process requiring an IRS Online Account. The downloadable PDF instructions continue to describe filing the completed form by mail with the applicable IRS office.

Taxpayers and practitioners should verify the requirements of the filing method selected.

For mailed elections, the IRS instructions recognize the operation of Section 7503 when the thirtieth day falls on a Saturday, Sunday, or legal holiday. Under the stated conditions, a timely postmark on the next qualifying business day may satisfy the deadline.

Maintaining evidence of timely submission is important. For mailed elections, that may include appropriate USPS mailing records. For electronic submissions, the taxpayer should retain the available submission confirmation.

### Copies and Recordkeeping

The taxpayer must provide a copy of the election to the person for whom the services were performed.

If the person performing the services and the transferee of the property are different, a copy must also be furnished to the transferee.

Since regulatory amendments effective in 2016, taxpayers are generally no longer required to attach another copy to their federal income tax returns.

Nevertheless, the signed election, filing confirmation, supporting valuation materials, and stock documentation should be retained.

Because an election can be valid even when the immediate taxable amount is zero, a taxpayer should not assume that the absence of current tax liability makes timely filing unnecessary.

## 8. Why Section 83(b) Matters in Corporate Practice

Section 83(b) is a tax provision, but the issue frequently arises through corporate transactional work.

Company counsel may prepare restricted stock purchase agreements, early-exercise documentation, board approvals, and related equity issuance materials.

Those documents determine important facts relevant to the tax analysis, including what property was transferred, when the transfer occurred, and which restrictions apply.

For example, a stock purchase agreement might permit the corporation to repurchase unvested shares at cost upon termination of service.

That restriction may establish the substantial risk of forfeiture underlying the Section 83 analysis.

Corporate attorneys should therefore recognize when an equity issuance warrants consideration of an election, even when specialized tax counsel advises on its ultimate consequences.

Responsibility for preparing, filing, and documenting elections should also be clearly established.

The taxpayer bears the filing obligation, but company counsel may coordinate the process, provide the required stock information, or assist in maintaining appropriate records, depending on the engagement.

Counsel may also need to coordinate with payroll and tax advisors concerning compensation reporting and withholding obligations.

These considerations are particularly important because an error in identifying the stock transfer date or allocating filing responsibilities can have significant consequences.

## Conclusion

Section 83(b) allows an eligible recipient of substantially nonvested property to recognize the compensation element of a transfer immediately instead of when the property becomes substantially vested.

For startup equity, the election can prevent appreciation during the vesting period from being treated as ordinary compensation income. It can also affect tax basis, capital gains holding periods, and potentially the holding period relevant to QSBS treatment.

The benefits depend on the property's initial value, the recipient's tax circumstances, the applicable restrictions, and its subsequent performance.

For attorneys entering ECVC practice, understanding Section 83(b) means recognizing when a stock issuance implicates the election, understanding its economic consequences, and identifying the applicable transfer date and filing requirements.

The election's short statutory deadline makes those issues especially important at the time equity is issued.
