Suppose two businesses sell comparable products at nearly identical prices. Despite their similarities, customers consistently prefer one brand because of its reputation, product quality, and their previous experiences with the company.

That preference has commercial value and represents an important component of what the law recognizes as goodwill.

The relationship between trademarks and goodwill predates federal trademark legislation and helps explain many of the legal principles governing trademark protection today.

## What Is Goodwill?

Goodwill is an intangible commercial asset arising from the reputation, customer relationships, recognition, and other advantages a business develops through its operations.

Businesses cultivate goodwill in numerous ways, including:

- Providing reliable products and services.

- Developing positive customer relationships.

- Establishing a recognizable commercial identity.

- Investing in advertising and marketing.

- Maintaining consistent standards of quality.

Over time, consumers may associate a business with particular qualities or experiences that encourage repeat purchases and contribute to the company's value.

A trademark identifies the commercial source associated with those experiences. A recognizable name, logo, or other source identifier can therefore symbolize substantial commercial goodwill.

## How Did Trademark Protection Originate?

Although merchants have used identifying marks for centuries, American trademark law developed in part from English common-law principles concerning unfair competition and passing off.

Passing off generally involved a merchant representing their goods as those of another.

In *Perry v. Truefitt* (1842), an English court recognized that a merchant could not sell their own goods under the false pretense that they were produced by someone else.

Such conduct harmed both the original merchant and the purchasing public. The merchant could lose sales or suffer reputational damage, while consumers could unknowingly purchase goods from an unintended source.

Similar principles developed in American courts.

In *Thomson v. Winchester* (1837), one of the earliest reported American trademark cases, Samuel Thomson brought suit against a druggist who sold an allegedly inferior medicinal product under the name “Thomsonian Medicines.”

The Massachusetts court distinguished between fraudulently selling medicines as Thomson's own and legitimately using the name to describe a general type of medicine. That distinction anticipated an important principle of modern trademark law: businesses cannot claim exclusive trademark rights in generic terms for their goods or services.

For much of the nineteenth century, American trademark protection developed primarily through state law. Congress enacted the first federal trademark legislation in 1870, but the U.S. Supreme Court declared its trademark provisions unconstitutional in 1879.

Congress subsequently enacted new trademark legislation in 1881 and 1905, eventually establishing the modern federal trademark framework through the Lanham Act of 1946.

## What Is the Relationship Between Trademarks and Goodwill?

In *Hanover Star Milling Co. v. Metcalf* (1916), the U.S. Supreme Court explained:

> The redress that is accorded in trademark cases is based upon the party's right to be protected in the good will of a trade or business.

The dispute involved competing flour manufacturers using the TEA ROSE trademark in different geographic markets.

In addressing their competing claims, the Court explained that common-law trademark rights arise through use rather than mere adoption. A trademark derives its commercial significance from its association with the goods or services of a particular business.

This principle helps explain why trademark ownership does not confer unrestricted ownership of a word, phrase, or symbol.

For example, a company may develop considerable goodwill under a particular name in the market for running shoes without necessarily acquiring exclusive rights to that name in unrelated industries.

The scope of trademark protection depends on factors including the mark's distinctiveness, its commercial use, the goods or services with which it is associated, and applicable priority rules.

## How Does Protecting Goodwill Benefit Consumers?

Suppose a customer purchases coffee from a particular brand and is satisfied with its quality. When purchasing coffee again, the customer can recognize the brand without independently investigating the manufacturer or production process.

Trademarks reduce the time and effort consumers spend identifying products and services they wish to purchase.

The U.S. Supreme Court discussed this economic function in *Qualitex Co. v. Jacobson Products Co.* (1995), explaining how trademarks facilitate purchasing decisions and allow businesses to benefit from the reputations associated with their products.

These principles also encourage businesses to invest in quality. When consumers can reliably identify the commercial source of a product, businesses have an incentive to maintain the standards that contribute to repeat purchases and favorable reputations.

## How Does Modern Trademark Law Protect Goodwill?

Several modern trademark doctrines reflect the relationship between trademarks and goodwill:

- **Trademark infringement:** Likelihood-of-confusion principles protect against commercial uses that are likely to mislead consumers about the source, sponsorship, or affiliation of goods or services.

- **Trademark assignments:** Section 10 of the Lanham Act generally requires the transfer of associated goodwill when assigning a registered trademark or a trademark subject to a federal application.

- **Trademark licensing:** Appropriate quality control over a licensee's goods or services helps preserve the trademark's significance as a reliable source identifier.

- **Trademark abandonment:** Discontinued commercial use accompanied by an intention not to resume use, or certain conduct that causes a mark to lose its significance as a trademark, can result in the loss of trademark rights.

- **Trademark dilution:** Federal law provides qualifying famous trademarks with additional protection against certain uses likely to impair their distinctiveness or harm their reputation, even without likely consumer confusion.

Although protecting goodwill is a central purpose of trademark law, benefiting from another company's reputation or harming its goodwill does not, by itself, establish trademark infringement.

Businesses may legitimately refer to competitors' products, engage in truthful comparative advertising, and criticize other brands, subject to applicable legal requirements.

## Why Is Protecting Goodwill Important for Businesses Today?

A company may spend years developing a reputation that its competitors cannot easily replicate. In the digital marketplace, however, third parties can reproduce recognizable branding and reach substantial numbers of consumers with relatively little investment.

Consider a skincare company that has developed a reputation for producing high-quality products. An unrelated seller begins offering counterfeit products under the company's trademark through an online marketplace.

Consumers who unknowingly purchase the counterfeit products may attribute negative experiences to the original company. Beyond losing sales, the company risks damage to the reputation it has spent years developing.

Similar concerns may arise from fraudulent websites, misleading social media accounts, and other activities that create a false impression of affiliation with an established business.

Trademark infringement can therefore affect future purchasing decisions, customer loyalty, and the long-term value of a brand.

## How Can Businesses Protect Their Trademark Goodwill?

Businesses can take several measures to help protect the goodwill associated with their trademarks:

- Conducting appropriate trademark clearance before investing substantially in a brand.

- Considering federal trademark registration.

- Maintaining consistent, qualifying commercial use of their marks.

- Monitoring the marketplace for potentially infringing uses.

- Exercising appropriate quality control over licensed trademarks.

- Properly documenting trademark ownership transfers.

Trademark clearance is particularly important during the early stages of brand development. A business that must change its name because of another party's earlier trademark rights may face substantial rebranding expenses and difficulty preserving the customer recognition it has already established.

Although trademark rights may arise through use without federal registration, registration provides important additional legal protections and procedural advantages.

A trademark attorney can help evaluate potential conflicts, existing trademark rights, and registration opportunities as a business develops its brand.

## The Bottom Line

Goodwill is a valuable intangible asset that businesses develop through their commercial activities, reputation, and customer relationships.

Since its common-law origins, trademark law has helped protect commercial goodwill by enabling consumers to reliably identify the sources of goods and services.

Protecting the trademarks associated with a business can help preserve the commercial value it has developed over time.
