Suppose you're preparing to launch a business. After settling on a name, you discover that the corresponding .com domain is available. You purchase it, register your business, and begin developing your website.

Does owning the domain mean you also own the trademark rights to the name?

Not necessarily. Domain names and trademarks serve different legal purposes. Although a domain name can sometimes function as a trademark, registering one does not automatically establish trademark rights or prevent another business from using the same name.

## Does Registering a Domain Name Give Me Trademark Rights?

No. Registering a domain name does not, by itself, establish trademark rights.

A domain name identifies an internet address, allowing users to locate a website. A trademark identifies and distinguishes the commercial source of goods or services.

Domain names are registered through domain registrars, while federal trademarks are registered through the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO).

These processes are independent. When a registrar makes a domain available for purchase, it generally does not determine whether another business has trademark rights in the corresponding name.

For example, the availability of `SummitForge.com` does not necessarily mean a new business can use "Summit Forge" as its brand without encountering another party's existing trademark rights.

Trademark rights generally arise through qualifying commercial use, subject to applicable registration and priority rules.

## Can a Domain Name Function as a Trademark?

Yes. A domain name may qualify for trademark protection when it identifies the commercial source of goods or services.

The distinction depends on how the designation is used.

For example, a business might display `SummitForge.com` only as a website address in its contact information. Consumers encountering it in that context may understand it simply as a way to access the company's website.

Alternatively, the business might prominently use "`SummitForge.com`" as the brand identifying its products or services. In that context, the designation may function as a trademark.

The USPTO evaluates whether consumers would perceive the designation as identifying a commercial source rather than merely providing a website address.

A domain name must also satisfy ordinary trademark registration requirements, including distinctiveness and the absence of disqualifying conflicts.

Adding ".com" to a word or phrase does not automatically make the resulting designation distinctive.

## What If Someone Else Owns the Trademark for My Domain Name?

Owning a domain does not prevent another party from asserting existing trademark rights.

Suppose a company has operated for several years under the trademark "Summit Forge" in connection with fitness equipment.

Another entrepreneur subsequently registers `SummitForge.com` and begins selling similar products through the website.

Although the entrepreneur acquired the domain through an authorized registrar, the website's branding and commercial activities could raise trademark infringement concerns.

The legal analysis would depend on the parties' respective rights, the goods or services involved, and whether consumers are likely to be confused about the source or affiliation of the businesses.

However, a trademark owner does not automatically have superior rights to every domain containing its mark. A domain registrant may have legitimate rights or interests arising from independent commercial use or other circumstances.

## What If I Registered My Domain Before Someone Else Registered the Trademark?

The date a domain was registered does not necessarily establish priority of trademark rights.

In the United States, trademark rights can arise through qualifying commercial use, even without federal registration. Merely holding an unused domain generally does not establish those rights.

For example, suppose an entrepreneur registers `SummitForge.com` but leaves it unused for two years.

During that period, another company begins selling products under the name "Summit Forge" and establishes trademark rights through qualifying commercial use.

The earlier domain registration would not, by itself, establish that the entrepreneur has superior trademark rights.

However, the registration date may still matter in a domain dispute. For example, under the UDRP, a domain registered before the complainant acquired trademark rights will generally present a significant obstacle to proving bad-faith registration, although exceptions may apply.

Attorneys evaluating these disputes examine the parties' trademark rights, their histories of commercial use, and the circumstances surrounding the domain's acquisition and use.

## What Is Cybersquatting?

Cybersquatting generally involves acquiring or using domain names to improperly exploit another party's trademark rights.

Federal law addresses certain forms of cybersquatting through the Anticybersquatting Consumer Protection Act (ACPA), codified at Section 43(d) of the Lanham Act.

The ACPA provides a potential claim against a person who registers, traffics in, or uses certain domain names with a bad-faith intent to profit from another party's protected mark.

For example, a person might register a domain resembling an established trademark to exploit the mark's reputation or sell the domain to the trademark owner at an inflated price.

Courts consider several circumstances when evaluating bad faith, including the registrant's legitimate interests, prior use, intentions concerning the domain, and history of similar registrations.

Registering a domain containing another party's trademark does not automatically constitute cybersquatting. A registrant may have legitimate rights or interests, and domain investing or reselling is not inherently unlawful.

Whether conduct violates the ACPA depends on the statutory requirements and surrounding facts.

## Can a Trademark Owner Recover a Domain Name From Someone Else?

Several legal mechanisms may be available when a domain registration conflicts with another party's trademark rights.

### Uniform Domain-Name Dispute-Resolution Policy (UDRP)

The UDRP is an administrative dispute-resolution process established through the Internet Corporation for Assigned Names and Numbers (ICANN).

It applies to certain domain registrations and addresses allegedly abusive registrations.

To prevail, a complainant generally must establish that:

- The domain is identical or confusingly similar to a trademark or service mark in which the complainant has rights.

- The registrant lacks rights or legitimate interests in the domain.

- The domain was registered and is being used in bad faith.

A successful UDRP proceeding may result in cancellation or transfer of the domain registration, but not monetary damages.

### Federal litigation

Trademark owners may also pursue qualifying claims in federal court, including claims under the ACPA.

Depending on the circumstances, courts may order the transfer or cancellation of a domain and award other relief authorized by law.

The UDRP and federal litigation involve different legal requirements and remedies. Neither guarantees that a trademark owner will obtain a disputed domain.

## Does Owning a Trademark Mean I Automatically Get the Matching Domain?

No. Federal trademark registration does not automatically entitle a business to every domain containing its mark.

For example, businesses operating in unrelated industries may have legitimate reasons to use identical or similar names. A domain registrant may also have acquired legitimate rights before another party established its trademark rights.

The significance of these circumstances depends on the parties' rights, how the domain is used, and the applicable legal standards.

Trademark registration can provide grounds for challenging certain domain registrations, but it does not confer unrestricted ownership of every internet address containing the mark.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Evaluate a Domain Name Dispute?

An attorney evaluating a domain dispute may examine the relevant trademark registrations, evidence of commercial use, and the domain's ownership history.

The analysis may also consider when the domain was acquired, how it has been used, the parties' goods or services, and evidence of legitimate interests or bad faith.

These facts help distinguish disputes involving potentially confusing commercial activity from those involving allegedly abusive domain registrations.

Attorneys also evaluate whether the circumstances implicate trademark infringement, the ACPA, the UDRP, or other applicable legal principles.

## The Bottom Line

Registering a domain name does not automatically create trademark rights in the corresponding name.

A domain may qualify for trademark protection when used to identify the commercial source of goods or services, but domain ownership and trademark ownership remain legally distinct.

Likewise, owning a federal trademark registration does not automatically entitle a business to every matching domain. Competing rights depend on the parties' commercial activities and the applicable legal standards.
