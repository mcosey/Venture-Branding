You've chosen a name for your business, registered a domain, and launched a website. You may even have a product ready for testing.

But have you actually begun using your trademark in commerce?

This distinction matters because federal trademark applications generally require qualifying use of a mark in connection with the goods or services identified. Although businesses can file applications before launching, most domestic applications cannot proceed to registration until the applicant establishes use in commerce.

The legal requirements are more specific than displaying a name or logo on a website.

## What Does “Use in Commerce” Actually Mean?

Under Section 45 of the Lanham Act, 15 U.S.C. § 1127, use in commerce means bona fide use of a trademark in the ordinary course of trade, rather than use made merely to reserve rights in a mark.

Generally, this requires a business to use its trademark in connection with actual goods or services in commerce that Congress may lawfully regulate.

Simply choosing a business name, incorporating a company, or purchasing a domain name does not ordinarily establish trademark use in commerce.

Likewise, artificial transactions arranged solely to support a trademark application generally do not satisfy the requirement.

The nature of the required use depends on whether the mark identifies goods or services.

## How Does Use in Commerce Differ for Goods and Services?

The Lanham Act establishes different requirements for trademarks used with goods and service marks used with services.

### For Goods

A trademark generally must appear on the goods, their packaging, labels, or qualifying displays associated with the goods. The goods must also be sold or transported in commerce.

For example, a clothing company might display its trademark on labels attached to shirts sold to customers.

For downloadable software, a qualifying display might be a webpage identifying the software under the mark and allowing customers to obtain it.

Merely advertising goods that are not yet available for sale or distribution generally does not establish qualifying use.

### For Services

A service mark generally must be used or displayed in the sale or advertising of services, and those services must actually be rendered in qualifying commerce.

For example, a company providing online project management software might display its trademark on the website through which customers access the service.

However, advertising services that the company has not yet begun providing generally does not establish use in commerce.

The distinction is particularly relevant to software businesses. Downloadable applications are generally treated as goods, while software as a service (SaaS) is generally classified as a service.

## Does Having a Website Mean You're Using Your Trademark?

Consider TempoGarden, a hypothetical startup developing productivity software.

The founders have incorporated their business, registered TempoGarden.com, and created a website displaying the TempoGarden name and logo.

The website announces that the product is coming soon and invites visitors to join a waiting list.

Although the company has taken meaningful steps toward launching, those activities alone generally do not establish trademark use in commerce for its software.

Now suppose TempoGarden launches its browser-based productivity platform and begins providing the service to users under the TempoGarden mark.

The company is now providing software functionality rather than merely advertising a future offering.

Depending on the nature of the service, the commercial circumstances, and how the mark is displayed, that activity may constitute qualifying trademark use.

## Do You Need Paying Customers?

Not necessarily.

A business does not always need to generate revenue before its trademark use can qualify under federal law.

For example, distributing downloadable software without charge can potentially establish trademark use when the distribution is genuine and consistent with ordinary practices in the software industry.

Similarly, offering a free online service or public beta may constitute qualifying use even before paid subscriptions become available.

The USPTO recognizes that a functioning website labeled "beta" may demonstrate actual trademark use. The beta designation alone does not determine whether the use qualifies.

An attorney evaluating a beta launch may examine whether the product or service is genuinely available to outside users, whether services are actually being rendered, how the trademark identifies the offering, and whether the activity is consistent with ordinary commercial practices.

A limited internal test among employees may present different circumstances from a functioning public beta.

The legal distinction concerns genuine commercial use rather than the collection of revenue.

## Does the Business Have to Operate Across State Lines?

Federal trademark law applies to commerce that Congress may lawfully regulate.

Interstate commerce, such as selling products to customers in multiple states, is a familiar example. Commerce between the United States and another country may also qualify.

However, a business operating entirely within one state is not necessarily excluded from federal trademark registration.

Certain intrastate activities can qualify when they sufficiently affect interstate or other federally regulated commerce.

For example, a restaurant operating in one state may serve interstate travelers. An online business located in one state may provide services to customers elsewhere.

Whether particular activities satisfy the federal commerce requirement depends on the circumstances.

A trademark attorney therefore examines the business's actual commercial activity rather than assuming that having only one physical location determines eligibility.

## How Does Use in Commerce Affect When You Can File?

Federal trademark applications commonly proceed under one of two domestic filing bases.

### Section 1(a): Use in Commerce

A Section 1(a) application is based on existing qualifying trademark use.

The mark must already be in use in commerce for the goods or services identified under that filing basis when the application is filed.

For example, if TempoGarden offers its browser-based platform but has not yet launched its downloadable application, use associated with the online service does not necessarily establish qualifying use for the downloadable software.

### Section 1(b): Intent to Use

A Section 1(b) application allows a business to file before establishing qualifying use, provided it has a bona fide intention to use the mark in commerce.

TempoGarden might file an intent-to-use application while its founders are still developing the product.

The company can begin the application process before launch, but generally must establish qualifying use and complete the applicable filing requirements before registration issues.

## How Does an Attorney Evaluate Trademark Use?

Determining whether a mark has been used in commerce requires understanding what the business offers and how the mark appears in connection with those offerings.

A trademark attorney may examine when products were first sold or distributed, when services were first rendered, and whether those activities satisfy the applicable commerce requirements.

The attorney also evaluates whether the mark functions as an identifier of commercial source.

For example, a company might prominently display its corporate name on promotional materials while using a different brand to identify its product. That distinction can affect which mark is being used and for what goods or services.

Another consideration is the date of first use.

For applications based on use in commerce, the USPTO generally requires two dates:

- **Date of first use anywhere:** When the mark was first used in connection with the relevant goods or services in a bona fide manner, regardless of whether the use occurred in interstate or other federally regulated commerce.

- **Date of first use in commerce:** When the mark was first used in connection with those goods or services in commerce that Congress may lawfully regulate.

The dates may be identical, but they are not necessarily the same.

## Why Does Continued Trademark Use Matter?

Use in commerce remains important after registration.

Federal registrations generally require periodic filings demonstrating continued qualifying use or establishing an applicable basis for excusable nonuse.

A trademark may also be abandoned when its use is discontinued with an intent not to resume.

Under the Lanham Act, three consecutive years of nonuse constitutes prima facie evidence of abandonment. This creates a rebuttable presumption, rather than automatic cancellation after three years.

A company that discontinues a product or service may therefore need to consider the implications for its trademark rights.

## The Bottom Line

Use in commerce generally requires genuine trademark use connected to actual goods or services, rather than preparation to enter the marketplace.

A business does not necessarily need paying customers or operations in multiple states, but its activities must satisfy the applicable legal requirements.

For founders preparing to register a trademark, the distinction between choosing a brand, advertising a future offering, and actually using the mark can affect the appropriate filing basis and timing of registration.

Determining whether qualifying use has occurred requires evaluating the particular goods, services, and commercial activities involved.
