When starting a business, founders often focus on how to divide ownership among themselves. But that ownership can change as the company hires employees, raises money, and issues additional equity.

A capitalization table, commonly called a *cap table* , tracks those changes. It shows who owns the company, how much each person owns, and how existing or potential equity issuances may affect ownership percentages.

Understanding your cap table is particularly important before fundraising. A founder who owns 50% of a company today may own substantially less after accounting for employee equity, early investors, and future financing rounds.

## 1. What Is a Cap Table?

A cap table summarizes a company's equity ownership. It typically identifies stockholders, the number and types of shares they hold, and their percentage ownership.

As a company grows, its cap table may also track stock options, warrants, convertible securities, and shares reserved for future employee equity awards.

Founders use cap tables to evaluate ownership, understand the effects of proposed investments, and plan equity grants. Investors examine them to identify existing ownership interests and potential dilution.

A cap table should be consistent with the company's legal records, including stock issuance agreements, equity award documentation, and its stock ledger. The cap table itself does not establish that shares were legally issued.

## 2. Understanding Shares and Ownership Percentages

A share represents an ownership interest in a corporation. A stockholder's percentage ownership depends on the number of shares held relative to the total shares included in the calculation.

The basic formula is:

**Ownership Percentage = Shares Owned ÷ Total Outstanding Shares × 100**

Suppose two founders establish a corporation and each receives 4 million shares.

| Stockholder | Shares | Ownership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founder A | 4,000,000 | 50% |
| Founder B | 4,000,000 | 50% |
| Total | 8,000,000 | 100% |

Each founder owns half the company's outstanding stock.

The absolute number of shares matters less than the percentage they represent. Owning 4 million shares out of 8 million produces the same ownership percentage as owning 400 shares out of 800.

### Authorized vs. Outstanding Shares

A corporation's *authorized shares* are the shares it is permitted to issue under its certificate of incorporation. *Outstanding shares* are shares that have been issued and remain outstanding.

Suppose the founders' corporation authorized 10 million shares but issued only 8 million.

The remaining 2 million shares are authorized but unissued. They do not belong to anyone and are not included when calculating ownership based solely on outstanding shares.

Authorizing additional shares does not itself dilute existing stockholders. Issuing additional shares generally reduces their percentage ownership, while broader capitalization calculations may account for shares that could be issued later.

## 3. What Is Fully Diluted Ownership?

A founder may encounter two different ownership percentages for the same shares: outstanding ownership and fully diluted ownership.

**Outstanding ownership** considers shares currently issued and outstanding.

**Fully diluted ownership** uses a broader share count that includes specified potential issuances, such as shares underlying stock options and warrants and, depending on the calculation, shares reserved for future employee grants.

Consider the same two-founder company. It establishes an employee equity incentive plan reserving 2 million shares for future awards.

| Holder or category | Shares | Fully diluted ownership |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founder A | 4,000,000 | 40% |
| Founder B | 4,000,000 | 40% |
| Employee equity reserve | 2,000,000 | 20% |
| Total | 10,000,000 | 100% |

Each founder still owns 50% of the company's 8 million outstanding shares. Under a fully diluted calculation that includes the employee reserve, however, each founder has 40%.

No employee has received those reserved shares. The difference comes from including potential future issuances in the calculation.

Fully diluted ownership does not have a single definition applicable to every transaction. Financing agreements may treat options, reserved shares, and convertible instruments differently. When reviewing an ownership percentage, it is important to understand which securities were included.

## 4. How Employee Equity Affects Your Cap Table

Startups frequently offer equity compensation to attract employees and other service providers.

A common arrangement is a *stock option* , which gives the holder the right to purchase shares at a specified exercise price, subject to the terms of the award.

For example, a company might grant an employee an option to purchase 50,000 shares. The employee generally does not own those shares until the option is exercised and the shares are issued.

Other arrangements include restricted stock, which consists of issued shares that may remain subject to vesting or repurchase restrictions, and restricted stock units (RSUs), which generally provide rights to receive shares or their value when specified conditions are satisfied.

These distinctions matter when reading a cap table. Shares issued to employees, shares underlying outstanding options, and shares available for future grants are separate categories.

As a company grants options from its existing equity reserve, part of that reserve becomes allocated to specific employees. This generally does not increase the fully diluted share count if the granted options were already included in the reserve and no additional shares are authorized for the plan. Maintaining accurate equity records helps founders distinguish current stock ownership from potential future dilution.

## 5. How Fundraising Changes Your Cap Table

When a company raises capital by issuing new shares, investors become stockholders and existing owners generally experience dilution.

Two valuation concepts help explain the effect.

A **pre-money valuation** is the negotiated value of the company immediately before a financing. A **post-money valuation** includes the new investment.

**Post-Money Valuation = Pre-Money Valuation + New Investment**

Suppose the hypothetical company raises $5 million at a $15 million pre-money valuation.

Its post-money valuation would be $20 million.

Assume the financing uses the company's 10 million fully diluted pre-financing shares, including the employee equity reserve, to determine the purchase price. There are no outstanding convertible securities or additional option pool increases.

The price per share would be:

**$15,000,000 ÷ 10,000,000 = $1.50**

For their $5 million investment, the new investors would receive approximately 3.33 million shares.

| Holder or category | Before financing | After financing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Founder A | 40% | 30% |
| Founder B | 40% | 30% |
| Employee equity reserve | 20% | 15% |
| New investors | 0% | 25% |
| Total | 100% | 100% |

The new investors acquire approximately 25% of the company's post-financing fully diluted capitalization. Each founder's percentage decreases from 40% to 30%, even though neither founder sells any shares.

This is *dilution* : the founders retain the same number of shares, but those shares represent a smaller percentage of the expanded capitalization.

Dilution is not necessarily unfavorable. The company has also received $5 million in new capital. A founder may own a smaller percentage of a more valuable business, although the value of that ownership ultimately depends on the company's performance and the rights attached to its securities.

## 6. How SAFEs and Convertible Notes Affect Ownership

Not every startup investment immediately results in issued shares.

Early-stage companies frequently raise money through *simple agreements for future equity* *(SAFEs)* or convertible promissory notes.

A SAFE generally gives an investor the right to receive equity upon a future financing or another specified event. A convertible note is debt that may convert into equity under its terms.

Because these instruments ordinarily do not immediately issue shares, a cap table showing only outstanding stock may not reflect their eventual impact on ownership.

### Why Conversion Matters

Suppose the founders' company raises money through several SAFEs before completing its Series A financing.

When those SAFEs convert, investors may receive preferred stock, increasing the company's outstanding shares and diluting existing stockholders.

The number of shares depends on the SAFEs' conversion provisions, including their valuation caps or discounts.

For example, a $500,000 investment under a standard post-money SAFE with a $5 million valuation cap generally corresponds to 10% of the relevant capitalization immediately before the new investment in a subsequent priced financing, assuming the cap-based conversion mechanics apply.

The percentage can change if the SAFE converts under different pricing provisions. New investment and an associated option pool increase can also cause additional dilution. Founders with outstanding convertible instruments should therefore understand both their current cap table and a *pro forma cap table* , which models ownership after anticipated conversions and financing transactions.

The actual results depend on the executed agreements and their capitalization definitions.

## 7. The Option Pool Shuffle: Why Fundraising Terms Matter

An employee equity reserve can affect financing economics in ways that are easy to overlook.

Investors sometimes require startups to expand their option pools before completing an investment, ensuring that shares remain available for future employee grants.

The timing of that expansion matters.

In many venture financings, investors negotiate for the additional option pool shares to be included in the company's pre-money capitalization when determining the purchase price.

This is commonly called a *pre-money option pool increase* .

Under that arrangement, the additional reserve generally reduces existing stockholders' fully diluted ownership without reducing the new investors' negotiated percentage.

For example, an investor might offer to invest at a $15 million pre-money valuation but require a larger employee equity reserve as part of the financing.

If that additional reserve is included in the pre-money share count, the purchase price per share decreases. The investor receives more shares for the same investment, while existing stockholders retain a smaller percentage.

Two financing offers with identical headline valuations can therefore produce different ownership outcomes.

This is why the employee equity reserve deserves attention alongside valuation when evaluating financing terms.

## 8. Why Your Cap Table Matters Beyond Fundraising

An accurate cap table is useful throughout a company's development.

When adding a co-founder, it helps illustrate how a proposed equity allocation affects existing ownership. When recruiting employees, it helps the company understand how much equity remains available for future grants. Investors examine capitalization during due diligence to identify outstanding securities, existing owners, and contractual rights to acquire equity.

Cap tables are also important during acquisitions and other exit transactions.

However, ownership percentages do not necessarily determine how sale proceeds are distributed.

Venture capital investors frequently hold preferred stock with liquidation preferences that may entitle them to receive certain proceeds before common stockholders.

A founder who owns 30% of a company should therefore not assume they will receive 30% of the proceeds from every acquisition.

The outcome depends on the company's capitalization, the rights attached to its securities, and the terms of the transaction.

## 9. Common Cap Table Mistakes

Several misunderstandings can make capitalization records misleading.

**Confusing authorized and outstanding shares.** Authorized but unissued shares do not represent existing ownership.

**Treating the option pool as employee-owned stock.** Shares reserved for future grants are not necessarily issued or owned by employees.

**Ignoring SAFEs and convertible notes.** Outstanding convertible instruments may create substantial dilution when they convert.

**Overlooking option pool increases.** The size and timing of a negotiated equity reserve can change the economics of a financing.

**Assuming ownership percentages remain constant.** New financings, stock issuances, and equity awards may change a founder's percentage ownership.

**Relying on the cap table instead of legal documentation.** Recording shares or options in a spreadsheet does not establish that they were properly authorized or issued.

Founders should maintain accurate capitalization records that remain consistent with the company's legal documentation.

A cap table provides a useful picture of ownership, but its value depends on understanding the share counts, potential future issuances, and assumptions underlying its percentages.
