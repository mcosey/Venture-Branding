Delaware is the preferred state of incorporation for many publicly traded companies and venture-backed startups in the United States. More than two-thirds of Fortune 500 companies are incorporated there, according to the Delaware Division of Corporations. Many operate primarily in other states.

Delaware's appeal rests on its flexible corporate statute, specialized judiciary, extensive body of corporate precedent, and familiarity to institutional investors. Together, these advantages have established Delaware as the leading jurisdiction for companies seeking venture capital financing.

## 1. The Delaware General Corporation Law

The Delaware General Corporation Law (DGCL), codified in Title 8 of the Delaware Code, governs the formation, management, and dissolution of Delaware corporations.

The DGCL is generally considered an *enabling statute*, meaning it allows corporations substantial flexibility in structuring their governance and capitalization. Companies can establish different classes of stock, allocate governance rights, and adopt arrangements suited to their particular circumstances, subject to statutory limitations.

This flexibility is particularly useful for venture-backed startups. Under Section 151, for example, a corporation may create classes or series of stock with different rights and preferences. Founders might initially receive common stock, while investors in a subsequent financing receive preferred stock carrying liquidation preferences, conversion rights, and special voting protections.

Several DGCL provisions are particularly important in corporate practice:

- **Section 102:** Establishes the requirements for certificates of incorporation and permits certain additional governance provisions.

- **Section 141:** Generally vests management of a corporation's business and affairs in its board of directors.

- **Section 152:** Governs the board's authorization of stock issuances and related consideration.

- **Section 228:** Generally permits stockholder action by written consent without a meeting, subject to statutory and charter limitations.

- **Section 242:** Governs amendments to certificates of incorporation, including applicable approval requirements.

These provisions form the statutory foundation for routine corporate transactions, including stock issuances, amendments to governing documents, and venture financing rounds.

The DGCL's flexibility operates alongside mandatory statutory requirements and fiduciary obligations. Understanding Delaware corporate law therefore requires familiarity with both the statute and the judicial doctrines governing its application.

## 2. Important Delaware Corporate Law Principles

Several foundational principles help explain how Delaware corporate law operates and why it is attractive to companies and investors.

### The Internal Affairs Doctrine

The *internal affairs doctrine* is a choice-of-law principle under which a corporation's internal affairs are generally governed by the law of its state of incorporation.

Internal affairs concern the relationships among a corporation, its directors, officers, and stockholders, including fiduciary duties, governance arrangements, and stockholder voting rights.

In *VantagePoint Venture Partners 1996 v. Examen, Inc.*, 871 A.2d 1108 (Del. 2005), the Delaware Supreme Court held that Delaware law governed certain preferred-stockholder voting rights in a merger involving a Delaware corporation headquartered in California, notwithstanding a conflicting California statutory requirement.

The doctrine generally allows corporations operating across multiple states to rely on a consistent body of law governing their internal affairs. It does not exempt Delaware corporations from otherwise applicable employment, securities, tax, or other state and federal laws.

### Fiduciary Duties and the Business Judgment Rule

Directors of Delaware corporations owe fiduciary duties, traditionally described as the duties of care and loyalty.

The duty of care generally requires directors to make informed decisions through an appropriate decision-making process. The duty of loyalty requires directors to place the corporation's interests ahead of conflicting personal interests. Delaware law also recognizes good faith as an essential component of fiduciary conduct.

The *business judgment rule* generally presumes that, in making business decisions, directors act on an informed basis, in good faith, and in the honest belief that their decisions serve the corporation's best interests.

When the presumption applies and is not rebutted, courts ordinarily defer to directors' business judgments rather than second-guess their decisions.

The rule provides meaningful protection for directors making decisions involving commercial uncertainty. However, conflicts of interest, inadequate decision-making processes, and certain other circumstances may subject corporate actions to heightened judicial scrutiny.

### Exculpation and Indemnification

Section 102(b)(7) of the DGCL permits corporations to include provisions in their certificates of incorporation limiting or eliminating certain directors' and officers' personal liability for monetary damages arising from fiduciary-duty breaches.

For directors, these provisions commonly protect against monetary liability for breaches of the duty of care. They do not eliminate liability for breaches of the duty of loyalty, bad-faith conduct, intentional misconduct, knowing violations of law, or transactions involving improper personal benefits.

In 2022, Delaware expanded Section 102(b)(7) to permit exculpation of specified corporate officers, subject to additional limitations. Notably, officer exculpation does not apply to actions brought by or in the right of the corporation.

Exculpation should be distinguished from *indemnification*, governed principally by Section 145. While exculpation limits certain monetary liabilities, indemnification concerns a corporation's ability or obligation to cover specified expenses and liabilities incurred by its directors and officers. Advancement of litigation expenses is a related but separate protection.

These mechanisms help corporations attract qualified directors and officers while preserving important limitations on their protection from liability.

### Corporate Authority and Stockholder Rights

Section 141(a) generally places management of a Delaware corporation in its board of directors. Stockholders retain voting rights over matters specified by statute and the corporation's governing documents.

For example, amendments to a certificate of incorporation ordinarily require board approval and the applicable stockholder vote under Section 242. Certain amendments may also require separate class or series approvals.

Venture-backed corporations frequently have additional approval requirements established through preferred stock protective provisions or contractual investor rights.

Consequently, an attorney evaluating a proposed financing must consider the DGCL, the corporation's certificate of incorporation and bylaws, and its existing financing agreements. A corporate action may be permissible under Delaware law but still require additional approvals under the corporation's governing documents or contractual arrangements.

## 3. The Delaware Judiciary: The Court of Chancery and Delaware Supreme Court

Delaware's judiciary is a major reason companies choose to incorporate in the state. The Court of Chancery and Delaware Supreme Court have developed a substantial body of precedent addressing fiduciary duties, corporate governance, mergers, stockholder rights, and commercial transactions.

### The Court of Chancery

The Delaware Court of Chancery is a specialized court of equity that routinely adjudicates complex corporate disputes.

Its jurisdiction includes many matters involving fiduciary duties, mergers and acquisitions, corporate governance, and the interpretation of corporate instruments. These cases are generally decided by experienced judicial officers without juries.

The court's specialization is particularly valuable when disputes require prompt resolution. For example, a contested merger may involve a request for an injunction preventing the transaction from closing. The Court of Chancery can evaluate the relevant corporate law questions and, where appropriate, provide equitable relief.

Its extensive body of written opinions also gives transactional attorneys guidance when assessing legal risks and structuring transactions.

### The Delaware Supreme Court

The Delaware Supreme Court is the state's highest court and the ultimate authority on Delaware corporate law.

Appeals from the Court of Chancery generally proceed directly to the Supreme Court, allowing significant corporate law questions to receive authoritative appellate review without an intermediate appellate court.

The Supreme Court has played a central role in developing Delaware's fiduciary-duty jurisprudence.

For example, in *Smith v. Van Gorkom*, 488 A.2d 858 (Del. 1985), the court found that directors had breached their duty of care by approving a merger through a grossly negligent decision-making process. The decision helped prompt Delaware's subsequent adoption of Section 102(b)(7), permitting specified limitations on directors' monetary liability.

The Supreme Court's decisions establish binding precedent that guides the Court of Chancery and informs corporate practice.

### The Value of Judicial Predictability

The relationship between the Court of Chancery and Delaware Supreme Court creates an important institutional advantage.

Corporate attorneys can consult decades of judicial decisions when evaluating fiduciary obligations, drafting governance provisions, and structuring transactions. Although litigation outcomes remain fact-dependent, this developed body of precedent can reduce legal uncertainty.

Delaware's attractiveness therefore derives not only from the DGCL itself, but also from the courts responsible for interpreting and applying it.

## 4. Why Venture Capital Investors Prefer Delaware Corporations

Institutional venture capital investors commonly invest in Delaware C corporations. Their familiarity with Delaware law permits the use of established financing structures and transaction documents.

The National Venture Capital Association (NVCA), for example, publishes model documents widely used in venture financings, including certificates of incorporation, stock purchase agreements, investors' rights agreements, voting agreements, and right of first refusal and co-sale agreements.

Consider a startup preparing for its first priced equity financing. The company may need to authorize a new series of preferred stock, amend its certificate of incorporation, obtain board and stockholder approvals, and negotiate agreements governing investors' economic and voting rights.

Delaware law provides a familiar framework for implementing these arrangements. Attorneys and investors who routinely work with Delaware corporations can also draw on established documentation and precedent when evaluating the transaction.

This familiarity reinforces Delaware's position in the venture capital market. Investors frequently prefer Delaware corporations because their legal structures are well understood, while startups anticipating institutional financing often incorporate in Delaware to accommodate those expectations.

Although Delaware incorporation is not legally required to obtain venture capital, companies incorporated elsewhere may be asked to reorganize as Delaware corporations before completing an institutional financing.

## 5. The Continuing Evolution of Delaware Corporate Law

Delaware's corporate law continues to evolve through judicial decisions and legislative amendments.

In March 2025, Delaware enacted significant amendments to Sections 144 and 220 of the DGCL. The changes established additional statutory safe harbors for certain transactions involving interested directors, officers, and controlling stockholders, and revised the framework governing stockholder inspection of corporate books and records.

These developments illustrate how Delaware's legislature responds to changes in corporate practice and judicial doctrine. They also demonstrate why attorneys must consider recent statutory amendments rather than relying exclusively on established case law.

## Conclusion

Delaware's prominence as a state of incorporation reflects the combined advantages of its flexible corporate statute, specialized judiciary, developed case law, and familiarity among investors and transactional attorneys.

These features are particularly valuable for companies anticipating institutional investment, where sophisticated equity structures and governance arrangements are common.

Understanding Delaware's appeal ultimately requires understanding how its statutes, judicial doctrines, and transactional practices work together. That legal infrastructure explains why Delaware remains the preferred jurisdiction for many emerging and established corporations.
