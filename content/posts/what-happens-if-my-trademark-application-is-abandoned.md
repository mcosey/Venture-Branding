Suppose you filed a federal trademark application several months ago and assumed it was progressing toward registration. When you check its status, however, you discover that the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) has marked the application as abandoned.

Does this mean you have lost your trademark rights? Can the application be recovered, or will you need to start over?

Depending on the reason for abandonment and the applicable deadlines, an abandoned application may be eligible for revival or reinstatement.

## What Does It Mean When a Trademark Application Is Abandoned?

An abandoned trademark application is no longer active and cannot proceed to registration unless it is restored.

However, abandonment does not necessarily extinguish trademark rights independently established through commercial use. A business that has used its trademark for several years may retain common-law trademark rights even after its federal application becomes abandoned.

Abandonment of an application should therefore be distinguished from legal abandonment of the underlying trademark.

For additional background, see [*Can I Trademark a Name That Someone Else Has Abandoned?*](can-i-trademark-a-name-that-someone-else-has-abandoned.html)

## Why Do Trademark Applications Become Abandoned?

Common reasons for trademark application abandonment include:

- **Failure to respond to an Office Action:** The applicant misses the deadline to respond to a refusal or requirement.

- **Failure to respond to a suspension inquiry:** The applicant does not provide information requested by the USPTO concerning a suspended application.

- **Failure to satisfy intent-to-use requirements:** The applicant does not timely submit a Statement of Use or request an extension.

- **Incomplete responses:** The applicant submits a response that fails to satisfy applicable procedural requirements.

- **Voluntary abandonment:** The applicant expressly abandons the application.

Abandonment may also affect only part of an application. For example, if an unanswered refusal expressly concerns particular goods or services, those goods or services may be deleted while the remainder of the application continues.

The reason for abandonment determines which procedural remedies may be available.

## Can I Revive an Abandoned Trademark Application?

Under federal trademark regulations, an applicant may file a **Petition to Revive** an application abandoned because of an unintentional failure to respond to an Office Action or satisfy certain intent-to-use filing requirements.

A petition generally requires:

- A signed statement from someone with firsthand knowledge confirming that the delay was unintentional.

- Payment of the applicable USPTO petition fee.

- Submission of outstanding responses or other required documents, when applicable.

Additional requirements depend on the reason for abandonment and whether the applicant received the relevant USPTO correspondence.

If the petition is granted, the application becomes active again, and examination generally resumes where it left off.

Revival does not eliminate outstanding substantive refusals. For example, if the USPTO previously refused registration because of a likelihood of confusion with an existing trademark, that refusal must still be resolved.

## How Long Do I Have to Revive an Abandoned Application?

An applicant who receives a Notice of Abandonment generally must file a Petition to Revive within two months of the notice’s issuance date.

If the applicant did not receive the notice, the petition generally must be filed:

- Within two months of the date the applicant actually learned of the abandonment; and

- No later than six months after the abandonment date shown in the USPTO’s electronic records.

The applicant must also provide the required declaration concerning nonreceipt of the notice.

Partial abandonment may involve different notices and procedural requirements. Relief outside ordinary petition deadlines may be available in extraordinary circumstances, but such requests are rarely granted.

Applicants are responsible for monitoring their applications and maintaining current correspondence information with the USPTO. Failing to receive a notice does not provide an unlimited opportunity to recover an application.

## What If My Intent-to-Use Application Was Abandoned?

Applications filed on an intent-to-use basis under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act involve additional deadlines.

After the USPTO issues a Notice of Allowance, the applicant generally has six months to submit a Statement of Use or request an extension.

Eligible applicants may obtain up to five additional six-month extensions, allowing a maximum of 36 months from issuance of the Notice of Allowance to submit a Statement of Use.

An applicant who unintentionally misses an intermediate deadline may be eligible for revival. However, a Petition to Revive cannot extend the statutory 36-month period under the existing Notice of Allowance.

In certain circumstances, an applicant who did not receive the Notice of Allowance may request that the USPTO cancel the original notice and issue a new one.

An attorney reviewing an abandoned intent-to-use application must therefore consider both the applicable petition deadlines and the statutory Statement of Use requirements.

## What If the USPTO Abandoned My Application by Mistake?

If an application was abandoned because of a USPTO error, the applicant may be eligible for reinstatement.

For example, an applicant may have submitted a timely response that the USPTO failed to associate with the application.

Filing confirmations, payment records, and other documentation may help establish that an administrative error occurred.

Unlike a Petition to Revive, a request for reinstatement based on USPTO error does not carry a USPTO fee under the applicable regulation.

Other circumstances, such as an allegedly improper finding that an Office Action response was incomplete, may require a separate Petition to the Director.

Reinstatement and other petition procedures are also subject to filing deadlines.

## Should I Revive My Application or File a New One?

Filing a new application may seem simpler than pursuing revival. However, starting over can have consequences for **trademark priority**.

Under Section 7(c) of the Lanham Act, an application that results in registration on the Principal Register can establish nationwide constructive-use priority for the registered goods or services dating back to the application’s filing date, subject to statutory exceptions.

Consider the following example:

A business files an intent-to-use trademark application in January. In April, another business files an application for a confusingly similar trademark covering related goods.

The first business subsequently misses an Office Action deadline, and its application becomes abandoned.

If the original application is successfully revived, the business generally preserves its January filing date. If it instead files a new application in September, it receives a later filing date, potentially affecting its priority relative to the competing applicant.

Any independently established trademark rights held by either business must also be considered.

When evaluating revival and refiling, attorneys may consider the significance of the original filing date, outstanding refusals, government fees, changes to the applicant’s business, and eligibility for procedural relief.

Filing a new application does not necessarily resolve the legal issues that affected the original one.

## Can I Continue Using My Trademark After My Application Is Abandoned?

Abandonment of a federal trademark application does not, by itself, make continued use of the trademark unlawful.

However, the circumstances surrounding abandonment may raise separate legal concerns.

For example, if the USPTO refused registration because of a likelihood of confusion with an existing trademark, abandoning the application does not resolve a potential conflict with the other trademark owner’s rights.

The ability to lawfully use a trademark and eligibility to obtain federal registration are separate legal questions.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Evaluate an Abandoned Application?

An attorney reviewing an abandoned application may examine:

- The reason for abandonment and the relevant dates.

- The application’s filing basis and procedural history.

- USPTO correspondence and evidence of prior submissions.

- Applicable deadlines and available petition procedures.

- Outstanding substantive refusals or requirements.

- Potential consequences for trademark priority.

The USPTO’s Trademark Status and Document Retrieval (TSDR) system provides access to application records and correspondence that can assist in this evaluation.

These records help attorneys assess whether the original application may be restored and the implications of pursuing a new application instead.

## The Bottom Line

An abandoned trademark application may be recoverable through revival, reinstatement, or another applicable petition procedure.

When restoration is unavailable, filing a new application may remain an option. However, doing so can affect trademark priority and leave unresolved legal issues from the original application.

Timely evaluation is particularly important because procedural deadlines may determine whether the applicant can preserve the original application and its filing date.
