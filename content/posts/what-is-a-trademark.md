Let's say you've just started a company. You've picked a name, formed an LLC, purchased the domain, and begun building your website.

Naturally, you might assume the name belongs to you.

But not necessarily.

Registering an LLC doesn't automatically give you trademark rights in your company's name. Neither does purchasing a domain. And even if no one else appears to be using the name, that doesn't necessarily mean you're free to use it.

That's where trademark law comes in.

## What Exactly Is a Trademark?

Simply put, **a trademark is something that identifies your business as the source of particular goods or services.**

Trademarks most commonly take the form of words, names, logos, and slogans. However, they can also include less conventional identifiers, such as certain sounds, colors, or even product designs.

The important thing isn't necessarily what the trademark looks like. It's what the trademark *does*: help consumers identify and distinguish the source of goods or services.

Consider a fictional startup called Bright Path Labs LLC, which develops a productivity app named TempoGarden.

Although Bright Path Labs is the company's legal name, TempoGarden might be the name customers associate with its product.

In that situation, TempoGarden could function as a trademark, provided it's used in a way that identifies the source of the app.

This is the basic purpose of trademark law: allowing consumers to recognize whose products or services they're purchasing.

## What Does a Trademark Actually Protect?

Here's something that surprises many founders: **having trademark rights doesn't mean you own a word or phrase in every possible context.**

Trademark protection generally concerns the use of a mark in connection with particular goods or services.

For example, two unrelated businesses operating in different industries might be able to use identical names without infringing on each other's trademark rights.

Whether that use creates a legal problem generally depends on the likelihood that consumers would be confused about the source, sponsorship, or affiliation of the businesses' goods or services.

That's why trademark analysis involves more than checking whether someone else is using your exact name.

Even a different name could create a problem if it's sufficiently similar to an existing mark and is used in connection with related goods or services.

## What Makes a Strong Trademark?

Not all trademarks receive the same level of legal protection.

Some names are inherently more distinctive than others.

For example, Apple is an ordinary English word, but it doesn't inherently describe computers. When used for computers, it's considered an *arbitrary trademark*.

By contrast, a name that merely describes what a product does may be more difficult to protect.

Trademark law generally places marks along a spectrum of distinctiveness:

- **Fanciful:** Invented words created to function as trademarks.

- **Arbitrary:** Existing words used in an unrelated context.

- **Suggestive:** Names that require some imagination to connect them to the product or service.

- **Descriptive:** Names that directly describe a characteristic, quality, or feature of the product or service.

- **Generic:** Common names for the product or service itself, which cannot function as trademarks for those goods or services.

Fanciful, arbitrary, and suggestive marks are generally considered inherently distinctive. Descriptive marks may acquire protection through sufficient use and consumer recognition.

For founders, this creates a practical consideration. A descriptive name might immediately communicate what your product does, but a more distinctive name may be easier to protect.

Of course, even a highly distinctive name could conflict with someone else's existing trademark rights. Distinctiveness and availability are two different questions.

## Do I Have to Register My Trademark?

No.

In the United States, trademark rights can arise through actual use of a mark in commerce, even without federal registration. These are commonly called *common law trademark rights*.

However, common law rights can be geographically limited and may be more difficult to establish or enforce.

Federal registration with the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) provides several important benefits, including:

- A legal presumption of ownership and the exclusive right to use the registered mark nationwide for the identified goods or services, subject to applicable limitations.

- Public notice of your claim through the federal trademark register.

- The ability to use the ® symbol with your registered mark for the goods or services covered by the registration.

- Additional legal remedies and enforcement advantages.

You may also see businesses using the ™ or ℠ symbols. Unlike ®, these symbols can be used to indicate a claimed trademark or service mark even without federal registration.

For businesses intending to serve customers throughout the United States, federal registration can be particularly valuable.

## Isn't Registering My LLC Name Enough?

No. And this is an important distinction for anyone starting a business.

Registering an LLC or corporation with your state generally establishes the legal entity and its approved name under state naming rules. It does not necessarily establish that you have the right to use that name as a trademark.

The same is true of purchasing a domain name or securing a social media handle.

Returning to our earlier example, Bright Path Labs LLC might successfully register its entity name and purchase tempogarden.com, only to discover that another business already has superior rights in a confusingly similar mark. The company could then face the expensive prospect of rebranding after investing time and money into developing its product.

This is why trademark clearance should be considered before committing substantial resources to a new brand.

## Can I File for a Trademark Before Launching My Business?

Yes.

Federal trademark law permits applicants with a *bona fide intent to use* a mark in commerce to file an **intent-to-use trademark application**.

This can be particularly helpful for startup founders who have selected a product name but haven't launched their business yet.

An intent-to-use application allows a founder to begin the federal registration process before actual commercial use, provided the applicant genuinely intends to use the mark for the specified goods or services.

However, an intent-to-use application generally cannot mature into a registration until the applicant satisfies the applicable use requirements and submits the required evidence.

Filing early can also provide important priority benefits once the mark registers, subject to existing rights and other legal requirements.

## What's the Difference Between Trademark Clearance and Registration?

Although they're related, trademark clearance and trademark registration serve different purposes.

**Trademark clearance** involves investigating whether your proposed mark may conflict with existing trademark rights.

**Trademark registration** involves applying for federal protection of a mark through the USPTO.

And here's the critical distinction: **filing a trademark application doesn't necessarily mean you're legally entitled to use the mark.**

The USPTO examines applications for registrability, but existing trademark rights can extend beyond what's listed in its federal database. Someone may have enforceable rights in a mark even without registering it federally.

Consequently, a meaningful clearance analysis may involve examining federal and state trademark records, existing businesses, marketplace usage, and other relevant sources.

For founders preparing to invest substantially in a brand, this is an important step that shouldn't be overlooked.

## What Should I Do Before Choosing a Business or Product Name?

Before committing to a name, consider the following:

1. **Evaluate distinctiveness.** Does the name actually distinguish your product, or does it merely describe what you're selling?

2. **Investigate potential conflicts.** Don't rely exclusively on domain availability or an exact-match USPTO search.

3. **Identify what you're protecting.** Is the name associated with your company, a particular product, or a service?

4. **Consider your launch timeline.** If you haven't begun using the mark, an intent-to-use application may be appropriate.

5. **Consider professional clearance.** An experienced trademark attorney can help evaluate potential conflicts and develop an appropriate filing strategy.

Trademark protection is worth considering early, particularly when you're preparing to invest in product development, branding, advertising, and customer acquisition.

After all, the more successful your brand becomes, the more disruptive an unexpected naming conflict can be.

Understanding trademarks before you launch can help you make more informed decisions about the identity you're building.
