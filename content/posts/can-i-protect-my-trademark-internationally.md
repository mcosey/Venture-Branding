Suppose you've built a successful business in the United States and registered its name with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). As your business grows, you begin attracting customers in other countries.

Does your federal trademark registration protect your brand in those markets?

Generally, no. Trademark rights are territorial, meaning their legal protection is tied to particular countries or jurisdictions. A U.S. trademark registration provides important protections within the United States, but those protections do not automatically extend worldwide.

## Does My U.S. Trademark Registration Protect Me in Other Countries?

Federal trademark registration generally provides protection throughout the United States and its territories, subject to applicable limitations and earlier rights. It does not automatically grant enforceable trademark rights in other countries.

For example, imagine a U.S. company sells outdoor equipment under the registered trademark "Summit Forge." The company later expands into Europe and discovers another business using the same name for similar products.

Its U.S. registration would not, by itself, establish that the American company has superior trademark rights in the foreign market. Those rights would depend on the applicable laws and circumstances, including whether the company has obtained protection in the relevant jurisdiction.

This distinction is particularly relevant for online businesses. A website may be accessible worldwide, but accessibility alone does not establish worldwide trademark rights.

## Can I Register the Same Trademark in Multiple Countries?

Yes. Businesses may seek trademark protection in multiple jurisdictions, although registration requirements vary.

A trademark registered in the United States may also qualify for protection elsewhere. However, the USPTO's decision to register a mark does not require foreign trademark authorities to reach the same conclusion.

A name that is registrable in the United States may face obstacles abroad, including conflicting rights held by another business.

International trademark protection can involve separate national or regional applications or international filing mechanisms designed to simplify applications in participating jurisdictions.

Regardless of the method, protection remains subject to the applicable legal requirements.

## What Is the Madrid Protocol?

The Madrid Protocol is an international treaty that provides a centralized system for seeking trademark protection in multiple participating jurisdictions.

Administered by the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO), the Madrid System allows eligible trademark owners to request protection in multiple jurisdictions through a streamlined application process.

For example, a U.S. business with a qualifying USPTO trademark application or registration may be eligible to use the system to seek protection in participating countries.

Importantly, the Madrid Protocol does not create a single trademark registration granting enforceable rights worldwide. Each designated jurisdiction applies its own laws and determines whether protection is available.

An international registration also generally depends on the underlying application or registration in the country of origin during its first five years, which can affect the protection sought abroad.

The Madrid System simplifies certain administrative aspects of international trademark protection without eliminating differences among national trademark laws.

## What Happens If Someone Uses My Trademark in Another Country?

Discovering another business using an identical or similar trademark abroad does not necessarily mean it is infringing your U.S. trademark rights.

Because trademark rights are territorial, the legal analysis depends partly on where the commercial activity occurs and which laws govern the dispute.

For instance, a business operating exclusively in another country may not be subject to a U.S. trademark infringement claim merely because its name resembles a federally registered U.S. trademark.

In *Abitron Austria GmbH v. Hetronic International, Inc.* (2023), the U.S. Supreme Court held that the federal trademark infringement provisions at issue extend only to claims involving domestic infringing use in commerce.

The decision underscores the geographic limitations of U.S. trademark law. Determining whether particular conduct falls within those limitations requires examining the circumstances of the alleged infringement.

Disputes involving trademark rights under foreign law may require assistance from counsel qualified in the relevant jurisdiction.

## What Should U.S. Businesses Consider When Expanding Internationally?

The relevance of international trademark protection depends largely on where a business operates and where it plans to expand.

A company selling exclusively within the United States may face different concerns than one preparing to enter several foreign markets.

When evaluating international trademark protection, attorneys may consider:

- **Current markets:** Where the business presently sells goods or provides services.

- **Expansion plans:** Which foreign markets are commercially important.

- **Existing rights:** Whether the business has obtained trademark protection outside the United States.

- **Potential conflicts:** Whether similar marks are being used or registered in relevant markets.

- **Commercial priorities:** How additional protection fits the business's plans and resources.

Timing may also matter. Trademark rights and registration priorities vary among jurisdictions, potentially affecting a business's ability to protect its brand in new markets.

These considerations help attorneys assess whether additional protection may be appropriate and which jurisdictions warrant further review.

## Can a U.S. Trademark Attorney Help With International Protection?

A U.S. trademark attorney can help businesses understand the scope of their federal trademark rights and evaluate how international expansion may affect their branding concerns.

This may include reviewing existing U.S. applications and registrations, discussing international filing mechanisms at a general level, and identifying matters that may warrant foreign legal advice.

When a matter requires analysis of another country's trademark laws, assistance from attorneys qualified in that jurisdiction may be necessary.

U.S. and foreign counsel may also coordinate when a business seeks protection in multiple markets.

## The Bottom Line

A U.S. federal trademark registration provides important domestic protections but does not automatically protect a brand internationally.

Businesses entering foreign markets may need additional trademark protection, depending on the jurisdictions involved and their commercial activities.

Understanding the geographic limits of trademark rights helps businesses anticipate potential branding concerns as they expand beyond the United States.
