Suppose you’ve developed a successful brand and another company offers to purchase it. You’re interested in selling the brand but intend to continue operating the rest of your business.

Can you transfer ownership of the trademark without selling your entire company?

Yes. Trademarks are intellectual property assets that can be sold or otherwise transferred. However, federal trademark law imposes certain requirements on these transactions, particularly concerning the goodwill associated with the trademark.

## What Is a Trademark Assignment?

A trademark assignment is a transfer of trademark ownership from one party to another. The party transferring ownership is known as the assignor, while the party receiving ownership is known as the assignee.

Trademark assignments commonly occur in connection with:

- Business and asset acquisitions.

- Sales of individual brands or product lines.

- Transfers between founders and their business entities.

- Corporate reorganizations.

Both federally registered trademarks and trademarks protected through common-law rights may be assigned.

An assignment differs from a trademark license. Under a licensing arrangement, the trademark owner permits another party to use the mark while retaining ownership.

Not every business acquisition requires a trademark assignment. For example, if someone purchases all the shares of a corporation that owns a trademark, the corporation generally remains the trademark owner despite the change in its shareholders.

## Why Must Goodwill Be Transferred With a Trademark?

Goodwill refers to the commercial reputation and customer recognition that a business develops through its operations. A trademark symbolizes the goodwill associated with the goods or services offered under that mark.

Under Section 10 of the Lanham Act, a trademark assignment must include the goodwill associated with the mark.

A purported transfer of a trademark without its associated goodwill is known as an *assignment in gross* and is invalid.

This requirement helps prevent consumers from being misled when a familiar trademark changes ownership. Including language in an assignment agreement stating that goodwill has been transferred does not necessarily establish that an effective transfer has occurred.

A notable example arose in *Sugar Busters LLC v. Brennan* (1999).

The company behind a popular diet book acquired a registered service mark from a retail business selling products and supplies for individuals with diabetes. The company subsequently attempted to enforce the acquired mark against the publishers of a competing book.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Fifth Circuit found that publishing a diet book was not sufficiently similar to operating a retail store selling diabetic supplies to preserve the goodwill associated with the acquired mark.

The court concluded that the assignment was invalid.

## Can I Sell My Trademark Without Selling My Entire Business?

Yes. A business may transfer an individual trademark and its associated goodwill while retaining its other brands and operations.

For example, a clothing company operating three distinct brands may sell one brand to another manufacturer while continuing to operate the remaining two.

Depending on the circumstances, the transaction might include:

- Product designs and specifications.

- Manufacturing arrangements.

- Customer relationships.

- Inventory and branded packaging.

- Websites, domain names, and marketing materials.

Transferring physical assets is not necessarily required for a valid assignment. Courts may examine whether the goods or services offered by the new owner are sufficiently similar to those previously associated with the mark to preserve its goodwill and avoid misleading consumers.

The appropriate assets to include in an assignment depend on the nature of the business and the trademark being transferred.

## Can I Transfer a Pending Trademark Application?

Generally, yes. However, additional restrictions apply to applications filed on an intent-to-use basis under Section 1(b) of the Lanham Act.

An intent-to-use application allows an applicant to seek federal trademark registration before beginning qualifying commercial use of the mark.

Under Section 10 of the Lanham Act, an intent-to-use application generally cannot be assigned until the applicant files an Amendment to Allege Use or a Statement of Use.

An exception permits an assignment to a successor to an existing and ongoing business, or the relevant portion of that business, to which the trademark pertains.

These restrictions are intended to prevent trafficking in trademarks that have not yet been commercially used.

Suppose a founder files an intent-to-use application personally before establishing a limited liability company. The founder subsequently forms the company and wishes to transfer the application to it.

Whether the transfer is permissible may depend on whether the company succeeds to an existing and ongoing business associated with the proposed trademark. Forming a company or owning all of its membership interests does not, by itself, establish that the exception applies.

An assignment that violates these restrictions can invalidate the application and any resulting registration.

## How Do I Transfer Ownership of a Trademark?

Section 10 of the Lanham Act requires assignments of federally registered trademarks and trademarks subject to pending federal applications to be executed in writing.

A trademark assignment agreement will generally identify:

- The current and new trademark owners.

- The trademarks and associated applications or registrations being transferred.

- The trademark rights and associated goodwill being conveyed.

- The effective date of the transfer.

For trademarks subject to pending federal applications or existing federal registrations, the assignment should generally be recorded with the U.S. Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) through its Assignment Center.

Recordation provides a public record of the transfer and helps document the trademark’s chain of title, which is its ownership history.

Under Section 10, an assignment is void against a subsequent purchaser for value without notice of the earlier transfer unless the assignment is recorded within three months of the assignment date or before the subsequent purchase.

USPTO recordation does not establish that an assignment is legally valid. Additionally, recordation does not always automatically update the ownership information displayed in the USPTO’s trademark database.

Following recordation, the parties should verify that the ownership information displayed in the Trademark Status and Document Retrieval (TSDR) system accurately reflects the transfer.

## Does the New Owner Inherit the Trademark’s Existing Rights?

A valid assignment generally allows the new owner to succeed to the trademark rights conveyed through the transaction, including the previous owner’s established priority.

For example, if a business has established trademark rights through ten years of continuous commercial use, a valid assignment may allow the new owner to rely on that earlier use when establishing priority against another business.

An assignment does not expand the scope of the existing trademark rights or restart the maintenance deadlines associated with a federal registration. The new owner remains responsible for maintaining the registration and preserving the underlying trademark rights.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Evaluate a Trademark Transfer?

When evaluating a proposed trademark assignment, an attorney may consider:

- **Ownership:** Whether the proposed assignor owns the trademark rights being transferred.

- **Chain of title:** Whether previous ownership transfers were legally effective.

- **Goodwill:** Whether the transaction conveys the goodwill associated with the trademark.

- **Application status:** Whether restrictions on assigning an intent-to-use application apply.

- **Existing agreements:** Whether licenses, security interests, or contractual restrictions affect the proposed transfer.

- **Documentation:** Whether the assignment agreement and applicable USPTO records accurately reflect the transfer.

These considerations help attorneys evaluate whether the transaction will effectively convey the intended trademark rights.

## The Bottom Line

A trademark can be sold or transferred without selling the entire business that owns it. However, a valid trademark assignment requires the transfer of its associated goodwill.

Intent-to-use applications are subject to additional assignment restrictions, making the timing and structure of certain ownership transfers particularly important.

Proper documentation and timely USPTO recordation can help protect the new owner’s interests, but neither substitutes for a legally valid transfer of the underlying trademark rights.
