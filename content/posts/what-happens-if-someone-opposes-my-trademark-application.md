After months of waiting, your trademark application has finally been approved by the United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO). The examining attorney has completed the review, and your mark is scheduled for publication.

But approval for publication does not mean your trademark has been registered.

Before most marks can be registered on the Principal Register, they must be published in the USPTO's *Trademark Official Gazette* . Publication gives third parties an opportunity to challenge the proposed registration.

If someone believes your trademark should not be registered, they may initiate a legal proceeding known as a *trademark opposition* .

## What Is the Trademark Official Gazette?

The Trademark Official Gazette is a weekly electronic publication maintained by the USPTO.

When an examining attorney approves an application for publication, the proposed trademark appears in the Gazette, allowing other businesses and trademark owners to learn about marks seeking federal registration.

Publication begins a **30-day opposition period** during which eligible third parties may challenge the application.

This process provides an opportunity for objections that may not have arisen during the USPTO's examination.

For example, an examining attorney generally searches federal trademark records for conflicting marks. However, a business may possess earlier common-law trademark rights that do not appear in those records.

That business may have grounds to oppose a later application even if the examining attorney approved it for publication.

Publication is not registration. The application must still satisfy any remaining requirements before a federal registration can issue. The ordinary pre-registration opposition procedure applies to marks seeking registration on the Principal Register. Marks on the Supplemental Register are not published for opposition before registration, although their registrations may be subject to cancellation proceedings.

## What Is a Trademark Opposition?

A trademark opposition is a formal proceeding in which a third party challenges another party's application for federal trademark registration.

Oppositions are heard by the *Trademark Trial and Appeal Board* (TTAB), an administrative tribunal within the USPTO.

Under Section 13 of the Lanham Act, a party that believes it would be damaged by registration may oppose an application on legally recognized grounds.

Consider a hypothetical software company seeking to register BRIGHTPATH for project management software.

Another business has been using a similar mark, BRIGHT PATH, for closely related software services.

The earlier business believes registration of BRIGHTPATH would conflict with its existing trademark rights.

Depending on the circumstances, that business may be entitled to oppose the application based on a likelihood of consumer confusion.

The opposing party does not necessarily need a federal trademark registration. Earlier common-law rights may provide a basis for opposition.

However, simply disliking a proposed trademark is insufficient. An opposer must establish its legal entitlement to bring the proceeding and a recognized ground for refusing registration.

## Why Would Someone Oppose a Trademark?

One of the most common grounds for opposition is *likelihood of confusion* .

An existing trademark owner may argue that a proposed mark is sufficiently similar to its own, and that the relevant goods or services are sufficiently related, that consumers could mistakenly believe the businesses are connected.

Other potential grounds include:

- **Descriptiveness or genericness:** The proposed mark may not qualify for registration because it merely describes the goods or services without sufficient acquired distinctiveness, or identifies a category of goods or services rather than their commercial source.

- **Lack of bona fide intent to use:** An intent-to-use applicant may have lacked the required bona fide intention to use the mark for particular goods or services when the application was filed.

- **Dilution:** In appropriate circumstances, the owner of a qualifying famous mark may challenge registration based on a likelihood of dilution.

- **Other statutory grounds:** An opposition may involve additional objections recognized under federal trademark law.

Each claim requires legal and factual support.

The filing of an opposition does not establish that the allegations are correct or that the applicant's mark is unregistrable.

## How Long Does Someone Have to Oppose a Trademark?

The initial opposition period lasts **30 days from publication** in the Official Gazette.

During that period, a potential opposer may file a formal Notice of Opposition or request additional time to oppose.

An initial 30-day extension is generally available upon timely request without a government filing fee.

Longer extensions may be available, but they are subject to additional requirements, including applicable fees, a showing of good cause, the applicant's consent, or extraordinary circumstances.

Under USPTO rules, the opposition deadline cannot be extended beyond 180 days from the original publication date.

Importantly, **requesting an extension does not mean an opposition has been filed** .

A business might request additional time to investigate a potential conflict, assess its legal position, or discuss the matter with the applicant.

An extension signals a possible challenge, but a formal dispute does not begin unless a Notice of Opposition is filed.

## What Happens If Someone Files an Opposition?

Filing a Notice of Opposition initiates a contested proceeding before the TTAB. Although the TTAB is part of the USPTO, an opposition differs substantially from ordinary examination by a trademark examining attorney.

The proceeding resembles civil litigation, involving pleadings, deadlines, discovery, evidence, and legal argument.

### 1. The Notice of Opposition

The opposing party files a document identifying the application being challenged and stating the grounds for opposition.

For example, an opposer alleging likelihood of confusion may claim earlier trademark rights and explain why registration of the applicant's mark would conflict with those rights.

The TTAB institutes the proceeding and establishes a schedule for the parties.

### 2. The Applicant's Answer

The applicant ordinarily responds through a formal pleading called an *answer* , admitting or denying the relevant allegations and asserting any appropriate defenses.

Failure to answer by the applicable deadline can have serious consequences.

The TTAB may issue a notice of default, giving the applicant an opportunity to show good cause why default judgment should not be entered.

If the applicant fails to establish good cause, the Board may enter default judgment, preventing registration of the challenged mark.

### 3. Discovery and Evidence

If the proceeding continues, the parties may exchange information and documents through *discovery* .

Depending on the issues, the evidence may concern trademark priority, commercial use, the relationship between goods or services, consumer perceptions, or other relevant matters.

The parties may then submit evidence and legal arguments during the TTAB's trial and briefing stages.

Unlike a conventional courtroom trial, TTAB proceedings are generally decided on a written evidentiary record, although an oral hearing may be requested.

These proceedings can therefore be considerably more involved than responding to a USPTO office action.

## Can a Trademark Opposition Be Resolved Without a Trial?

Yes. Not every opposition proceeds to a final contested decision.

Depending on the circumstances, the parties may resolve their disagreement through negotiation or settlement.

For example, a dispute involving similar marks used with related products might be resolved through an agreed limitation of the goods or services identified in an application.

In other cases, the parties may negotiate a coexistence agreement addressing their respective uses of the marks.

Some disputes result in an applicant withdrawing its application or an opposer withdrawing its challenge.

Substantive amendments to applications involved in opposition proceedings generally require TTAB approval, together with the other party's consent or an appropriate Board ruling.

Settlement is not guaranteed, and an agreement between the parties does not necessarily resolve every potential registration issue.

Because oppositions can involve discovery, motions, and other litigation procedures, their costs and duration can be significant.

## What Does the TTAB Actually Decide?

The TTAB's authority is narrower than that of a court hearing a trademark infringement lawsuit.

In an opposition, the TTAB determines whether the challenged mark is entitled to federal registration.

If the opposer succeeds, registration may be refused for the challenged goods or services.

If the applicant prevails, the application may proceed, subject to any remaining registration requirements.

The TTAB does not ordinarily determine whether a party has the right to continue using its mark in the marketplace. It also cannot award damages or issue an injunction prohibiting trademark use.

Those remedies generally require an appropriate court proceeding. Accordingly, filing an opposition does not automatically require the applicant to stop using its trademark. However, the underlying dispute may involve separate allegations of infringement or other legal claims.

## What Happens If Nobody Opposes the Application?

If the opposition period expires without a challenge, or any opposition is resolved in the applicant's favor, the application generally proceeds to the next stage.

What happens next depends on the filing basis.

**Use-Based Applications**

For applications based on qualifying use in commerce under Section 1(a), the USPTO generally proceeds toward registration once the remaining requirements are satisfied.

**Intent-to-Use Applications**

For applications filed under Section 1(b), the USPTO generally issues a *Notice of Allowance* .

The applicant must then satisfy the applicable use requirements, typically by submitting a Statement of Use and an acceptable trademark specimen within the required timeframe.

A Notice of Allowance is not itself a registration.

## The Bottom Line

Approval for publication is an important milestone, but it does not guarantee federal trademark registration.

Publication in the Official Gazette gives eligible third parties an opportunity to challenge a proposed mark before registration.

An opposition may involve significant legal questions, including trademark priority, likelihood of confusion, and whether the proposed mark otherwise qualifies for registration.

Some disputes are resolved through negotiation. Others develop into formal proceedings before the Trademark Trial and Appeal Board.
