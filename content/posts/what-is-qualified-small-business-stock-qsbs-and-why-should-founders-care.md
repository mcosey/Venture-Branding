Imagine starting a company, spending years building it, and eventually selling your shares for $10 million. Ordinarily, much of that gain could be subject to federal capital gains taxes. But under certain circumstances, you might owe no federal income tax on the gain at all.

That possibility exists because of qualified small business stock, commonly called *QSBS*.

Under Section 1202 of the Internal Revenue Code, eligible shareholders can exclude some or all of the gains from selling qualifying stock. For successful founders and early investors, the potential tax savings can reach millions of dollars.

Qualifying requires more than simply owning shares in a startup. The company's structure, business activities, stock issuance history, and the length of time the shares are held all matter.

## What Is Qualified Small Business Stock?

Qualified small business stock generally refers to shares issued by an eligible domestic C corporation that satisfies certain requirements under federal tax law.

Congress created the QSBS exclusion to encourage investment in smaller businesses by providing favorable tax treatment to qualifying shareholders.

The exclusion generally applies to eligible noncorporate taxpayers, including individuals, who sell qualifying shares after satisfying the applicable holding-period requirements.

For example, suppose a founder purchases shares in a newly formed corporation for $10,000. Several years later, the founder sells those shares for $10 million.

The founder has realized approximately $9.99 million in capital gain.

If the shares qualify for a 100% QSBS exclusion and the gain falls within the applicable statutory limit, the founder may exclude the entire gain from federal income tax.

However, the shares must satisfy specific legal requirements, and favorable federal treatment does not necessarily eliminate state income taxes.

## How Much Can Founders Exclude Under QSBS?

The amount of gain eligible for exclusion depends partly on when the shareholder acquired the stock.

Congress significantly expanded Section 1202 through legislation enacted on July 4, 2025. The changes introduced shorter holding periods, higher exclusion limits, and expanded eligibility for certain companies.

### Stock Acquired on or Before July 4, 2025

For qualifying shares acquired after September 27, 2010, and on or before July 4, 2025, shareholders generally must hold the shares for more than five years to qualify for a 100% federal gain exclusion.

The exclusion is generally limited to the greater of:

- $10 million in eligible gain, subject to reductions for certain prior exclusions and special rules; or

- Ten times the shareholder's qualifying adjusted tax basis in the shares.

Older shares may qualify for lower exclusion percentages.

### Stock Acquired After July 4, 2025

For qualifying shares acquired after July 4, 2025, the law provides a tiered exclusion based on the holding period.

| **Holding Period** | **Federal Gain Exclusion** |
| --- | --- |
| At least 3 years | 50% |
| At least 4 years | 75% |
| At least 5 years | 100% |

The general dollar limitation also increased to $15 million, with inflation adjustments beginning in 2027. The alternative limitation of ten times the shareholder's qualifying tax basis remains available.

These limits generally apply on a per-issuer basis, taking into account applicable rules concerning prior exclusions.

Consider a founder who acquires qualifying shares in August 2025 for $10,000 and eventually realizes a $12 million gain.

If the founder sells after three years, the 50% exclusion could shelter $6 million of the gain from federal income tax. After four years, the 75% exclusion could shelter $9 million. After five years, the 100% exclusion could shelter the full $12 million.

This example assumes that all other QSBS requirements are met and that the applicable exclusion limits have not been exhausted. Any remaining taxable gain may be subject to special federal tax treatment.

The new rules provide greater flexibility for founders whose companies are acquired before reaching the five-year mark.

## What Requirements Must a Company Meet?

Not every startup qualifies for QSBS treatment. Several requirements concern the company itself.

### 1. The Company Must Be a Domestic C Corporation

QSBS generally must be issued by a domestic C corporation.

Stock in an S corporation does not qualify while the company is taxed as an S corporation. Likewise, ownership interests in an LLC taxed as a partnership are not QSBS.

An LLC may convert to a C corporation, potentially allowing shares issued in connection with the conversion to qualify. However, appreciation that occurred before the conversion generally cannot be sheltered by the QSBS exclusion.

This is one reason a company's initial legal structure can have important long-term tax consequences.

### 2. The Company Must Satisfy the Gross Assets Test

The issuing corporation must satisfy a statutory gross assets limitation.

For stock acquired on or before July 4, 2025, the relevant threshold generally was $50 million.

For stock acquired after July 4, 2025, the threshold increased to $75 million, subject to inflation adjustments beginning in 2027.

The corporation's aggregate gross assets must not exceed the applicable threshold before and immediately after the relevant stock issuance.

This test is not based simply on the company's market valuation. It generally measures cash and the adjusted tax basis of other assets, subject to special statutory rules.

A startup valued at $100 million is therefore not necessarily disqualified solely because of that valuation. Conversely, a company may fail the test even when its market valuation is relatively modest.

A corporation that grows beyond the threshold after issuing qualifying stock does not automatically cause previously issued shares to lose QSBS status.

### 3. The Company Must Conduct a Qualified Business

Section 1202 also requires the corporation to satisfy an active business requirement during substantially all of the shareholder's relevant holding period.

Generally, at least 80% of the company's assets, measured by value, must be used in the active conduct of one or more qualified trades or businesses, subject to detailed statutory rules.

Certain businesses are excluded from QSBS eligibility, including specified professional services, financial businesses, farming, mining, hotels, restaurants, and other designated activities.

Many technology and product-oriented businesses may qualify, although eligibility depends on the company's actual activities.

Founders should not assume that a company qualifies simply because it is organized as a C corporation or operates in the technology sector.

## Do Founders Have to Purchase Their Shares Directly From the Company?

Generally, yes.

Section 1202 ordinarily requires shareholders to acquire qualifying stock at its original issuance, either in exchange for money, eligible property, or services provided to the corporation.

For example, a founder who purchases shares directly from a newly formed C corporation may satisfy the original issuance requirement.

By contrast, someone who later purchases those shares from the founder in a private secondary transaction generally does not qualify merely because the shares previously constituted QSBS.

Special exceptions can apply to certain gifts, inheritances, and qualifying exchanges.

The original issuance requirement also matters when employees receive equity compensation. Receiving a stock option does not itself mean the employee owns QSBS. Shares acquired through the exercise of an option may qualify if the applicable requirements are satisfied, but the relevant holding period generally begins when the stock is acquired rather than when the option is granted.

## Can Founders Lose QSBS Eligibility?

Yes. QSBS eligibility depends on more than the company's circumstances when shares are initially issued.

Several situations deserve particular attention.

**Changing the company's business.** A material shift into an excluded business activity may affect whether the active business requirement continues to be satisfied.

**Repurchasing company shares.** Certain stock redemptions occurring around an issuance can disqualify otherwise eligible stock under Section 1202's anti-redemption rules.

**Restructuring the company.** Conversions, reorganizations, and other changes in corporate structure may affect QSBS eligibility, although special rules preserve favorable treatment in some transactions.

**Selling too early.** Shareholders who sell before satisfying the applicable holding period generally cannot claim the Section 1202 exclusion. Section 1045 may permit qualifying shareholders who have held QSBS for more than six months to defer certain gains by reinvesting in replacement QSBS within 60 days, subject to additional requirements.

The consequences depend on the transaction and the applicable statutory rules. Founders should obtain appropriate legal and tax advice before undertaking transactions that could affect their shares' eligibility.

## Does QSBS Apply When a Startup Is Acquired?

Potentially, but the structure of the acquisition matters.

In a stock sale, shareholders sell their shares to the buyer. If those shares qualify as QSBS and the applicable requirements are satisfied, eligible shareholders may claim the exclusion on qualifying gains.

An asset sale is different.

When a C corporation sells its assets, the corporation generally recognizes any taxable gain from that sale. Section 1202 does not eliminate corporate-level income tax merely because the company's shareholders own QSBS.

A subsequent distribution of sale proceeds to shareholders can create additional tax consequences.

Some acquisitions involve exchanges of stock rather than immediate cash payments. Certain transactions may preserve QSBS benefits under special rules, but the outcome depends on the transaction's structure.

For founders anticipating an acquisition, evaluating QSBS treatment should be part of the transaction planning process.

## Does Every State Recognize the QSBS Exclusion?

No.

Section 1202 is a federal income tax provision. State treatment depends on the applicable state's tax laws.

Some states generally follow the federal QSBS exclusion, while others do not.

California, for example, does not generally allow the federal Section 1202 exclusion for California personal income tax purposes.

Consequently, a founder who qualifies for a substantial federal exclusion may still owe state income taxes on the gain.

The shareholder's residency, applicable state tax rules, and circumstances of the transaction can all affect the ultimate tax outcome.

## Why Should Founders Think About QSBS Early?

QSBS eligibility often begins with decisions made at a company's formation.

Consider a founder who establishes a business as an LLC, develops valuable intellectual property, attracts investors, and converts to a C corporation several years later.

Although shares issued in connection with the conversion may subsequently qualify, the founder generally cannot use the QSBS exclusion to shelter appreciation attributable to the company's value before the conversion.

Similarly, a founder who delays acquiring shares may begin the applicable holding period later than expected.

Founders should therefore consider QSBS when deciding how to organize the company, when and how to issue equity, and whether future changes to the business or ownership structure could affect eligibility.

Maintaining records of incorporation, stock issuances, acquisition dates, and relevant corporate transactions can also make it easier to evaluate eligibility when a sale eventually occurs.

## Planning for QSBS

Qualified small business stock can provide one of the most significant federal tax benefits available to eligible startup founders and investors.

The 2025 changes expanded that opportunity by introducing shorter holding periods for partial exclusions, increasing the general dollar limitation, and raising the gross assets threshold.

However, QSBS treatment is not automatic. Eligibility depends on the company's structure, business activities, stock issuance history, holding periods, and applicable statutory limitations.

Founders do not need to predict whether their companies will eventually sell for millions of dollars to benefit from early tax planning. Understanding QSBS during formation, financing, and exit planning can help preserve opportunities that might otherwise be lost.
