*An introduction to venture capital transactions for law students and junior attorneys.*

Imagine two founders who have spent the past year developing a software company. Their product has launched, customers are beginning to sign up, and the founders believe they have an opportunity to grow significantly.

To do so, they'll need capital.

The company wants to hire engineers, improve its product, and expand its customer acquisition efforts. After reviewing their financial projections, the founders decide to raise $3 million from outside investors.

What happens next?

For attorneys beginning to work in emerging companies and venture capital (ECVC), understanding this process provides context for the documents they'll eventually draft and negotiate.

A venture financing involves several related steps, from negotiating investment terms and conducting due diligence to obtaining corporate approvals and closing the transaction.

To understand how those pieces fit together, it helps to follow a financing from beginning to end.

## 1. What Exactly Is a Financing Round?

A financing round is an organized effort by a company to raise capital from investors, generally in exchange for equity or contractual rights to receive equity in the future.

The easiest way to understand a round is to think of it as a fundraising campaign.

Suppose TempoGarden, Inc., a Delaware corporation developing productivity software, determines that it needs $3 million to expand its operations.

The founders approach prospective investors, negotiate investment terms, and seek commitments sufficient to reach that target.

Eventually, three investors agree to participate:

| **Investor** | **Investment** |
| --- | --- |
| Alpha Ventures | $1,500,000 |
| Beta Capital | $1,000,000 |
| Gamma Ventures | $500,000 |
| **Total** | **$3,000,000** |

Together, these investors participate in one financing round.

If the transaction is structured as a priced Series A financing, they would generally purchase shares of Series A Preferred Stock at the same price per share, with the same fundamental rights attached to that series. Individual investors may also negotiate additional contractual rights.

The financing might close on a single date or involve multiple closings.

Eighteen months later, TempoGarden may need another $8 million. The company could then conduct a Series B financing, negotiating new terms with another group of investors. Some Series A investors might participate again, while others might not.

The company would generally issue a new series of preferred stock. Following the Series B, both Series A and Series B Preferred Stock could remain outstanding, each with its respective rights and preferences.

**This is the essential distinction between financing rounds. Series A and Series B generally represent successive capital-raising transactions, each with its own investors, negotiated economics, and securities.**

The labels themselves are conventions rather than legally prescribed financing stages. Companies may also raise capital through pre-seed rounds, seed rounds, extensions, or convertible instruments.

For attorneys, understanding the structure of the particular financing is more important than the label assigned to it.

## 2. Determining the Company's Capital Needs

Before approaching investors, TempoGarden's founders evaluate how much capital the company needs and what they hope to accomplish with it.

They determine that $3 million should provide approximately 18 months of operating runway, allowing the company to hire employees, develop its product, and pursue additional customers.

The founders must also consider how the financing will affect ownership.

That requires examining the company's *capitalization table*, commonly called a *cap table*.

The cap table records the company's equity ownership and other securities that may affect its capital structure. It may include founder shares, employee stock options, outstanding preferred stock, and convertible instruments issued in earlier financings.

Suppose TempoGarden previously raised $500,000 through SAFEs. Depending on their terms, those instruments may convert into equity during the Series A financing.

Company counsel must understand those obligations before determining the number of shares available for issuance and the ownership percentages following closing.

## 3. Choosing a Financing Structure

Not every startup raises capital by immediately selling preferred stock.

Early-stage companies often use convertible instruments, such as SAFEs or convertible notes, before pursuing a priced financing.

A *Simple Agreement for Future Equity (SAFE)* is a contractual arrangement under which an investor provides capital in exchange for rights to receive equity upon specified events. A standard SAFE is not debt and generally has no interest or maturity date.

A *convertible note* is a debt instrument that contemplates conversion into equity under specified circumstances, often during a subsequent financing.

In a *priced equity financing*, investors purchase shares at an agreed price per share. Venture investors commonly receive preferred stock, which carries negotiated economic and governance rights.

These structures differ in their legal characteristics, economics, and documentation.

We will examine them more closely in *Understanding SAFEs and Convertible Notes*, *How Convertible Instruments Convert in a Priced Financing*, and *Understanding Preferred Stock in Venture Financings*.

For now, assume TempoGarden decides to raise its $3 million through a priced Series A Preferred Stock financing.

## 4. Finding Investors and Negotiating a Term Sheet

TempoGarden's founders begin meeting with venture capital funds and other prospective investors.

Eventually, Alpha Ventures expresses interest in leading the financing and proposes investing $1.5 million.

The *lead investor* typically plays a central role in negotiating the principal economic and governance terms of the transaction.

Those negotiations commonly result in a **term sheet**, which summarizes the proposed investment terms before the parties prepare the definitive financing documents.

In a priced equity financing, the term sheet commonly addresses:

- The amount of capital being raised.

- The company's pre-money valuation.

- The type of securities being issued.

- Liquidation preferences and conversion rights.

- Board composition and governance arrangements.

- Investor participation rights in future financings.

- Any contemplated expansion of the employee equity pool.

Term sheets are generally nonbinding concerning the obligation to complete the investment, although provisions addressing confidentiality, exclusivity, expenses, and similar matters may be binding.

Not every investor necessarily negotiates or signs a separate term sheet.

In a typical venture financing, the company and lead investor negotiate the principal terms of the round. Other investors agree to participate on those terms, often executing the same definitive Stock Purchase Agreement, which identifies each investor's investment amount and share allocation.

Investors purchasing the same series generally receive the same fundamental stock rights and pay the same price per share. However, larger investors may negotiate additional contractual rights, including information rights, board designation rights, or other arrangements.

The term sheet establishes the framework for the financing, while the definitive agreements implement the transaction.

## 5. Understanding Valuation and Dilution

One of the principal economic terms in a priced financing is the company's valuation.

Suppose TempoGarden and its investors agree to the following:

| **Term** | **Amount** |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-money valuation | $12 million |
| New investment | $3 million |
| Post-money valuation | $15 million |

The *pre-money valuation* reflects the agreed value of the company before the new investment.

The *post-money valuation* generally equals the pre-money valuation plus the new capital raised.

In a simplified transaction without outstanding convertible securities or other relevant capitalization adjustments, investors contributing $3 million at a $15 million post-money valuation would own 20% of the company after closing.

The existing stockholders would collectively own the remaining 80%.

Their reduction in percentage ownership is called *dilution*.

Actual calculations may be more complicated. Outstanding SAFEs, convertible notes, stock options, and changes to the employee equity pool can affect the price per share and post-closing ownership.

Attorneys therefore rely on detailed capitalization calculations, often incorporated into a financing spreadsheet, to confirm that the securities issued at closing reflect the negotiated economics.

## 6. What Happens to Existing SAFEs and Convertible Notes?

Recall that TempoGarden previously raised $500,000 through SAFEs.

If the Series A qualifies as an equity financing under those agreements, the SAFEs generally convert into shares of preferred stock according to their terms.

Depending on the applicable valuation cap or discount, earlier investors may receive shares at a lower effective price than the new Series A investors.

Convertible notes may undergo a similar conversion, although their debt terms, including accrued interest, can affect the calculation.

The resulting shares must be accounted for in the financing documents and post-closing cap table.

Attorneys should distinguish this process from the conversion rights contained in preferred stock itself.

A SAFE converting into preferred shares during a financing is a different mechanism from preferred stock converting into common stock under its governing terms.

Both are relevant to venture financings, but they arise under different legal instruments.

The mechanics will be examined in *How Convertible Instruments Convert in a Priced Financing*.

## 7. Due Diligence

After the parties agree on the principal terms, the transaction moves into due diligence and definitive documentation.

Investor counsel typically investigates the company's legal and business affairs to identify issues that might affect the investment.

The review may include corporate records, capitalization, intellectual property ownership, employment arrangements, material contracts, outstanding liabilities, and litigation exposure.

For TempoGarden, investors may want to confirm that the company owns the intellectual property underlying its software and that its outstanding shares and convertible securities were properly issued.

Due diligence findings can affect the transaction documents, closing conditions, or even the parties' willingness to proceed.

The scope and mechanics of this process will be addressed in *Due Diligence in Venture Capital Financings*.

## 8. Preparing the Financing Documents

While diligence proceeds, counsel prepares and negotiates the documents needed to implement the round.

In a typical Delaware preferred stock financing, five principal documents commonly form the core transaction package.

**Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation**

The company's charter establishes its capital structure and the rights and preferences of its preferred stock, including liquidation preferences, conversion provisions, and certain investor approval rights.

**Stock Purchase Agreement**

This agreement governs the purchase and sale of the new shares. It identifies the participating investors, purchase price, representations and warranties, closing conditions, and other transaction obligations.

**Investors' Rights Agreement**

This agreement commonly addresses investor information rights, registration rights, participation rights in future financings, and other contractual protections.

**Voting Agreement**

The voting agreement establishes specified arrangements concerning stockholder voting, often including board composition and certain company-sale transactions.

**Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement**

This agreement generally regulates specified transfers of stock by founders and other covered stockholders, including circumstances in which the company or investors may have rights to purchase or participate in a proposed sale.

The [National Venture Capital Association](https://nvca.org/model-legal-documents/) publishes model versions of these documents, which serve as widely used starting points for U.S. venture financings.

Although the documents address different matters, they operate together. Their provisions must reflect the negotiated terms and remain consistent with the company's governing documents and existing contractual obligations.

We'll examine the function and principal provisions of each in *Understanding the Five Principal Venture Financing Documents*.

## 9. Why Do Prior Financing Documents Matter?

A new financing must account for rights established in earlier transactions.

Suppose TempoGarden previously issued Series Seed Preferred Stock before pursuing its Series A.

The Series Seed investors may have negotiated rights to approve certain corporate actions, participate in future financings, or receive specified information.

Those rights may appear in the company's certificate of incorporation, investors' rights agreement, or other existing documents.

Company counsel must review those instruments to determine whether the Series A requires additional approvals, notices, amendments, or waivers.

For example, existing investors may possess contractual participation rights allowing them to purchase a portion of the new securities. If they will not participate, a waiver may be necessary depending on the terms of the agreement.

**The prior financing documents establish existing rights and obligations. They do not necessarily establish the economic terms of the proposed round.**

That distinction is especially important when junior associates are asked to prepare stockholder consents or review participation rights based on documents from an earlier financing.

## 10. Obtaining Corporate Approvals

Before closing, TempoGarden must obtain the corporate approvals required by Delaware law and its governing documents.

The board generally must authorize the issuance of shares and approve the relevant transactions.

The financing may also require an amendment to the certificate of incorporation to authorize or establish the new preferred stock and its rights.

Under the Delaware General Corporation Law, a charter amendment ordinarily requires board and stockholder action, subject to applicable statutory provisions and exceptions.

Additional approval requirements may arise under existing preferred stock protective provisions or contractual arrangements.

Company counsel must therefore determine the applicable voting thresholds for the particular transaction.

Depending on those requirements, counsel may prepare board resolutions, stockholder written consents, preferred stockholder approvals, and waivers of existing investor rights.

These approvals establish the corporate authority needed to complete the financing.

## 11. Closing the Financing

Once the documents are finalized, diligence issues addressed, and closing conditions satisfied, the parties can close.

At a typical closing:

1. The required corporate approvals have been obtained.

1. The amended certificate of incorporation is filed and becomes effective, if required.

1. The parties execute and deliver the definitive agreements.

1. Investors provide the agreed purchase funds.

1. The company issues the shares and records any required conversions of outstanding instruments.

1. Counsel confirms completion of the closing requirements and the company's capitalization records are updated.

The precise order and timing depend on the documents and closing arrangements. For example, signatures and funds may be collected in advance and held pending satisfaction of the closing conditions.

The financing may also permit subsequent closings, allowing additional investors to participate after the initial closing.

Some transactions provide for investments in installments, sometimes called *tranches*, based on specified dates or milestones.

The essential result is that the company receives investment capital and the participating investors acquire the securities and rights contemplated by the financing.

Counsel must also address applicable securities-law requirements.

Venture financings are commonly structured as private offerings relying on exemptions from Securities Act registration. When a company relies on Regulation D, a Form D notice generally must be filed with the SEC within 15 calendar days after the first sale.

For this purpose, the first sale generally occurs when the first investor becomes irrevocably contractually committed to invest, which may precede the transfer of funds. Applicable state securities notice requirements must also be considered.

## 12. What Happens After Closing?

The financing becomes part of TempoGarden's ongoing corporate structure.

Investors may now possess contractual rights to financial information, board representation, participation in later financings, or approval of specified corporate actions.

The company must comply with those obligations and maintain accurate capitalization records.

Meanwhile, the founders use the proceeds to hire employees, develop the product, and pursue their business objectives.

If TempoGarden later needs additional capital, it may conduct another financing round.

That subsequent transaction must account for the securities and investor rights established in the Series A.

## 13. What Are Up Rounds and Down Rounds?

Suppose TempoGarden completes its Series A and returns to investors 18 months later to raise a Series B.

The parties will negotiate a new valuation and price per share.

If the company sells shares at a higher price per share than in its previous priced financing, the transaction is generally called an *up round*.

If the new shares are sold at a lower price per share, it is generally called a *down round*.

A financing at approximately the same price per share is commonly described as a *flat round*.

These classifications generally compare share prices, with appropriate adjustments for stock splits and similar changes, rather than simply comparing headline valuations.

Down rounds can create additional legal complications because outstanding preferred stock may include anti-dilution protections.

Depending on their terms, those provisions can adjust the conversion price of earlier preferred shares when the company issues new securities at a lower price.

The resulting adjustments may affect the ownership percentages of founders, employees, and existing investors.

Down rounds can also raise questions concerning investor approval rights, recapitalizations, and negotiations among different series of stockholders.

These issues will be explored in *Understanding Down Rounds and Anti-Dilution Protection*.

## Putting the Financing Together

A venture financing is easier to understand when viewed as a coordinated transaction rather than a collection of unfamiliar legal documents.

The founders identify a capital need and seek investments sufficient to meet it. The company and its investors negotiate the economics and governance arrangements. Counsel investigates the company's existing obligations, prepares the transaction documents, and obtains the necessary approvals.

At closing, the company receives capital and the investors acquire securities with negotiated rights.

Future financings build upon that existing legal and capital structure.

For junior attorneys, understanding this sequence provides context for the individual assignments that arise during a deal.

A Stock Purchase Agreement makes more sense when you understand the securities being issued and the investors purchasing them. A Voting Agreement makes more sense when you understand the governance arrangements negotiated in the term sheet. And a stockholder consent makes more sense when you understand the approvals necessary to authorize the transaction.

Each document implements a different part of the financing.

Understanding how they fit together is the foundation for developing competence in venture capital transactions.
