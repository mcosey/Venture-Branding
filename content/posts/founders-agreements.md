Starting a business with someone often begins with a shared idea and an understanding of how each person will contribute. But as the business develops, disagreements may emerge over responsibilities, ownership, financial contributions, or major decisions.

A founders' agreement helps establish expectations early, before those disagreements become more difficult to resolve. It can address how ownership will be divided, what each founder is expected to contribute, how decisions will be made, and what happens if someone leaves the business.

## 1. What Is a Founders' Agreement?

A founders' agreement is an agreement among the individuals establishing a business that defines important aspects of their relationship, including their respective rights and responsibilities.

There is no universally prescribed form. Founders may enter into an agreement before forming the company, documenting their intentions concerning ownership, contributions, and management.

Once the company is formed, those arrangements may be implemented through more formal documents. For a corporation, these may include stock purchase agreements, vesting provisions, intellectual property assignments, bylaws, and stockholder agreements. For a limited liability company (LLC), many of these matters may instead be addressed in its operating agreement.

A standalone founders' agreement is not required for every business. What matters is that the founders' arrangements are clearly documented and properly implemented through the appropriate legal agreements.

## 2. Ownership and Equity Allocation

One of the first decisions founders face is how to divide ownership of the company.

An equal division may be appropriate when founders anticipate making comparable contributions. In other circumstances, different allocations may better reflect their responsibilities, capital contributions, prior work, or expected involvement.

Consider two individuals starting a software company. One developed the original technology and will oversee engineering. The other will manage operations, sales, and fundraising. They might agree to split ownership equally or negotiate a different allocation based on their respective contributions.

Whatever arrangement they choose, they should consider both past contributions and anticipated future involvement.

Founders should also distinguish an agreement concerning ownership from the legal issuance of equity. For example, agreeing that each founder will own 50% of a corporation does not necessarily mean that shares have been issued. Issuing stock generally requires appropriate corporate authorization and documentation.

## 3. Founder Vesting

Vesting provisions can protect the company if a founder leaves before making the contributions originally anticipated.

For corporations, founders often receive their shares at formation, subject to a contractual right allowing the company to repurchase unvested shares if the founder departs.

A common arrangement uses a four-year vesting schedule with a one-year cliff.

Suppose a founder receives 400,000 shares subject to this schedule. After the first year, 25% of the shares, or 100,000, vest. The remaining shares typically vest monthly over the following three years.

If the founder leaves after one year, the company may have the right to repurchase the remaining 300,000 unvested shares, generally at the price specified in the stock agreement.

The agreement may also address whether vesting accelerates following an acquisition or certain termination events.

Vesting is particularly important when founders receive substantial ownership interests in anticipation of years of future work. Without appropriate restrictions, a founder who departs early may retain a significant ownership interest despite no longer contributing to the business.

**A related tax consideration is the Section 83(b) election.** Founders who receive restricted stock subject to vesting should promptly obtain tax advice concerning whether an election is appropriate. When available, the election generally must be filed with the IRS within 30 days after the property is transferred. Missing that deadline can have significant tax consequences.

## 4. Roles, Responsibilities, and Time Commitments

Founders should establish what each person is expected to contribute beyond capital.

An agreement may address responsibility for product development, operations, marketing, finances, hiring, and fundraising. It may also identify each founder's expected time commitment.

For example, one founder may work full-time while another contributes part-time until the company obtains financing. That arrangement can work, provided both understand when, or whether, the second founder is expected to transition to full-time involvement.

Founders may also address outside employment, involvement in other businesses, and potential conflicts of interest.

Defining responsibilities early helps establish a common understanding of each founder's role, particularly as the business grows and those roles evolve.

## 5. Decision-Making and Disagreements

Founders should consider how major business decisions will be made and what happens when they disagree.

An agreement might identify who is responsible for ordinary operational decisions and which matters require approval from multiple founders.

For example, one founder might oversee product development while significant expenditures, additional fundraising, or major changes in business strategy require broader agreement.

Decision-making arrangements must also reflect the company's legal structure. In a corporation, management authority generally rests with the board of directors, subject to applicable law and governing documents. A founders' agreement should be coordinated with that structure.

**Deadlock deserves particular attention when two founders share equal ownership or decision-making authority.**

If neither founder can approve a major decision without the other's consent, a persistent disagreement may prevent the company from moving forward.

Founders may consider defined areas of responsibility, mediation, or other negotiated procedures for resolving disagreements. The appropriate mechanism depends on the business and must be structured consistently with its governing documents.

## 6. Intellectual Property Ownership

Intellectual property ownership is especially important for startups developing software, technology, branding, or creative products.

Suppose one founder develops the initial software before the company is formed. The founders may intend for the business to own the software, but incorporation alone does not necessarily transfer those rights to the company.

Under U.S. copyright law, transfers of copyright ownership generally require a written instrument signed by the rights owner or an authorized representative, subject to certain exceptions.

Founders should therefore address whether intellectual property developed before the company's formation will be assigned or licensed to the business, along with ownership of work created afterward.

Formal intellectual property assignment agreements are commonly used to establish those rights.

Preexisting employment agreements, university policies, and third-party licenses may also affect ownership. For example, a founder who develops technology while employed elsewhere may have contractual obligations concerning inventions created during that employment.

Clear intellectual property ownership is particularly important when the company seeks investment or is acquired, as investors and buyers commonly examine whether the company actually owns the technology and other assets underlying its business.

## 7. Financial Contributions and Compensation

Founders may contribute different amounts of money, property, or services to the business.

Those contributions should be documented, particularly when one founder provides substantially more initial capital than another.

For example, if a founder contributes $25,000 to fund development, the parties should establish whether the contribution is intended as an equity investment, a loan, or another arrangement.

Compensation expectations may also differ. Some founders initially work without salaries, while others require compensation to participate full-time.

Any arrangements concerning salary, reimbursement, or future compensation should be coordinated with the company's formal approvals and applicable tax and employment laws.

## 8. What Happens If a Founder Leaves?

A founders' agreement should address how a departure affects the founder's ownership and relationship with the business.

The consequences may differ depending on whether the founder resigns, is removed from a management position, becomes unable to participate, or dies.

Important considerations include:

- Whether the company can repurchase unvested shares.

- Whether vested shares remain with the departing founder or are subject to additional purchase rights.

- How any repurchase price will be determined.

- Whether the founder retains voting or other stockholder rights.

- What confidentiality and intellectual property obligations continue after departure.

A founder who leaves the company may still retain vested shares and remain a stockholder. Departing from an operational role does not automatically terminate ownership rights.

Transfer restrictions, rights of first refusal, and repurchase provisions can help define what happens to that ownership interest.

## 9. Confidentiality and Dispute Resolution

Founders often have access to sensitive information concerning the company's technology, finances, customers, and business plans.

Confidentiality provisions can establish obligations regarding the use and disclosure of that information, including after a founder leaves.

An agreement may also establish procedures for resolving disputes, such as mediation, arbitration, or litigation in a specified forum.

The enforceability of these provisions depends on applicable law. Restrictions on competition, solicitation, and other post-departure conduct require particular care because state laws differ considerably in how they regulate such agreements.

## 10. When Should Founders Put an Agreement in Place?

Founders should address these issues early, preferably before substantial resources have been committed or the company has accumulated significant value.

That does not necessarily require a lengthy standalone agreement before the business has been formed.

Early discussions can establish the intended ownership allocation, responsibilities, time commitments, and treatment of intellectual property. Those decisions can then be incorporated into the appropriate legal documents as the company is organized.

For venture-backed corporations, founder arrangements are often implemented through several agreements addressing stock ownership, vesting, intellectual property, and governance.

The important objective is ensuring that those documents accurately reflect the founders' understandings and operate consistently with one another.
