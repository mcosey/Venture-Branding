Receiving a venture capital term sheet is an important milestone for a startup. It signals that an investor is interested in financing the company and is prepared to discuss the terms of an investment.

The term sheet may identify the amount the investor proposes to contribute, the company's valuation, the preferred stock the investor will receive, and the rights the investor expects in exchange for its capital.

For founders, understanding these terms is essential. An attractive valuation may come with investor approval rights, changes to the board of directors, or economic preferences affecting how proceeds are distributed if the company is sold.

Understanding a term sheet helps founders evaluate the proposed investment before its terms become part of binding financing documents.

## 1. What Is a Venture Capital Term Sheet?

A venture capital term sheet is a preliminary document outlining the principal terms of a proposed investment.

In a typical priced equity financing, the company and a lead investor negotiate a term sheet before preparing the definitive agreements. Other investors may subsequently participate in the same round on the agreed terms.

Term sheets generally address three categories of provisions:

- **Economic terms:** The investment amount, company valuation, investor ownership, and financial rights attached to the securities.

- **Governance terms:** Board representation, investor approval rights, and other arrangements affecting corporate decision-making.

- **Transaction terms:** Closing conditions, expenses, confidentiality, and restrictions on pursuing alternative financing proposals.

The term sheet establishes the framework for the proposed transaction. It does not ordinarily issue shares or transfer investment proceeds to the company.

### Is a Term Sheet Legally Binding?

Most venture capital term sheets provide that their principal investment terms are nonbinding.

Signing a term sheet therefore does not ordinarily obligate the investor to contribute the proposed funds or the company to issue shares. Those obligations generally arise under the definitive financing documents, subject to their terms.

However, certain provisions may be expressly binding when the term sheet is signed, particularly confidentiality, exclusivity, and expense provisions.

The legal effect depends on the document's language and applicable law. Founders should understand which provisions create immediate obligations, even if the investment never closes.

## 2. Investment Amount, Valuation, and Ownership

The economic terms of a venture financing usually begin with the investment amount and the company's valuation.

Suppose a startup receives the following proposal:

| Term | Amount |
| --- | --- |
| Pre-money valuation | $9,000,000 |
| New investment | $3,000,000 |
| Post-money valuation | $12,000,000 |
| Investor ownership | 25% |

The *pre-money valuation* represents the negotiated value of the company's equity before the investment. The *post-money valuation* generally equals the pre-money valuation plus the new capital invested.

Under this simplified financing, the investor contributes $3 million and acquires 25% of the company's post-financing equity. Existing stockholders collectively retain 75%.

The calculation assumes no outstanding convertible instruments, changes to the employee equity pool, or other capitalization adjustments.

### Why the Option Pool Matters

Term sheets frequently specify how shares reserved for employee equity compensation will be treated when calculating the investment price.

An investor may require the company to expand its employee option pool before the financing. If the additional reserve is included in the pre-money capitalization, existing stockholders generally bear the associated dilution.

Two financing offers with the same stated valuation and investment amount can therefore produce different ownership outcomes for founders.

The term sheet should make clear how fully diluted capitalization is calculated and whether the proposed valuation includes any required increase to the employee equity pool.

### What About Existing SAFEs and Convertible Notes?

If the company previously raised capital through SAFEs or convertible notes, those instruments may convert into preferred stock during the financing.

Their conversion can increase the share count and affect the resulting ownership percentages.

The capitalization underlying the term sheet should account for these instruments according to their contractual terms.

Counsel can help prepare or review a pro forma capitalization table showing the anticipated ownership after conversion and the new investment.

## 3. Preferred Stock and Liquidation Preferences

Venture capital investors commonly receive preferred stock rather than the common stock typically held by founders.

Preferred stock may carry special economic and governance rights established in the company's certificate of incorporation.

One of the most important economic rights is the *liquidation preference* .

### What Is a Liquidation Preference?

A liquidation preference determines how certain proceeds are distributed among stockholders when the company liquidates or completes another transaction covered by the governing provisions, commonly including specified acquisitions.

A conventional *1x nonparticipating liquidation preference* generally entitles an investor to receive its applicable preference before common stockholders receive distributions, or to convert into common stock if doing so produces a greater return.

Consider an investor who contributes $3 million for 25% of a company. Assume the company is later acquired, with $8 million available for distribution to stockholders after satisfying other obligations. For simplicity, assume there are no other preferred stockholders or competing liquidation preferences.

Under a 1x nonparticipating preference, the investor would generally receive $3 million, leaving $5 million for common stockholders.

Alternatively, the investor could convert into common stock and receive 25% of the $8 million, or $2 million.

Under these assumptions, taking the $3 million preference produces the better result.

If the company instead has $20 million available for distribution, the investor's 25% as-converted interest would be worth $5 million, making conversion more attractive.

Actual distributions depend on the company's capitalization, outstanding securities, and applicable governing provisions.

### Participating vs. Nonparticipating Preferred Stock

Under conventional nonparticipating preferred stock, the investor generally receives either its liquidation preference or the proceeds attributable to its shares on an as-converted basis, whichever is more favorable.

Participating preferred stock may allow the investor to receive its liquidation preference and then share in additional proceeds alongside common stockholders, subject to any negotiated participation cap.

The distinction can materially affect the proceeds available to founders.

If a company has multiple preferred stock series, their relative priority also matters. A new series may rank senior to existing preferred stock or *pari passu* , meaning on equal footing for the relevant liquidation distributions.

These rights are established in the company's governing documents.

## 4. Anti-Dilution Protection and Other Preferred Stock Rights

Venture capital term sheets commonly address protections against certain future events.

### Anti-Dilution Protection

Price-based anti-dilution protection generally applies when a company issues additional shares below an existing preferred stock series' applicable conversion price. Suppose Series A investors purchase preferred stock at $2.00 per share. The company subsequently raises another round at $1.00 per share.

Depending on the Series A terms, an anti-dilution provision may adjust the conversion price, increasing the number of common shares into which the preferred stock can convert.

Two common approaches are *weighted-average* and *full-ratchet* anti-dilution protection.

Weighted-average protection generally accounts for the price and size of the subsequent issuance. Full-ratchet protection typically adjusts the conversion price to the lower issuance price without regard to the size of that issuance, subject to the governing terms and exceptions.

Full-ratchet protection can therefore produce substantially greater dilution for common stockholders.

The precise adjustments depend on the formulas and exclusions established in the definitive documents.

### Dividends, Conversion, and Redemption

Term sheets may also address dividend rights, conversion, and redemption.

Dividends may be cumulative or noncumulative, with different consequences for whether unpaid amounts accrue.

Conversion provisions determine when preferred shares may or must convert into common stock.

Redemption provisions, where included, may provide rights relating to the company's repurchase of preferred shares under specified circumstances, subject to applicable corporate law.

These rights should be considered alongside the liquidation preference and other economic terms.

## 5. Board Representation and Investor Control Rights

Governance provisions determine how investors may influence corporate decisions after the financing closes.

Two important categories are board representation and protective provisions.

### Board Representation

Venture investors frequently negotiate the right to designate one or more directors. For example, a term sheet might propose a three-member board consisting of two common stockholder designees and one preferred stockholder designee.

Other arrangements may provide for a larger board or an independent director selected through an agreed process.

Board composition matters because directors oversee the corporation's management and authorize actions requiring board approval.

A founder's ownership percentage does not necessarily determine their control over the board.

Founders should understand the proposed board composition, how directors will be selected and replaced, and whether particular board decisions will require additional approvals.

### Protective Provisions

Preferred stockholders may negotiate approval rights over specified corporate actions. These are commonly called *protective provisions* .

For example, the company may be required to obtain approval from a specified percentage of preferred stockholders before:

- Issuing stock with rights senior to the existing preferred stock.

- Amending certain provisions of its certificate of incorporation.

- Declaring dividends or redeeming specified securities.

- Incurring debt above a negotiated threshold.

- Completing certain acquisitions or liquidation transactions.

The scope of these rights depends on the negotiated provisions.

A protective provision may allow investors to block a transaction even without owning a majority of the company's shares. These contractual or charter-based approval rights must also be distinguished from approvals required under corporate law.

Founders should understand which decisions will require investor approval after closing, particularly those affecting future fundraising, governance, and a potential sale.

## 6. Investor Rights and Founder Obligations

In addition to preferred stock rights, investors may negotiate contractual protections that continue after closing.

### Information Rights

Investors may receive rights to financial statements, budgets, and other company information. These rights are often limited to investors satisfying a specified ownership threshold.

### Pro Rata Participation Rights

Investors may also negotiate rights to participate in future equity financings.

A *pro rata right* generally gives an eligible investor the opportunity to purchase a portion of newly issued securities, potentially allowing it to maintain its ownership percentage.

The right depends on the agreement's definitions, exceptions, and exercise procedures. It does not guarantee that the investor can maintain the same percentage in every future transaction.

### Founder Vesting and Transfer Restrictions

A term sheet may require founders to maintain or adopt vesting restrictions on their shares.

For example, an investor might request that a founder's remaining unvested equity continue vesting over an agreed period following the financing.

The parties may also negotiate changes to existing vesting schedules, repurchase rights, or acceleration provisions.

Investors may seek restrictions on certain transfers of founder shares, including company or investor rights of first refusal and co-sale rights.

These terms can affect founders personally and should be reviewed alongside their existing equity agreements.

### Drag-Along Rights

A drag-along provision may require specified stockholders to support a qualifying sale once the transaction receives the approvals required under the agreement.

Depending on its terms, the provision may require stockholders to vote in favor of the sale, transfer their shares, or take other specified actions.

The relevant questions include which stockholders are bound, what approvals trigger the obligation, and what protections apply to participating holders.

## 7. Exclusivity, Confidentiality, and Transaction Expenses

Certain transaction provisions may create binding obligations before the investment closes.

### Exclusivity and No-Shop Provisions

An exclusivity provision, often called a *no-shop* , generally restricts the company from soliciting or pursuing alternative financing proposals for a specified period.

Investors may request exclusivity because they expect to incur expenses conducting due diligence and negotiating definitive agreements.

For the company, however, exclusivity can restrict financing alternatives while the proposed investment remains uncertain.

Founders should understand the provision's duration, scope, and termination conditions before signing.

### Confidentiality

Confidentiality provisions may restrict disclosure of the term sheet, negotiations, or information exchanged by the parties.

These provisions should accommodate appropriate communications with legal counsel, other advisers, and relevant stakeholders.

### Legal Fees and Expenses

A term sheet may specify which party bears the legal expenses incurred in negotiating and closing the financing.

For example, the company might agree to reimburse the lead investor's reasonable legal fees up to a negotiated amount.

The provision should clarify whether reimbursement is payable only upon closing or may apply even if the financing is not completed.

## 8. What Happens After the Term Sheet Is Signed?

After signing a term sheet, the parties generally proceed with due diligence and negotiation of the definitive financing documents.

For a conventional preferred stock financing, five documents commonly form the core transaction package.

| Document | Principal purpose |
| --- | --- |
| Stock Purchase Agreement | Governs the investment, representations and warranties, and closing |
| Amended and Restated Certificate of Incorporation | Establishes the preferred stock's rights and preferences |
| Investors' Rights Agreement | Addresses information, registration, and participation rights |
| Voting Agreement | Establishes specified voting commitments and governance arrangements |
| Right of First Refusal and Co-Sale Agreement | Regulates certain stock transfers |

The National Venture Capital Association publishes model versions of these documents, which are frequently used as starting points in U.S. venture financings.

The transaction also ordinarily requires corporate approvals, satisfaction of closing conditions, and other financing-specific steps.

### Does a Signed Term Sheet Guarantee Funding?

No. An investor may decide not to proceed because of due diligence findings, unresolved documentation, unsatisfied conditions, or other circumstances.

Unless an enforceable investment commitment exists, signing the term sheet does not guarantee that the financing will close.

This uncertainty is particularly important when the company has agreed to exclusivity restrictions that limit its ability to pursue other financing opportunities.

## 9. What Should Founders Focus on Before Signing?

Founders should pay particular attention to the ownership dilution, liquidation preferences, board composition, protective provisions, and changes to existing founder equity arrangements.

They should also understand the term sheet's capitalization assumptions and any immediately binding obligations.

Existing financing arrangements deserve attention. Outstanding SAFEs, convertible notes, preferred stock, or investor agreements may affect the proposed transaction and require additional consents or amendments.

Counsel can help identify the provisions with the greatest legal and economic consequences, model potential ownership and exit outcomes, and evaluate the proposed terms against the company's existing obligations. Addressing significant issues at the term sheet stage can reduce disagreements and legal expenses when preparing the definitive documents.

## Conclusion

A venture capital term sheet establishes the framework for a proposed investment, including the capital the company may receive, the equity investors expect in return, and the economic and governance rights they seek.

Although most investment terms are initially nonbinding, they provide the foundation for the agreements that will ultimately govern the financing.

Understanding those terms before signing helps founders evaluate the proposed investment's effects on ownership, financial rights, and corporate control.
