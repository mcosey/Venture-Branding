You've chosen a name for your business, commissioned a logo, and started building your website. Now you're considering federal trademark registration.

Should you register the name, the logo, or both?

The answer depends on what you're trying to protect. Although a business name and logo often appear together, they aren't necessarily treated as the same trademark for registration purposes.

Understanding the distinction can help founders make informed decisions about their trademark filing strategy, particularly when working with limited budgets.

## What's the Difference Between a Word Mark and a Design Mark?

The United States Patent and Trademark Office (USPTO) generally recognizes two formats for trademark applications: *standard-character marks* and *special-form marks*.

A **standard-character mark**, commonly called a word mark, protects wording without limiting the registration to a particular font, size, color, or design.

Suppose you've launched a productivity app called TempoGarden.

Registering TEMPOGARDEN in standard characters would provide protection for the wording itself, rather than any particular way you display it.

A **special-form mark** protects a particular visual presentation. This can include stylized lettering, standalone graphics, or a logo combining words and design elements.

For example, TempoGarden might use a distinctive leaf-shaped icon alongside its name. That icon, the stylized name, and the combined logo could each be the subject of a separate trademark application.

The choice matters because the format of a registration affects the scope of protection it provides.

## Why Might a Business Register Its Name Separately?

One advantage of a standard-character registration is flexibility.

Suppose TempoGarden initially uses a minimalist green wordmark. Two years later, the company adopts a different font and color scheme.

A valid standard-character registration generally continues to cover the unchanged wording because it isn't limited to the company's original typography or colors.

This can be particularly useful for startups, which frequently refine their visual identities as their products develop.

There are limits, however. A word-mark registration doesn't give its owner exclusive rights to use the name in every industry or context. Trademark rights depend on the relevant goods or services and other legal considerations.

The wording must also satisfy applicable registration requirements, including distinctiveness and the absence of disqualifying conflicts with existing trademarks.

## What Does Registering a Logo Protect?

A special-form registration protects the mark as depicted in the application, including its relevant visual characteristics.

Suppose TempoGarden develops a leaf-shaped icon that customers recognize independently of the product's name.

If the icon functions as a trademark and meets the applicable registration requirements, the company may be able to register it separately.

This could provide protection for an important element of the company's identity, especially if it appears independently on app icons, merchandise, or other materials.

A company can also apply to register a logo that combines its name with a design.

However, **registering a combined logo isn't necessarily equivalent to registering the wording and graphic separately.** The combined mark is evaluated as a whole, even though its individual elements may affect the scope of protection.

For businesses that regularly use their names and symbols independently, separate registrations may offer greater flexibility.

## What If My Startup Changes Its Logo?

Changes to a company's visual identity can affect its trademark registrations.

A standard-character registration generally accommodates changes in how the registered wording is displayed, provided the mark's commercial impression and relevant use remain consistent.

A special-form registration requires closer attention.

Because the registration depicts a particular mark, a substantial redesign may fall outside the scope of what was registered.

The USPTO generally does not permit amendments that materially alter a trademark. Whether a change is material depends on how it affects the mark's overall commercial impression.

A company that significantly changes its logo may therefore need a new application to protect the revised design.

For founders still experimenting with branding, this is a relevant consideration when deciding which elements to register.

## Does It Cost More to Register Both?

Yes. Registering a business name and logo separately generally requires separate trademark applications.

The USPTO permits only one mark per application, and filing fees are assessed according to the number of classes of goods or services covered.

As of October 2026, the USPTO's base application fee is $350 per class.

For example, filing one application for TempoGarden's name and another for its logo, each covering a single class, would ordinarily cost at least $700 in initial government filing fees.

Attorney fees, potential application surcharges, and subsequent prosecution or maintenance expenses are additional considerations.

These costs can influence how a startup approaches its trademark portfolio, particularly if it has several product names, logos, or slogans it wishes to protect.

## How Does a Trademark Attorney Decide Which to Register First?

An attorney evaluating a company's filing strategy generally considers how its marks function in the marketplace, their distinctiveness, and the business's intended use.

For example, if customers primarily identify a company through its name, a standard-character application may offer broader and more flexible protection for that wording.

But the analysis can differ when the wording is descriptive or otherwise difficult to register independently.

A distinctive design accompanying relatively weak wording might warrant separate consideration, although registering a stylized or combined mark doesn't automatically create exclusive rights in otherwise unprotectable wording.

An attorney would also consider whether the name and logo are already being used as trademarks, or whether the company has a bona fide intention to use them in commerce.

This distinction matters because obtaining registration ultimately requires appropriate evidence showing qualifying trademark use. A completed logo design, by itself, isn't necessarily proof that the design is functioning as a trademark in the marketplace.

Other considerations include the company's anticipated branding changes, potential conflicts with existing marks, and its budget for legal protection.

The appropriate filing strategy depends on the particular marks and how the business uses or intends to use them.

## The Bottom Line

A business name and logo may represent the same brand, but they can raise different trademark protection considerations.

A standard-character registration generally provides flexibility by protecting wording independent of its visual presentation. A special-form registration addresses the distinctive visual elements of a mark.

For startups, the decision often involves balancing the importance of each brand element against filing costs and the possibility of future branding changes.

Understanding these distinctions can help founders work with trademark counsel to develop a filing strategy suited to their businesses.
