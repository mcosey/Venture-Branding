Filing a certificate of incorporation or articles of organization generally establishes a company's legal existence. However, several important organizational matters may remain unresolved. Who has authority to act on behalf of the company? Who will serve as its directors or officers? What rules will govern its operations?

These matters are often addressed through a series of corporate or organizational actions commonly referred to as initial authorizing resolutions.

For founders, understanding these resolutions helps explain how a newly formed legal entity becomes an organized business capable of conducting its affairs.

## 1. What Are Initial Authorizing Resolutions?

Initial authorizing resolutions are formal decisions made in connection with organizing a newly formed company.

Depending on the entity type, applicable state law, and governing documents, these resolutions may be adopted by an incorporator, board of directors, LLC organizer, member, or manager acting within their respective authority.

They commonly address matters such as:

- Establishing the company's initial governing body.
- Adopting bylaws or an operating agreement.
- Appointing directors, officers, or managers.
- Authorizing the issuance of founder equity.
- Establishing banking and signing authority.
- Approving formation expenses and organizational transactions.

These decisions may be documented in the minutes of an organizational meeting or through written consents, where permitted by applicable law.

Unlike the company's formation documents, organizational resolutions are ordinarily maintained in its internal records rather than filed with the state.

## 2. What Is an Incorporator?

An incorporator is a person or entity responsible for taking the legal actions necessary to establish a corporation, principally executing and filing its formation documents.

A founder, attorney, or professional formation service may serve as the incorporator, depending on state law.

Being an incorporator does not automatically make someone a shareholder, director, or officer of the corporation. These are separate legal roles with different rights and responsibilities.

An incorporator may have additional organizational authority after the corporation has been formed.

For example, under Section 107 of the Delaware General Corporation Law, if the initial directors are not identified in the certificate of incorporation, the incorporator has temporary authority to manage the corporation's affairs and take actions necessary to complete its organization, including adopting bylaws and electing directors.

Other states allocate these responsibilities differently.

Tennessee law, for instance, permits incorporators in certain circumstances to elect directors and complete the corporation's organization themselves or elect a board that will complete the remaining organizational actions.

The extent of an incorporator's authority therefore depends on the law governing the corporation and the contents of its formation documents.

## 3. Can an Incorporator Be Personally Liable?

An incorporator does not ordinarily become personally responsible for a corporation's debts merely by serving as its incorporator. However, personal liability can arise from the incorporator's own conduct, particularly when acting before the corporation legally exists or outside the scope of their authority.

### Pre-Incorporation Activities

Individuals sometimes negotiate contracts, engage service providers, or acquire property before their company has been formed. Someone undertaking these activities to establish a business may be acting as a *promoter*.

An incorporator may also be a promoter, although the two roles are distinct.

Suppose a founder signs an office lease on behalf of a proposed corporation before its formation.

Because the corporation does not yet exist, ordinary principles of agency may not protect the founder from personal liability under the lease. Depending on applicable law and the agreement's terms, the founder may remain personally responsible even after the corporation is formed and accepts the agreement.

The corporation's subsequent adoption of the contract does not necessarily release the original contracting party. A separate agreement substituting the corporation for that party, commonly called a *novation*, may be necessary.

### Fiduciary and Agency Obligations

An incorporator's responsibilities may also extend beyond filing paperwork.

Where an incorporator acts as an agent for another person, agency law may impose obligations concerning loyalty, care, and compliance with authorized instructions.

Similarly, individuals acting as promoters may owe fiduciary obligations in connection with their organization of the business, particularly where they control formation transactions or have undisclosed personal interests in dealings with the prospective company.

The existence and scope of these duties depend on applicable law, the relationships involved, and the responsibilities actually undertaken.

An individual engaged solely to file formation documents may occupy a significantly different legal position from someone who negotiates contracts, manages company property, or arranges investments before incorporation.

## 4. The Initial Incorporator Consent

When a corporation's initial directors are not named in its formation documents, one of the incorporator's principal responsibilities may be establishing the initial board of directors.

This action is commonly documented through a written consent, often titled an *Action of Sole Incorporator* when there is only one incorporator.

Depending on applicable law, the consent may:

- Establish the initial number of directors.
- Elect the initial directors.
- Adopt the corporation's bylaws.
- Document the incorporator's resignation or completion of their organizational responsibilities.

Once the initial board has been properly established, the incorporator's temporary organizational role generally concludes.

For example, an attorney who serves as the incorporator for a startup might file its certificate of incorporation, execute a consent electing the founders as directors, and formally conclude their role as incorporator.

The founders can then act in their respective capacities as directors.

An incorporator's resignation does not, however, automatically eliminate liability arising from earlier conduct or terminate obligations undertaken in another capacity.

## 5. The Initial Board of Directors Resolutions

Once the initial directors have been established, the board commonly adopts its own organizational resolutions.

These resolutions address the corporation's governance, capitalization, and authority to begin conducting business.

### Adopting Bylaws

If the incorporator has not already adopted the bylaws, the initial board may have authority to do so under applicable law.

Bylaws establish many of the corporation's internal governance procedures, including how directors and shareholders take action, how meetings are conducted, how officers are appointed, and what voting requirements apply.

Bylaws operate alongside the certificate of incorporation and applicable corporate law.

### Appointing Officers

The initial board commonly appoints the corporation's officers.

These may include a chief executive officer, president, secretary, treasurer, or other positions established by the corporation's governing documents.

Officers are responsible for carrying out the company's business within the authority granted to them.

In an early-stage startup, one founder may occupy several officer positions while also serving as a director and shareholder, where permitted by applicable law.

### Authorizing Founder Equity

The initial board may also authorize the issuance of shares to the founders.

For example, a corporation's certificate of incorporation might authorize 10 million shares of common stock. This does not mean that 10 million shares have already been issued or that the founders automatically own them.

The initial resolutions may authorize specific issuances, establish the consideration payable for the shares, and approve related stock purchase agreements.

The actual issuance must also satisfy the applicable legal and contractual requirements.

### Other Organizational Matters

Initial board resolutions frequently address additional matters, including:

- Authorizing the opening of company bank accounts and designating authorized signatories.
- Approving or reimbursing formation expenses.
- Establishing the company's fiscal year.
- Approving intellectual property assignment agreements.
- Authorizing officers to obtain tax registrations or complete other administrative filings.
- Approving indemnification agreements or equity incentive plans, where appropriate.

Several organizational decisions may be consolidated into a single document sometimes referred to as an *omnibus written consent*.

## 6. How Do Initial Resolutions Work for LLCs?

Limited liability companies have a different organizational structure from corporations.

Rather than an incorporator, an LLC is commonly formed by an *organizer* or *authorized person*, depending on the terminology used in the applicable state.

As with a corporate incorporator, the person filing an LLC's formation documents does not automatically become an owner or manager of the business.

For example, Delaware law authorizes one or more authorized persons to execute the LLC's certificate of formation. Separate statutory provisions govern the admission of members and the designation of managers.

An LLC may document its initial organizational actions through an organizer consent, member consent, manager consent, or provisions contained in its governing agreement, depending on its structure and applicable law.

### Adopting an Operating Agreement

An operating agreement, also called a limited liability company agreement (LLCA), establishes the contractual framework governing an LLC.

It commonly addresses:

- The company's members and their ownership interests.
- Capital contributions.
- Management authority.
- Voting and approval requirements.
- Allocations of profits, losses, and distributions.
- Transfers of membership interests.
- The admission and withdrawal of members.

Although an operating agreement and corporate bylaws both address internal governance, an operating agreement frequently covers a broader range of economic and contractual matters among the owners.

The agreement may be entered into in connection with formation rather than adopted through a separate resolution.

### Establishing Management Authority

An LLC may be member-managed or manager-managed.

In a member-managed LLC, management authority generally rests with its members, subject to applicable law and the operating agreement.

In a manager-managed LLC, designated managers exercise the authority assigned to them under the company's governing framework.

Initial organizational actions may document the admission of members, designation of managers, appointment of officers, establishment of membership interests, and authorization of ordinary business activities.

The organizer's involvement may also formally conclude once the company's initial management structure has been established.

Unlike corporations, LLCs do not universally follow a prescribed sequence of incorporator and board resolutions. Their organizational procedures depend substantially on state law and the operating agreement.

## 7. Why Initial Resolutions Matter

Initial organizational records establish the history of a company's authority, governance, and ownership.

Their importance often becomes apparent when a company raises capital, obtains financing, or prepares for an acquisition.

During legal due diligence, attorneys may examine these documents to determine whether:

- The initial directors or managers were properly established.
- Governing documents were validly adopted.
- Founders' ownership interests were properly authorized and issued.
- Officers and other representatives had authority to enter into material agreements.

Missing or defective organizational records do not necessarily invalidate a company's existence or every action it has taken. However, they can create uncertainty concerning particular transactions and may require corrective corporate action.

Even a company with a single founder may have several distinct legal roles to document. A founder who serves as the sole shareholder, director, and officer is still acting in different capacities when exercising the authority associated with each position.

## Conclusion

Formation documents establish a company's legal existence. Initial organizational actions establish much of the framework through which that company conducts its affairs.

For corporations, these actions commonly involve the transition from the incorporator to the initial board of directors, the adoption of bylaws, the appointment of officers, and the authorization of founder equity.

For LLCs, they may involve establishing membership, entering into an operating agreement, and allocating management authority.

The specific process varies by jurisdiction and entity structure, but its purpose is generally the same: establishing who has authority to act for the company, how that authority is exercised, and how the company's initial decisions are documented.
