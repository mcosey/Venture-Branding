Startups often need outside capital before they are ready to negotiate a formal company valuation. Rather than immediately selling stock at an agreed price, many raise their initial funding through SAFEs or convertible notes.

Both instruments allow a company to receive investment capital in exchange for rights that generally result in equity ownership during a future financing. They can simplify early fundraising by postponing some of the negotiations involved in a priced equity round.

However, SAFEs and convertible notes create different legal obligations. Those differences can become particularly important if the company takes longer than expected to raise additional capital, is acquired before the instruments convert, or encounters financial difficulties.

Understanding how each instrument works can help founders evaluate the terms of an investment and anticipate its consequences for the company.

## 1. What Are SAFEs and Convertible Notes?

A **Simple Agreement for Future Equity (SAFE)** is a contract under which an investor contributes money in exchange for rights to receive equity upon specified future events.

Y Combinator introduced SAFEs in 2013 as an alternative to convertible notes. Its standard U.S. post-money SAFE automatically converts into preferred stock when the company completes a priced equity financing.

A SAFE is not a loan. It does not accrue interest or have a maturity date, and the investor generally does not become a stockholder simply by purchasing the SAFE.

A **convertible note**, by contrast, is a debt instrument. An investor lends money to the company under a promissory note that provides for conversion into equity under specified circumstances, typically a subsequent financing.

Convertible notes generally include an interest rate, maturity date, and provisions governing repayment or conversion.

The principal differences can be summarized as follows:

| **Feature** | **SAFE** | **Convertible Note** |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Legal structure | Contract for future equity | Debt obligation |
| Interest | None under standard SAFEs | Typically accrues |
| Maturity date | None under standard SAFEs | Generally included |
| Equity conversion | Upon specified triggering events | Upon specified triggering events |
| Valuation cap or discount | May be included | May be included |
| Ordinary repayment obligation | No loan repayment obligation, although specified event payments may apply | Subject to the note's repayment terms |
| Investor's position before conversion | Contractual rights under the SAFE | Creditor rights under the note |

The conversion triggers may also differ. Y Combinator's standard post-money SAFE converts automatically upon a priced equity financing without requiring the company to raise a minimum amount. Convertible notes commonly require a *qualified financing*, meaning a financing that satisfies a negotiated minimum fundraising threshold.

Neither instrument ordinarily results in an immediate issuance of stock. Instead, the investor's eventual ownership is determined under the applicable conversion provisions.

## 2. What Obligations Does Each Instrument Create?

The difference between debt and a contractual right to future equity has practical consequences for both the company and its investors.

### SAFEs: No Interest or Maturity Date

A standard SAFE does not require the company to pay periodic interest or repay the investment on a specified maturity date.

If the company raises a priced equity round, the SAFE generally converts into shares according to its terms.

If that financing never occurs, the SAFE may remain outstanding for an extended period.

This can provide flexibility for companies whose fundraising timelines are uncertain. The absence of a maturity date removes the need to negotiate extensions merely because the anticipated financing has been delayed.

However, the absence of an ordinary loan repayment obligation does not mean the SAFE holder has no payment rights. Standard SAFEs also address acquisitions, initial public offerings, and dissolution events, which may trigger contractual entitlements to proceeds.

### Convertible Notes: Interest, Maturity, and Repayment

A convertible note generally requires the company to account for three additional obligations.

**Principal.** The company owes the amount borrowed, subject to the note's conversion and repayment provisions.

**Interest.** Interest ordinarily accrues while the note remains outstanding. Depending on the agreement, accrued interest may be paid or converted into additional equity alongside the principal.

**Maturity.** The note specifies a date at which repayment, conversion, or another negotiated treatment may become available or required.

The consequences of maturity depend on the agreement. Some notes require repayment, while others give qualifying noteholders the ability to elect repayment or conversion. Extensions and amendments may also be negotiated.

The debt belongs to the company. Founders are not ordinarily personally responsible for repayment solely because they own or manage the company, although a separately negotiated personal guarantee or other obligation could change that result.

For founders, the maturity provisions are particularly important because they can create financial pressure when the company has not completed its next financing.

## 3. What Happens If Your Startup Never Raises Another Round?

Consider a startup that raises $300,000 to finance product development. Its founders expect to complete a Series A financing within 18 months.

The company could raise the money through a standard SAFE or a convertible note.

Initially, either structure provides the same amount of cash. Their consequences differ if the anticipated financing does not occur.

### Scenario A: The Company Uses a SAFE

Assume the company issues a standard post-money SAFE for $300,000.

Eighteen months pass, but the company has not completed a priced equity financing.

The SAFE does not become due merely because the founders' anticipated financing date has passed. It generally remains outstanding until an event covered by the agreement occurs.

The company may continue operating without facing a contractual maturity deadline under that SAFE.

The investor retains enforceable contractual rights, including rights associated with a future equity financing or other specified event.

### Scenario B: The Company Uses a Convertible Note

Now assume the company instead issues a $300,000 convertible note with an 18-month maturity date and 6% annual simple interest.

After 18 months, the note has accrued $27,000 in interest, bringing the outstanding principal and accrued interest to $327,000.

If the note requires repayment at maturity, the company may face a $327,000 payment obligation even though its anticipated financing has not occurred.

That obligation may be difficult to satisfy if the company has already used the borrowed funds to operate its business.

Depending on the note's terms, the company and its investors might negotiate an extension, convert the debt into equity, or pursue another agreed solution.

If the company fails to satisfy an obligation that has become due, the note's default provisions and applicable law determine the creditor's available remedies.

### Why the Difference Matters

A SAFE generally removes the maturity-related repayment pressure associated with convertible debt.

A convertible note gives the investor creditor rights that may provide additional protection, but those rights can complicate a company's position if its fundraising plans change.

That does not make convertible notes inherently unsuitable. Their debt features may be appropriate where the parties anticipate a near-term financing or negotiate other protections that justify the structure.

The important question is whether the company understands the obligations that could arise if its expected timeline proves incorrect.

### What If the Company Is Acquired Before Conversion?

An acquisition presents another important distinction.

Under Y Combinator's standard valuation-cap SAFE, an acquisition before equity conversion generally entitles the holder to the greater of its original investment amount or the proceeds attributable to a specified as-converted interest, subject to the agreement and available proceeds.

Those payment rights generally rank behind outstanding debt and alongside the relevant preferred stock entitlements.

A convertible note may instead provide for repayment of outstanding principal and interest, conversion into equity, or another negotiated payment upon a change of control.

The note's status as debt can also affect its priority relative to equity interests.

The actual outcome depends on the governing instruments, transaction terms, and available assets. Neither instrument guarantees that an investor will recover the full amount invested.

## 4. How Do SAFEs and Convertible Notes Affect Founder Ownership?

Although neither instrument ordinarily issues shares immediately, both can dilute existing stockholders when they convert.

The number of shares issued depends on the investment amount and the applicable conversion terms.

Two important provisions are valuation caps and discounts.

### Valuation Caps

A *valuation cap* establishes a ceiling on the valuation used to calculate an investor's conversion price under the contractual formula.

Suppose a founder raises $300,000 through a SAFE with a $6 million post-money valuation cap.

If cap-based conversion applies under the standard post-money SAFE framework, the investment generally corresponds to approximately 5% of the relevant capitalization immediately before the new-money priced financing.

This percentage can differ if the priced round's valuation is at or near the cap. The subsequent financing and any new or increased option pool established in connection with that round can also dilute the SAFE holder's resulting ownership.

A valuation cap does not necessarily establish the company's current fair market value or the valuation that future investors will accept.

For convertible notes, valuation caps serve a similar pricing purpose, but capitalization definitions and conversion formulas may differ. A note with a $6 million cap should not automatically be assumed to produce the same ownership percentage as a $6 million post-money SAFE.

### Conversion Discounts

A conversion discount allows an early investor to acquire shares at a lower price than investors participating in the subsequent financing.

Suppose new investors purchase preferred stock at $2.00 per share.

An earlier investor entitled to a 20% discount may convert at $1.60 per share.

A $300,000 investment would purchase 187,500 shares at the discounted price, compared with 150,000 shares at $2.00 per share.

The earlier investor receives more shares because it provided capital before the priced financing.

Some instruments contain both a valuation cap and a discount. Their terms generally determine which calculation provides the more favorable conversion price rather than automatically combining both benefits.

### Interest Can Increase Dilution

Convertible notes introduce another consideration: accrued interest.

Returning to the $300,000 note with 6% annual simple interest, after 18 months the outstanding balance would be $327,000.

If both principal and interest convert at $1.60 per share, the investor would receive 204,375 shares.

By comparison, a $300,000 investment converting at the same price without accrued interest would produce 187,500 shares.

The difference arises because interest increases the amount converted into equity.

Actual calculations depend on the note's terms, including whether interest converts and how the conversion price is determined.

### Why Post-Money SAFEs Matter

Y Combinator's standard post-money valuation-cap SAFE was designed to provide greater visibility into ownership sold through SAFE financings.

For example, a founder who raises $600,000 through post-money valuation-cap SAFEs, all with $6 million caps, generally sells approximately 10% of the relevant capitalization before the next new-money priced round, assuming cap-based conversion under the standard structure.

This can help founders understand the cumulative effect of multiple SAFE investments.

However, different caps, discounts, convertible notes, and future financing terms can complicate the calculations.

A company's capitalization table should therefore account for outstanding instruments and model their expected conversion before additional capital is raised.

## 5. What Should Founders Understand Before Signing?

The choice between a SAFE and a convertible note should begin with the actual terms of the proposed financing.

For either instrument, counsel should evaluate the conversion provisions, valuation cap or discount, applicable definitions, and the investor's rights if the company is sold before conversion.

For convertible notes, particular attention should be given to the maturity date, interest provisions, repayment terms, default remedies, and any negotiated security interests or guarantees.

For SAFEs, the review should address the applicable triggering events, liquidity and dissolution provisions, and any additional rights granted through side letters.

Y Combinator's standard U.S. SAFE documentation includes separate versions providing for a valuation cap, a discount, or a most-favored-nation arrangement. Investors may also negotiate additional contractual rights, such as the opportunity to participate in a future financing.

Both structures require appropriate corporate authorization and compliance with applicable securities laws. Using a widely available form does not eliminate those requirements.

Founders should also understand the cumulative effect of their company's financing obligations.

Several instruments issued at different times, with different caps, discounts, and conversion provisions, can produce substantial dilution and complicate a later financing.

Counsel can help evaluate those terms, identify conflicting obligations, and model their economic consequences before the company accepts additional capital.

## Conclusion

SAFEs and convertible notes both allow startups to raise capital before completing a priced equity financing, but they create different obligations.

Standard SAFEs do not accrue interest or have maturity dates, providing flexibility when the timing of the next financing is uncertain. Convertible notes create debt obligations that may introduce financial and contractual consequences if the company does not raise capital as expected.

Both instruments can significantly affect founder ownership when they convert.

Understanding their legal terms, potential payment obligations, and anticipated dilution is essential to evaluating either financing structure.
